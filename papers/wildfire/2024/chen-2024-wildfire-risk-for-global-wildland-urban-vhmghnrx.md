Intensifying wild�res and human settlement expansion have placed more people and infrastructure at the wildland–urban interface (WUI) areas under risk. Wild�re management and policy response are needed to protect ecosystems and residential communities; however, maps containing spatially explicit information on the distribution of WUI areas are limited to certain countries or local regions, and therefore global WUI patterns and associated wild�re exposure risk remain unclear. Here, we generated the �rst global WUI data layer with 400-m spatial resolution for 2020 by incorporating �ne-resolution housing and vegetation mapping. We estimated the total global WUI area to be 6.62 million km<sup>2</sup> . By overlapping 2015–2020 wild�re burned area maps and �neresolution population dataset, our analysis revealed that globally, 6.16% (11.27%) of WUI areas housing 3.83 million (9.0 million) people are within a 2400-m (4800-m) buffer zone of wild�re threat. Regionally, we found that the United States, Brazil, China, India, and Australia account for the majority of WUI areas, but African countries experience higher wild�re risk. Our analysis is the �rst to quantify global WUI patterns and the associated wild�re risk, and could support improvement of wild�re management. 

Wild�res play an integral role in shaping ecosystem properties<sup>1</sup> and have widespread impact on the environment, wildlife, human health, and infrastructure<sup>2–5</sup> . Recent years have brought more record-breaking wild�re activities globally from Australia to the Arctic and across Europe and North and South America<sup>6</sup> . Such wild�res have consumed vast areas of vegetation, threatened communities, caused severe air pollution, and burdened �re-�ghting expenditure<sup>7,8</sup> . Media reports of catastrophic wild�res have garnered global attention and positioned the need to mitigate wild�re risk as more critical than ever. 

Climate is a dominant controlling factor of wild�re activities, regulating the interplay among ignition sources, vegetation productivity, fuel moisture, and weather conditions<sup>9</sup> . Rising air temperature<sup>10–12</sup> , increasing atmospheric dryness<sup>8,13</sup> , declining precipitation<sup>14</sup> , and widespread drought<sup>15,16</sup> have all been credited with causing intensi�cation of wild�re regimes from regional to global scales. In addition to natural processes, human activities, and land-use change have further exacerbated wild�re risk<sup>8,9,17−19</sup> . In particular, the rapid expansion of human settlements in wilderness regions, known as the wildland–urban interface (WUI)<sup>20</sup> , has placed more people at the intersection between natural vegetation and living communities, and enhanced human accessibility to volatile �ammable fuels through expansion of road networks. Additionally, expansion of electrical infrastructure and transmission lines with WUI development has created further wild�re risk, especially under extreme weather conditions such as lightning storms, extreme heatwaves, and strong wind gusts<sup>3,17</sup> . Over recent decades, the WUI has become one of the main issues of wild�re policy and management globally, with particular exposure in the United States (US)<sup>18,20−23</sup> , Canada<sup>24</sup> , Australia<sup>25</sup> , and many European countries<sup>26,27</sup> . The WUI represents areas where housing is constructed in or near wildland vegetation<sup>20</sup> , and logic and evidence suggests that the WUI is the planetary zone in which wild�res pose the greatest risk to people owing to the proximity of �ammable vegetation<sup>18,20,28</sup> . As a result, WUI mapping with the objective of quantifying the spatial extent and temporal evolution of land areas subject to wild�re risk has received considerable attention from the scienti�c community and forest management agencies. 

Despite a growing body of research on WUI mapping<sup>28</sup> , a reliable and accurate dataset of global WUI areas remains lacking. More importantly, the variation in exposure to wild�re risk in different WUI areas remains unclear. For reliable comparison between different regions and countries, quanti�cation of the WUI area is critical, and assessment of the associated wild�re risk should be derived from the same or consistent data sources using the same or compatible mapping and assessment methods. Standardization of data, methods of risk assessment, legal formulae, and so on, will become more important in the future as the global insurance industry partners with governments to grapple with the thorny issue of property and personal insurance against wild�re risk<sup>29</sup> . 

To address these challenges, we generated a global WUI map using �ne-resolution housing and vegetation mapping from 2020 satellite sources, to quantify the spatial patterns of global WUIs and help assess globally relative risk of wild�re. Speci�cally, the study addressed the following three questions. (1) What is the spatial pattern of WUI areas globally? (2) What are the differences in wild�re risk among WUI areas? (3) To what extent is the global population exposed to wild�re risk in WUI areas?

Rapid expansion of human settlements into wildland environments has exacerbated wild�re problems in WUI areas<sup>18,19,21</sup> . Previous studies reported the widespread extent of the WUI in the US and many other parts of the world such as Argentina, Australia, France, and South Africa. Knowledge of the distribution and composition of WUIs is essential for prioritizing and designing �re management policies, adapting �re-resilient communities and developing insurance laws and practices<sup>23,30</sup> . In this study, we extended WUI mapping to the global context by characterizing the �ne-resolution footprints of houses and vegetation to produce reliable and spatially explicit WUI mapping at 400-m resolution globally for 2020. Our modelled dataset represents the �rst global mapping effort for providing comprehensive information on interface and intermix WUI areas. It is designed to enable more detailed analyses, to advance the �eld of WUIrelated wild�re management, and provide insight into future research, policymaking, planning, and management. First, we applied the general de�nition of the WUI to generate globally consistent mapping results, thereby making possible reliable comparisons and assessments across regions and countries. This approach, which represents a robust complement to existing WUI mapping studies at local and country levels, helps advance understanding of the global WUI distribution. Second, unlike WUI products derived using a zonally based approach<sup>18,21</sup> , we adopted the point-based approach with �ne-resolution satellitebased housing footprint and vegetation data to derive the spatial extent of WUI areas, which enables delineation of more detail in the spatial coverage and con�guration of WUI areas<sup>31</sup> (Fig. 2). Such re�ned WUI information will be critically important for supporting local management actions such as fuel treatment and evacuation response as well as aggregating up for better discriminated national, regional, and global policies and practices. 

Our analysis quantitatively evaluated the wild�re risk for global WUI areas. Global estimates reveal that 0.67% of WUI areas on average experienced wild�res during 2015–2020. It means that at least 0.28 million houses (estimated using the minimum threshold of housing density of > 1 house per 0.16 km<sup>2</sup> ) were exposed to wild�re risk. More alarming is the fact that 6.16% and 11.27% of WUI areas (at least 2.55 million and 4.66 million houses) are within 2400-m and 4800-m buffer zones of wild�re threat, respectively. On the one hand, although these WUI areas are secured by certain buffer distances to wild�re burned areas, they are highly exposed to the threat of air pollution from wild�res, and potentially less-secure owing to the spread of wild�re �ammability under extreme weather conditions. On the other hand, the widely documented trend of growth of the WUI is expected to result in more wild�re ignition<sup>18</sup> , which means that current and future WUI areas will be exposed to enhanced risk of wild�re. Globally, we identi�ed a contrasting pattern in the spatial distribution of WUI areas and associated wild�re risk. The majority of WUI areas are distributed in the US, Brazil, China, India, and Australia (Fig. 4). However, African countries have much higher rates of wild�re risk in their WUI areas (Fig. 5b) because of the intensi�ed wild�re regimes<sup>9</sup> . The impact of human pyrogenic activity upon wild�res in WUI areas is both negative (increasing density of ignitions) and positive (forcing wild�re suppression or prevention). This duality affects the geographical distribution of wild�re risk in WUI areas. The areas with largest risk in Africa and South America are where the density of ignition is highest and where �re duration is longest<sup>32</sup> , and in the Asian part of Russia and northern Australia owing to insu�cient �re suppression. Both ignition density and �re duration are low (owing to the prevention and suppression of wild�res) in highly urbanized Europe and North America<sup>32</sup> , resulting in low risk of wild�re in their WUI areas. The derived spatially explicit dataset outlining the geographic distribution of WUI areas and the associated wild�re risk, particularly vulnerable WUIs under serious threat of wild�res, is expected to have broad practical implications for potential bene�ciaries. 

Our study improves understanding of the spatial patterns of WUI areas and the associated wild�re risk globally, but we recognize that some uncertainties remain. First, we combined High-Resolution Population Density (HRPD) and World Settlement Footprint (WSF) datasets to characterize house footprints globally because of data availability. Certain intrinsic mismatches exist between these two datasets, e.g., the WSF product tends to underestimate isolated housing structures. With further updating of the Meta (formerly Facebook)-based HRPD dataset, our WUI products will be updated for those countries when the HRPD dataset becomes available. Second, we used the same de�nition of WUI as published by the US government in the Federal Register<sup>33</sup> . Although this de�nition has been adopted widely in the �eld of WUI assessment, some studies have highlighted that WUI will differ between locations, especially in relation to the optimal thresholds of the distance parameter, housing density, and vegetation amount<sup>31,34</sup> . Therefore, users of our global WUI maps are recommended to conduct a preliminary check to ensure that the maps are robust to different local spatial contexts. The authors can suggest appropriate algorithms to adapt to local situations. In particular, we emphasize the need to calibrate local maps for topological, service-provision and other determinants of �re-suppression, and with local meteorological data. Third, although the �ne-resolution WUI mapping developed here is by far the latest advancement in quantifying spatially explicit information suitable for informing development and wild�re management in relation to WUI, it represents only baseline mapping for 2020 without documenting historical dynamics. Our next step will be to generate time series dynamics of global WUI mapping using multisource satellite images and advanced change detection technologies. Historical time series of WUI products could help improve understanding of the biophysical and socioeconomic drivers of change in the WUI spatially and over past decades, enhance detection of the response of wild�re risk to the changing WUI, and support sustainable pathways of settlement development, wildlife conservation, and wild�re management.

Materials And Methods<br>Data<br><!-- End of picture text -->

Housing footprint. The WUI represents the area where houses and wildland vegetation either intermingle or abut. Therefore, quanti�cation of houses and vegetation represents the two essential components for generating reliable WUI maps. We used the 30-m building footprint from the Meta (former Facebook)’s High-Resolution Population Density (HRPD) maps to quantify the spatial distribution of the footprint and density of housing<sup>35</sup> . The HRPD maps represent the most accurate population datasets on a 30-m × 30-m grid (i.e., building footprint), which are derived from integration of high-resolution (0.5 m) commercial satellite images (DigitalGlobe), large-scale OpenStreetMap label data, state-of-the-art computer vision techniques, and Center for International Earth Science Information Network Gridded Population of the World data<sup>35</sup> . 

For countries without available HRPD maps, including Afghanistan, Canada, China, Cuba, Greenland, Morocco, Myanmar, North Korea, Russia, Somalia, Sudan, Syria, Ukraine, Venezuela, and Yemen, we used the 10-m World Settlement Footprint (WSF) map<sup>36</sup> to delineate human settlement locations. The WSF 

Page 4/11 

map is generated by integrating open-and-free optical and radar satellite (Sentinel-1 and Landsat-8) imagery and the robust classi�er of the support vector machine with the radial basic function Gaussian kernel. Validation using 900,000 samples from crowdsourcing photointerpretation of very-high-resolution Google Earth imagery revealed that the WSF map has remarkably higher accuracy than other similar human settlement products<sup>36</sup> such as the Global Urban Footprint<sup>37</sup> , Global Human Settlement Layer<sup>38</sup> , and GlobeLand30 products<sup>39</sup> . In particular, the WSF map considerably improves detection of very small settlements in rural regions and scattered suburban areas<sup>36</sup> . 

Wildland vegetation. We used the European Space Agency’s latest global baseline land cover product for 2020 (WorldCover) with 10-m spatial resolution to quantify the spatial distribution of wildland vegetation. The WorldCover map includes 11 different land cover classes with overall accuracy of 75% globally<sup>40</sup> . The combined use of Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 satellite data not only enhances the spatial resolution of the WorldCover map to 10 m, but also provides reliable land cover information in areas with persistent cloud cover<sup>40</sup> . We �rst extracted all types of forest, shrub, and grass from the WorldCover maps as wildland vegetation. To exclude vegetation that is clearly not wild, such as urban green space and human-induced plantations, we used the global urban boundary<sup>41</sup> , extracted from the 30-m Landsat-derived impervious area in 2018, and the 10-m global industrial and smallholder oil palm map for 2019<sup>42</sup> to remove all urban green space and oil palm plantations from the baseline wildland vegetation layer. 

We adopted the WUI de�nition as published by the US government in the Federal Register<sup>33</sup> , which has been used widely in the �eld of WUI mapping and assessment over past decades<sup>18,20,34</sup> . The de�nition speci�es two types of WUI: intermix and interface. The intermix WUI is where houses and wildland vegetation intermingle with housing density of > 1 house per 40 acres (~ 160,000 m<sup>2</sup> ) and where > 50% of the area is covered by wildland vegetation. The interface WUI represents housing areas (> 1 house per 40 acres) with < 50% coverage of wildland vegetation but lying within 2.4 km of a densely vegetated area (> 75% wildland vegetation) that is at least 5 km<sup>2</sup> in size<sup>18</sup> . 

Given the housing density threshold of > 1 house per 160,000 m<sup>2</sup> (i.e., 1 house per 400-m × 400-m grid), the spatial resolution of 400 m was chosen as the minimum unit of our WUI mapping. We aggregated the 30-m building footprint maps and 10-m WSF map to 400-m grids to calculate housing density. Similarly, we aggregated the 10-m wildland vegetation maps to 400-m grids to calculate the percentage vegetation coverage. We �rst identi�ed all intermix WUI areas by overlapping the 400-m housing density maps and percentage wildland vegetation coverage maps based on the housing (> 1) and vegetation (> 50%) thresholds. We then identi�ed interface WUI areas through the following steps: (i) selecting contiguous vegetation patches > 5 km<sup>2</sup> in size and with > 75% wildland vegetation coverage; (ii) generating a 2.4-km outside buffer area based on the vegetation areas selected in step (i); (iii) identifying housing areas with > 1 house per 40 acres and with < 50% wildland vegetation coverage; and (iv) extracting the overlapped areas determined in steps (iii) and (iv) as the interface WUI. 

We collected the WUI map for 2010 in the conterminous US derived from census block data and the US Geological Survey National Land Cover Database<sup>18</sup> , and compared it with our newly generated WUI data in terms of the intermix WUI, interface WUI, and total WUI at the state level. Linear regression was used to check the correlation between these two products and the derived R<sup>2</sup> value was used as a quantitative measure for validating mapping performance. 

We collected the Terra and Aqua combined MCD64A1 Version 6 Burned Area data product to quantify the geographic distribution of global wild�re regimes during 2015–2020. The MCD64A1 is a monthly, global gridded 500-m product containing per-pixel burned area, which has been used widely to characterize global wild�re dynamics<sup>43</sup> . We �rst summed the monthly burned area for a given calendar year using the date of burn for the 500-m grid cells to represent the spatial niche of �res for each year. We then generated two outside buffer zones for each burned area using a radius of 2400 and 4800 m to represent potential wild�re risk areas. The distance of 2400 m (~ 1.5 miles) represents an average estimate of how far a �rebrand can spread from a �re front, which is a rationale related to �re policy application<sup>44</sup> . Therefore, we used one and two times this empirical distance to represent two scenarios for wild�re risk assessment. Finally, we overlapped the original wild�re burned areas and potential wild�re risk areas in the 2400-m and 4800-m buffers with our derived WUI maps to estimate the WUI areas under threat of wild�re. Based on the spatially explicit map of WUI–wild�re interaction, we further calculated the proportion of the WUI as an entire unit and differentiated it in terms of the interface WUI and intermix WUI at risk of wild�re globally and across countries. 

We incorporated the spatially and temporally explicit WorldPop population dataset to estimate the population exposed to the risk of wild�re in WUI areas. WorldPop provides an estimate of the number of people residing in each 100-m × 100-m grid based on a random forest model and a global database of administrative unit-based census information<sup>45</sup> , which has much �ner spatial resolution and higher update frequency than other population datasets such as the Gridded Population of the World<sup>46</sup> and LandScan datasets<sup>47</sup> . Speci�cally, we intersected the population and WUI areas to achieve the spatially explicit population distribution in the WUIs, and then overlapped it with wild�re burned areas during 2015–2020 to calculate a suite of estimates that included the population within the WUIs and the wild�re-exposed population within the WUIs using three scenarios: direct-impact, 2400-m buffer impact, and 4800-m buffer impact. Statistics were estimated in terms of the entire WUI, interface WUI, and intermix WUI at both state and county levels.
