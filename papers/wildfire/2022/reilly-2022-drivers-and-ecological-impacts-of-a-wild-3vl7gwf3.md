During one of the warmest and driest droughts of the last century, the southern Appalachian Mountains experienced a regional outbreak of over a dozen large wildfires in late fall of 2016. We provide a synthesis of long-term forest changes leading up to the 2016 wildfires, examine the climatic setting and patterns of burn severity in relationship to topography, and discuss the ecological and management implications of these and future fires. During the pre- and post-European settlement periods, frequent low- and mixed-severity wildfires interacted with complex topographic gradients and maintained heterogenous landscapes dominated by several species of oak ( _Quercus_ spp.), pine ( _Pinus_ spp.), and the American chestnut ( _Castanea dentata_ ). Land-use changes associated with European settlement, loss of the American chestnut, and 20<sup>th</sup> century fire exclusion resulted in large scale shifts towards mesic, fire-intolerant species (i.e. mesophication). Wildfire activity began increasing in the early 1980s, but most fires in the region are small (<1,000 ha). In Fall of 2016, deciduous tree leaf fall occurred concurrently with a period of anomalously dry and warm weather, creating ideal conditions for fire ignition and spread. Thousands of ignitions across the region strained suppression resources and eight fires grew to >5,000 ha. The 2016 fires were larger and burned more area than in the previous three decades combined. In one unique landscape setting, the Chimney Tops 2 Fire, a synoptic wind event drove extreme fire behavior and burned large, high-severity patches resulting in devastating effects in the wildland urban interface. However, immediate post-fire burn severity mosaics for other fires were composed primarily of low (72%) and moderate severity (21%) fire effects. High severity fire comprised only 6% of the area burned, and occurred mostly on steep upper slopes and ridges on south-facing aspects, reflecting the importance of bottom-up topographic drivers in this region. Although the fires will likely enhance biodiversity by restoring fire-dependent species and creating early seral habitat, invasions of non-native plant species, delayed mortality of mature pines and oaks, and rapid resprouting of pyrophyllic shrubs pose significant management challenges. Although similar large fire outbreaks may become more common under future climatic conditions, they are unlikely to reverse the effects of mesophication and significantly alter forest dynamics at broad spatial scales. # **_1. Introduction_** 

Despite having one of the highest predicted fire frequencies in North America (Guyette et al. 2012), forests of the southern Appalachian Mountains have experienced relatively little recent area burned by wildfires. Prior to the fall of 2016, wildfire activity in the mixed-pine hardwood forests of the southern Appalachian Mountains of North Carolina, Georgia, South Carolina, and Tennessee was limited to relatively small (<2,500 ha) wildfires with the rare occurrence of wildfires exceeding 5,000 ha (Fig. 1). A moderate climate characterized by warm summers with frequent lightning, a long history of human occupation and occasional drought provide an environment conducive to fire. However, high humidity and fuel moisture often inhibit the spread of fire under moderate burning conditions while an extensive road network facilitates rapid response. Burning by Native Americans and European colonizers created a cultural landscape that was largely shaped by fire for millennia (Fowler and Konopik 2008) until 20<sup>th</sup> century policy shifts related to fire prevention and suppression virtually eliminated fire, with the exception of relatively small, prescribed fires (Arthur et al. 2021).

Projections for future climate change suggest an increasing role of wildfire in southern Appalachian landscapes (Liu et al. 2012, Vose and Elliott 2016), yet knowledge of recent wildfires is limited to a few case studies of relatively small fires and there is a need for a broad assessment of the ecological implications and management considerations of the 2016 fires. We provide a brief review of the forest vegetation of the region, along with a synthesis of fire history and the role of European settlers on forest change over the last two centuries. We then examine the climatic conditions that led to the 2016 wildfire outbreak during the months of October and November 2016. Finally, we assess patterns and topographic drivers of burn severity and discuss the ecological implications of the regional fire episode in the context of current management objectives and concerns. 

**_2. An exceptionally diverse region with a long history of frequent fire and an extensive anthropogenic footprint_** 

2.1 Forests of the southern Appalachians 

86 Forests cover approximately 6.8 million ha of the southern Appalachian Mountains (Fig. 2) in 87 western North Carolina, eastern Tennessee, north Georgia, northwestern South Carolina, and northeast 88 Alabama. The region is renowned for its biological diversity, which is partially due to complex underlying 89 environmental gradients that structure landscape patterns of vegetation composition (Whittaker 1956). 90 Multiple species of oak are present and include scarlet oak ( _Quercus coccine_ a), northern red ( _Q. rubra_ ), 91 white oak ( _Q. alba_ ), and chestnut oak ( _Q. prinus_ ). The American chestnut ( _Castanea dentata_ ) was a 92 dominant species historically but was decimated by the chestnut blight during the 20<sup>th</sup> century. Several 93 species of pine are common on dry ridges and slopes, including pitch pine ( _P. rigida_ ) and the endemic 94 Table Mountain pine ( _P. pungens_ ), both of which have serotinous cones. In more mesic coves and 95 ravines where soils are acidic, eastern white pine ( _Pinus strobus_ ) and eastern hemlock ( _Tsuga_ 96 _canadensis_ ) dominate, but eastern hemlock, has suffered extensive mortality from the non-native 97 pathogen, hemlock woolly adelgid ( _Adelges tsugae_ ; Vose et al. 2013). On richer cove sites, a diverse 

- 98 assemblage of hardwood species including tulip poplar ( _Liriodendron tulipifera_ ), magnolias ( _Magnolia_ 99 spp.), ash ( _Fraxinus_ spp), and sugar maple ( _Acer saccharum_ ) dominate the canopy. A dense layer of 

- 100 shrubs is often present and dominated by mountain laurel ( _Kalmia latifolia_ ) on dry sites and 101 rhododendron ( _Rhododendron spp.)_ on cooler, moister sites. High elevations include forests dominated 102 by red spruce ( _Picea rubens)_ and the endemic Fraser fir ( _Abies fraseri_ ) are the least fire prone. The 103 region is also known for its exceptionally diverse understory plant communities which included several 104 species of long-lived perennials, many of which are spring ephemerals (e.g. _Trillium_ spp.) that emerge 105 and flower prior to leaf out of deciduous trees. Most of the region has been impacted by livestock 106 grazing and logging during the 19<sup>th</sup> and 20<sup>th</sup> centuries. Great Smoky Mountains National Park harbors 107 the majority of the region’s unlogged forests with the exception of scattered isolated remnants, mostly 108 on National Forest or state lands (Davis 1996).

115 2.2 Historical fire regimes and land-use legacies 

128 129 130 131 132 133 134 135 136 137 138 

116 Paleoecological studies provide evidence of fire activity in the southern Appalachian Mountains 117 for at least the last 10,000 years during the Holocene (Delcourt and Delcourt 1997). Due to the relative 118 scarcity of natural lakes and ponds, our knowledge of fire activity during this period is limited to a few 119 locations across the region (Lafon et al. 2017). Dominance of oak, chestnut, and pine were related to 120 increased burning approximately 3,000 and 1,000 years ago that were associated with a shift in 121 prehistoric indigenous lifestyles away from hunting and gathering to a more sedentary agricultural 122 lifestyle (Delcourt and Delcourt 1998). Climate and lightning also played a role in fire activity, particularly 123 in the drier eastern and western escarpments of the Blue Ridge, but anthropogenic burning was likely 124 the primary driver of fire activity in this region and much of the eastern United States (Abrams and 125 Nowaki 2019). The effects of burning were likely greatest in close proximity to villages where indigenous 126 tribes burned pastures and woodlands to encourage seed mast, but the extent and impact of indigenous 127 burning remains an issue of debate across the eastern US (Matlack 2013, Stambaugh et al. 2015). 

Fire history studies from cross sections of fire-scarred trees provide much of our knowledge base regarding the historical role of fire in the southern Appalachian Mountains following European settlement. These studies include pine-dominated sites in Great Smoky Mountains National Park in eastern Tennessee and Linville Gorge on the Pisgah National Forest of North Carolina, though research in the central Appalachians of Virginia and West Virginia provide similar findings (Lafon et al. 2017). These studies document a fire regime dominated by dormant season fires with mean fire return intervals less than 15 years between the early 1700s to the early 20th century (Harmon 1982, Flatley et al. 2013, Lafon et al. 2017). However, there is little known about historical fire sizes (Lafon et al. 2017) or landscape patterns of burn severity, particularly for mesic forests of the Southern Appalachian landscape _._ There are no existing fire scar studies from higher elevation studies of the region, but fire return intervals from these forests are thought to be much longer (Guyette et al. 2012). 

139 Both the charcoal and fire scar records indicate that the early 20th century was a period 140 characterized by some of the most widespread burning in the region over the last several centuries 141 (Lafon et al. 2017). Much of the early 20<sup>th</sup> century fire was related to logging and burning activity 142 associated with European settlement. There is little to no documentation on fire sizes or perimeters 143 during the early 20<sup>th</sup> century but reports from the early 20<sup>th</sup> century suggest that fires were considerably 144 more extensive than at present (Ayres and Ashe 1905).  Ayres and Ashe (1905) provide numerous first145 hand accounts of high-severity fire on dry sites, and even-aged stands of the serotinous Table Mountain 146 pine suggest that this period was also characterized by stand-replacing fires (Williams 1998). The lower 147 elevation pine and oak forests have now recovered from high-severity wildfire of the early 20th century 148 but some higher elevation landscapes dominated by spruce-fir forests are still recovering where fires 149 were particularly severe (Korstian 1937). Many populations of serotinous pines that established 150 following fires during this period are now declining (Williams and Johnson 1992, Williams 1998, Brose 151 and Waldrop 2006).

# 2.3 20<sup>th</sup> Century Forest Change 

153 Wildfire suppression and prevention policies nearly eliminated fire across most of the region 154 except where prescribed fire has been reintroduced (Arthur et al. 2021). The effects of fire exclusion 155 include regional scale shifts in structure and composition of mixed pine and hardwood forests, 156 collectively referred to as “mesophication” (Nowacki and Abrams 2008). Increases in fire sensitive 157 mesophytic species such as red maple and blackgum also altered litter and fuel composition and 158 structure, effectively reducing flammability across the region (Kreye et al. 2013, Dickinson et al. 2016). In 159 addition to regional-scale effects of mesophication, industrial logging removed what little remained of 

170 

160 the large mature oaks and old-growth forests and an invasive pathogen devastated the American 161 chestnut, one of the most dominant and abundant trees in the region (Elliott and Vose 2011). This 162 species provided valuable seed mast for both wildlife and human consumption. The thin, papery leaves 163 of the American chestnut also provided fuels to facilitate late fall fires (Kane et al. 2019) and were 164 allelopathic to some fire-sensitive species including red maple and mountain laurel (Vandermast et al. 165 2002). Fire exclusion likely had less of an effect in more mesic and higher elevation forests, but these 166 forests have also declined from novel stressors in addition to hemlock woolly adelgid. High elevation 167 spruce-fir forests were also impacted by invasive pathogens and acid rain in the last century (Pauley et 168 al. 1996). The loss of dominant species combined with the effects of fire exclusion altered the 169 successional pathways of these forests into a no-analogue ecosystem state (Williams and Jackson 2007). 

# **_3. Climate and weather conditions leading up to and during the 2016 fires_** 

171 Climatic conditions during the late fall in 2016 were characterized as the fourth most extreme 172 drought the southern Appalachians experienced since at least 1895, though drought magnitude varied 173 across the region (Williams et al. 2017). Droughts of similar magnitude also coincide with notable 174 wildfire activity including 1914, 1925, 1942, and 2007 (Fig. 3). By September, much of northeast Georgia, 175 western North Carolina, and east Tennessee had received less than 50% of normal precipitation. This 176 trend continued into October and November. The Fall of 2016 was unique not only because of the 177 duration of rain-free days compared to other drought years, but also in that the drought overlapped 178 with leaf fall. Precipitation patterns and leaf fall resulted in airy, dry deciduous leaf litter that 179 experienced little to no rain since summer.

186 3.1 Simultaneous Ignitions and Fire Growth 

187 All but two of the fires that reached more than 3,0000 ha (Boteler and Rock Mountain) were 188 reportedly anthropogenic ignitions that occurred over the period of October to November. There were 189 also thousands of smaller fires scattered throughout the region that were contained and suppressed. 190 During a three-week period before Thanksgiving in November, the number of large fires increased from 191 five to thirty-five. During a single week, four fires approached or exceeded the size of any fire in the 192 region experienced since at least the 1950s (Fig. 4). Although many of these fires were initially 193 contained, subsequent ignitions nearly doubled the number of fires in the following week. The 194 simultaneous occurrence of so many ignitions spread local suppression resources thin, prompting an 195 escalation of suppression coordination. Furthermore, steep topography and ongoing leaf fall hampered 196 suppression efforts and initial attack across the rugged terrain, particularly in remote areas. The recently 197 fallen leaves had not yet received rain, and provided a deep, airy, continuous fuel bed across the 198 deciduously dominated landscapes. Winds created dynamic fuel situations and redistributed light, dry 199 leaves across fire lines and into areas that previously burned. Fuels normally not available for 200 combustion due to high moisture that would normally inhibit fire spread (e.g. stream sides and cove 201 vegetation) burned actively. After burning for almost a month, most of the fires appeared to be largely 202 contained by the end of November after extensive rainfall.

- 207 3.2 The Chimney Tops 2 Fire 

208 On November 23rd, the human-ignited Chimney Tops 2 was reported burning in steep terrain 209 off a popular hiking trail in Great Smoky Mountains National Park (GSMNP) approximately eight miles 210 east of Gatlinburg, Tennessee. Rugged terrain and cliffs around the area of ignition hindered initial 211 attack. The area had heath vegetation and associated deep histosols that were smoldering, and the fire 212 remained confined to a small area. After five days, the fire remained at less than 200 ha, but on the 213 afternoon of November 28th, a mountain wave wind event from the eastern slope of the mountains 

230 

214 reached gusts of over 120 kilometers per hour, driving rapid fire spread and extreme fire behavior. 215 Mountain waves are characteristic of the southern Appalachian Mountains (Gaffin 2009). These winds 216 are similar to foehn winds of other regions (e.g. Santa Ana, Chinook) and often occur on the leeward 217 slopes of large mountain ranges. Mountain waves are associated with cold fronts and occur multiple 218 times a year between November and March with winds up to 175 kilometers per hour (Gaffin 2007). 219 Mountain waves are driven by adiabatic compression of air masses that decrease in relative humidity 220 and increase in temperature as they descend downslope into valleys. The topographic setting directed 221 winds over the high dividing ridge of GSMNP and down the valley of the West Branch of the Pigeon River 222 towards Gatlinburg (Fig. 5). Winds drove air up south-facing slopes where it combined with turbulence 223 on the lee side, increasing the potential for ember transmission with long-distance spotting for 224 kilometers (Fig. 5). The Chimney Tops 2 Fire, in addition to several other fires ignited by downed 225 powerlines, created what could aptly be named a firestorm. The event was the largest wildfire in the 226 Gatlinburg area since the early 20<sup>th</sup> century and resulted in the loss of 14 lives and 2,545 structures in 227 the surrounding communities (National Park Service 2017). The frontal passage that drove the Chimney 228 Tops 2 fire brought rain that ultimately extinguished the regional fire event.

256 4.1 Landscape Patterns of Burn Severity 

- 257 Patterns of landscape burn severity in these four large wildfires (Fig. 6) indicate a wide range of 258 potential ecological outcomes in both the immediate and long-term future. Research on recent 259 prescribed fires and wildfires in the region indicate that low- and moderate-severity fire effects may 260 have many positive ecological outcomes that enhance understory plant diversity and potentially 261 promote regeneration of oak and pine species (Black et al. 2018, Brose et al. 2014) by removing litter 262 and organic soil (duff) as well as competing shrubs. However, single, and even repeated, low-severity 263 fires may have little long-term effect on regeneration as they stimulate resprouting of many shrub and 264 tree species which may eventually increase in density (Elliott et al. 2009, Keyser et al. 2019, Arthur et al. 

- 265 2021). Moderate and high fire severity effects in prescribed fires (Welch and Waldrop 2001) and 266 wildfires (Wimberly and Reilly 2007) also promote Table Mountain pine and pitch pine regeneration in 267 decadent, fire excluded stands that comprise much of the limited distribution of these species (Williams 268 and Johnson 1990, Williams and Johnson 1992, Williams 1998, Brose and Waldrop 2006). Reilly et al. 269 (2006a) found that wildfire promoted understory richness through immigration of early seral species of 270 forbs, and that perennial spring wildflowers are primarily resistant to fire across a range of burn 271 severities (Fig. 8).

- 275 4.2 Early Seral Habitat 

276 The creation of structurally and compositionally diverse early seral habitats in locations that 277 experienced high-severity fire and significant mortality of overstory trees will contribute to landscape 278 and regional biodiversity. As in other regions of the United States, decreases in early seral conditions are 279 a major conservation concern in recent decades (Greenberg et al. 2011). Early seral conditions following 280 wildfire in the southern Appalachians are associated with increases in understory plant diversity (Reilly 281 et al. 2006a), pollinator visitation (Campbell et al. 2007), and fruit production. Patches of early seral 282 habitat may provide foraging for insects and fruits for many species of wildlife (Greenberg et al. 2007). 283 Several bird species in the region are dependent on early seral habitats (Hunter et al. 2001), many of 284 which respond to high-severity fire at a range of spatial scales (Rose et al.  2016). Some species of bats 285 may suffer from negative effects of smoke and immediate loss of roosts (Ford et al. 2016). However, fire 286 is essential for long-term habitat persistence by both reinitiating pine stands and creating large pine 287 snags for roosting the federally endangered Indiana bat (O’Keefe and Loeb 2017). Other species of bats 288 may also benefit from low severity fire (Burns et al. 2019) and more open forest structure including 289 small patches of early seral habitat with sparse cover of vegetation which increase foraging efficiency 290 (Loeb and O’Keefe 2006). 

292 The effects of the 2016 wildfires will also inevitably challenge forest management in many ways, 293 but particularly in terms of non-native species invasions and fuels management. Princess tree 294 ( _Paulownia tomentosa_ ) is a common invader following fire (Langon and Johnson 1994, Kuppinger et al. 295 2010, Black et al. 2018). This species grows rapidly and matures quickly, often producing copious 296 amounts of wind-dispersed seeds in the first decade following establishment. Chinese silvergrass 297 ( _Miscanthus sinensis_ ), a large perennial non-native grass, is also associated with high-severity fire which 298 can promote invasion of more non-native plant species (Black et al. 2018). Management strategies to 299 address invasion of non-native include monitoring for early detection and rapid response to mitigate the 300 potential for invasion. Identification and eradication of invading populations can help reduce further 301 spread while rehabilitation and restoration of native plant species are also essential parts of integrated 302 management of invasives (Miller et al. 2013) 

# 291 4.3 Management Challenges 

303 Given the productivity of the region and resilience of southern Appalachian forests to even 304 severe wildfire (Reilly et al. 2006b), forests burned by the 2016 fires are likely undergoing rapid 305 structural change as forests respond to each fire.  Although fire at low- and moderate-severity initially 306 decreases density of pyrogenic shrubs like mountain laurel and midstory trees, resprouting mountain 307 laurel and hardwoods, as well as regenerating pines may exceed pre-fire and immediate post-fire 308 density within less than a decade (Harrod et al. 2000, Elliott et al. 2009, Elliott and Vose 2010, Hagan et 309 al. 2015, Black et al. 2018). Although there is limited work on reburns in this region, there is some 310 evidence to suggest that burn severity is likely to be higher than in the initial fire (Reilly et al. 2014, 311 Hagan et al. 2015). Post-fire landscapes in the region will likely change rapidly in the coming years and 312 may be especially vulnerable to high-severity reburn where resprouting ericaceous shrubs (i.e. mountain 313 laurel) respond quickly. Managing fuels with repeated prescribed fire where possible may help reduce 314 risk while potentially also reaching longer term restoration goals in unburned forests. Repeated 315 mechanical treatment of resprouting ericaceous shrubs could help reduce risk around homes and other 316 valued infrastructure where prescribed fire is not an option.

317 A further unanticipated challenge might lie in the paradox of higher post-fire mortality in fire318 adapted tree species driven by O horizon consumption. The same traits that confer flammability to litter 319 from fire associated tree species such as oaks and pines along with their mycorrhizal symbionts also 320 promote the formation of a deep O horizon (duff), more so than the mesic species such as _Acer rubrum_ 321 (Carpenter et al. 2021). Over time, fine roots grow into the duff layer. When duff is consumed during a 322 fire, considerable fine root mortality occurs which can initiate a physiological decline that ends in tree 323 death often delayed by several years (Robbins et al. 2022). While this phenomenon is extensively 324 documented in other historically frequent fire forest types in the southeastern United States (Varner et 325 al. 2007, O’Brien et al. 2010), little is known about how southern Appalachian forests will respond. 326 However, Carpenter et al. (2021), observed more post-fire canopy decline for pines and oaks (rather 327 than e.g. maples and poplars) three years after the Rock Mountain Fire where fire severity was initially 328 characterized as low (Fig. 7). This suggests that mesophication could be accelerated by wildfire as higher 329 mortality in oaks and pines will reduce their dominance (Robbins et al. 2022) and alter fuels to a less 330 flammable type (Dickinson et al. 2016). Further work based on longer term trends is needed, but lessons 331 learned when applying prescribed fire in other forest types with deep duff may be useful (e.g. fire 332 excluded longleaf pine; Varner et al. 2007, O’Brien et al. 2010). These include prescriptions that are 333 applied in a manner allowing fire to consume litter but minimizes duff consumption. In general, 334 repeated applications of fire over time can slowly reduce the O horizon by eliminating litter inputs and 335 allowing trees a chance to move roots from O horizon into to A horizon. 336 **_5. 337 The 2016 wildfire outbreak demonstrates the potential for large regional fire events in the 338 southern Appalachians and is consistent with expectations for more wildfire from a warming climate 339 and more frequent droughts (Liu et al. 2012, Mitchell et al. 2014, Vose and Elliott 2016). The 2016 340 wildfires also highlight a key vulnerability under future climatic conditions – multiple ignitions during 341 late fall drought. As the probability of ignition increases linearly with the number of consecutive dry days 342 (Lafon et al. 2017), more droughts that coincide with fall leaf abscission will increase the likelihood of 343 regional fire outbreaks from anthropogenic ignitions which may increase with a rapidly expanding 344 wildland-urban interface (Vose and Elliott 2016). If warmer droughts occur at a higher frequency and 345 extend the late fall fire season into early winter, the potential for ignitions to correspond during 346 mountain wave seasons would create a locally important but relatively poorly understood vulnerability. 347 Identifying landscape settings where evidence of similar fire events occurred in the past and mountain 348 waves are mechanistically likely from a topo-atmospheric perspective are important first steps to 349 understanding geographic patterns of vulnerability to events like the Sevier County Fires that devastated 350 Gatlinburg, Tennessee. 

The 2016 fires also present a clearer understanding of the drivers of large fires during drought. Patterns of burn severity suggest that even under extreme drought conditions, the effects of fall fires, like spring fires, are likely to be characterized by low and moderate burn severity across the vast majority of area burned. Although there is potential for extreme fire behavior in landscapes where mountain waves occur, topography is likely to be a major constraint on the occurrence of high severity fire effects which are most likely on steep upper slopes and ridges under more moderate fire weather conditions. Topographically driven patterns of burn severity may increase landscape heterogeneity and reinforce underlying environmental gradients that historically maintained landscape patterns of forest vegetation (Reilly et al. 2006b), however single fires are unlikely to restore historical conditions and meet many management objectives at stand and landscape scales (Arthur et al. 2021) and uncertainty remains on the role of delayed mortality following fire. 

362 Invasions of non-native species, rapid regrowth of ericaceous shrubs and resprouting 363 mesophytic species will create additional challenges for managers charged with restoring fire dependent 364 communities and preserving biodiversity across the region. While the future likely holds more large 365 wildfire events, the vast majority of the landscape remains unburned for almost a century and even 366 relatively large, infrequent wildfires are unlikely to alter the trajectory of mesophication at meaningful 367 scales. Future fires may potentially accelerate mesophication through the differential formation and 368 subsequent wildfire driven consumption of an O horizon (Carpenter et al. 2021), as well as by shifting 369 species dominance due to resprouting mesic species and delayed mortality of oaks (Robbins et al.2022). 370 In future, historical-like pine-oak may exist only on harsh inherently resilient sites like dry rocky slopes 371 where fire is less critical and where there are concerted management efforts to restore. Judicious use of 372 prescribed fire in strategic locations that experienced fire in 2016 may provide opportunities to leverage 373 wildfires and restore historical fire regimes at larger scales. Additionally, prescribed fire is the best 374 means for preventing the formation of duff in areas still dominated by oaks building resilience to future 375 fires. 

376 For forest and community planners, predictions of increased wildfire from climate change are 377 often broad and generalized. Geographically specific outcomes are inherently difficult to model for 378 heterogenous landscapes such as the Southern Appalachians (Robbins et al. 2022), and the need for 379 downscaling is particularly urgent as there is a high potential for wildfire, vulnerable assets, and 380 communities at risk. The region’s 2016 wildfire season offers insight into this complex hazard and how 381 future fire may be manifest locally with both readily anticipated outcomes and unanticipated surprises. 382 This local scale manifestation of what was arguably predictable based on limited experience with 

398 

383 contemporary wildfire and an unexpected, but normal wind event provides a strong lesson learned. 384 Such outcomes are likely to recur, and by understanding the mechanisms involved with each, 385 researchers can focus efforts on those areas that are least well understood. In this region, the most 386 notable novelty arose from extreme weather during the Chimney Tops 2 that led to the tragic loss of 387 lives and structures. However, in a historical context, novel management outcomes have and will 388 increasingly result from shifting forest composition and structure, as resulted from long-term 389 mesophication and non-native invasive species. 

390 As most of these fires were of human origin, shifting prevention efforts can radically change the 391 region’s fire future, just as they did a century ago when enforcement and education changed cultural 392 behavior and perceptions. As the wildland urban interface is rapidly expanding, future fire outbreaks 393 during similar weather may have worse consequences. Hazardous smoke from the region’s fires led to 394 cross-regional impacts in several metropolitan areas that are also expanding (Zhao et al. 2019). Over 395 time, population growth and shifting weather may affect how the region’s forests are actively managed 396 through use of prescribed fire. Insights into how extreme wildfire events manifest can help prioritize 397 where, how, and how often these actions can be undertaken.
