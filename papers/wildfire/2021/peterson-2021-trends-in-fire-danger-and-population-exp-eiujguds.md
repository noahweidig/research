# Trends in Fire Danger and Population Exposure along the Wildland–Urban Interface

## Abstract

The increased risk of wildfires and associated smoke exposure in the United States is a growing public health problem, particularly along the Wildland–Urban Interface (WUI). Using the measure of fire danger, the Energy Release Component, we define fire danger as the onset and duration of fire season, in the continental US, between 1979 and 2016. We then combine the measure of fire danger with census data to quantify changes in population fire exposure across the WUI. We determined that the largest increases in fire danger were observed in the Southwest, Intermountain, and Pacific Southwest regions. The increased fire danger, specifically during peak fire season, accounted for 6.1 more fires each year and 78,000 more acres burned each year, underscoring the link between fire danger and the risks of large fire occurrence and burn acreage. Finally, we observed significant population growth (121.2% between 1990 and 2010) within high-danger WUI areas, further implying significant increases in potential fire exposure.

## 1. Introduction

The increased risk of wildfires is a growing concern in the United States, particularly along the Wildland Urban Interface (WUI). With the increased frequency of large wildfires, the associated social and economic costs have also increased. Wildfires affect a broad range of community resources, including water, food, transportation, and energy, with impacts both during and after the fire.[1](#R1),[2](#R2) Preparing for fire seasons and understanding the risks faced by communities are necessary priorities for the health and well-being of people who may be impacted. Understanding the trends in fire danger may provide valuable information to determine communities at the highest risk and assist organizations tasked with response management.

While the increased danger faced by communities in the United States is rooted in a multitude of factors, arguably the problem has been compounded by the convergence of two recent trends in demographics and meteorology. From the meteorological perspective, fires occur more frequently when weather conditions become hotter and drier, as vegetation becomes more susceptible to fire ignition.[3](#R3),[4](#R4) A changing climate has resulted in hotter, drier weather and more frequent droughts, increasing the occurrence and duration of wildfires.[5](#R5)–[8](#R8) Fire danger related to fire weather conditions has been measured within the National Fire Danger Rating System (NFDRS) using various indices that show how conditions have changed over time. Jolly et al[5](#R5) showed that fire weather seasons have lengthened across 25.3% of Earths’ vegetated surfaces, resulting in an 18.7% increase in global mean fire weather season length. Additionally, Westerling et al[9](#R9) demonstrated a strong relationship between earlier spring and an increase in wildfire activity. Ruffault et al[10](#R10) predicted using fire potential simulations that heat-induced fire weather will increase by 14% by the end of the century. Wu et al[11](#R11) found that the global burn acreage had decreased since 2000 due to increased urbanization, but their models predict increased fire activity in less developed areas assuming warming global climates. These studies indicate that changes in fire dangers should be analyzed contemporaneously with changes in population growth within affected areas.

Communities have increasingly been established in areas that abut or cross into wildland vegetation, designated as WUI areas.[12](#R12) The growth of WUI areas in the United States has been rapid in terms of size and population, accounting for 43% of all new houses between 1990 and 2010.[13](#R13) According to the National Fire Protection Association, as of 2018, nearly 45 million homes abut or intermingle with wildlands, and more than 72,000 U.S. communities were at increased risk for wildfires.[14](#R14) The main drivers of demographic trends include socio-economic objectives (e.g. rising housing prices in urban areas, access to resources and amenities, aesthetics, and increased access to nature) and structural societal limits (e.g. zoning laws, growth incentives, and land use).[15](#R15) Human activities in the WUI make fire ignitions more likely in these areas.[16](#R16)–[18](#R18) Indeed, Radeloff et al.[19](#R19) observed increased chances of burning where WUI growth was strong largely because of increased human driven ignitions. Additionally, they found that these communities differ from those not in the WUI in their vulnerability to fire events because of fewer roadways needed for evacuation, less fire-resistant housing, and higher dependency on tourism and recreation. During recent fire seasons, power utilities in California de-energized the grid to reduce the possibility of equipment sparking a major fire, a response that disproportionately impacted WUI communities.[20](#R20),[21](#R21) Organizations involved with community fire management face additional burdens because of the geographical barriers to suppressing fires in the WUI.[22](#R22),[23](#R23) It is therefore critical to understand simultaneous changes in fire danger and population growth in the WUI.

In this paper, we examined the regional trends in fire dangers and population risk. Using the NFDRS’s Energy Release Component (ERC), we measured trends in fire danger in terms of the onset and length of fire season. We confirmed the relation between fire danger and the risks of large fire occurrence and their average burn acreage. We then summarized changes in fire danger and population exposure to fire within WUI areas of the continental U.S. and identified those areas with the highest increases.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1. Data Sources

#### 2.1.1. Measures of Fire Danger

We define two measures of fire danger: (1) the annual onset conditions of peak fire season and (2) the duration of peak fire season each year. These measures are derived from the Energy Release Component (ERC) from the U.s. Forest Service’s Wildland Fire Assessment System, a database associated with the National Fire Danger Rating System.[24](#R24) The ERC measures potential energy based on meteorological data (temperature, humidity, dew point, etc.) that affects the accumulation of dried fuel sources (i.e. vegetation).[25](#R25),[26](#R26) We use ERC data for each day between 1979 and 2016 on a 4x4-km grid across the continental United States. A higher daily ERC indicates that weather conditions are hotter and drier, rendering local fuel sources more susceptible to spread of large fires. However, studies typically analyze the annual quantiles of ERC to account for local climate conditions and reveal the annual “fire season” as days with the increased high fire danger.[27](#R27) For this study, we define the onset value of the peak fire season for each grid cell using the estimated 90*th* quantile of daily ERC values for each individual year. The first five years (1979–1983) of our simulation period are defined as the “baseline period.”

The duration of peak fire season at each location is defined as the portion of days during the year in which daily ERC values are above the location’s average onset value from the baseline period. If conditions in a single location are not changing over time, then the onset value for the fire season in each year should equal those observed during the baseline period at that location. Therefore, 10% of days would be expected to be designated as peak fire season for each year. Change in fire season duration is calculated at that location as the difference in the observed and expected (10%) proportion of days with values above the baseline. In [Supplemental Materials](#SD1), we report a similar analysis for the regular fire season using the 60*th* percentile of daily ERC values. These percentiles (60*th* and 90*th*) are used in practice and validated empirically here.

#### 2.1.2. Measures of the Risks of Large Fires

While fire danger measures the potential for fires to occur and spread, in this work, the risks of large fires refer to the results of observed fires in the corresponding geographic area. We measure the risks of large fires across the United States in two ways: the annual incidence rate and the annual average burn acreage. We obtain historical information on large-scale fires from the Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity (MTBS) program.[28](#R28) The MTBS databases compile the starting locations, starting dates, and burn acreages of observed fires between 1984 and 2016. We restricted the study to “large” fires where burn acreage exceeded 1,000 acres.

#### 2.1.3. Forest Service Region, Wildland-Urban Interface, and Census Population

The ERC grid locations are regionally classified by U.S. Forest Service (USFS) administrative regions[29](#R29) and by Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) designations.[12](#R12) The WUI measured interaction between human communities and wilderness vegetation.[13](#R13) An area is defined as “in WUI” if the housing density was at least one structure per 40 acres (as determined by U.S. Census data) and if it either contained at least 50% wild land vegetation or was within 1.5 miles of an area with at least 75% wild land vegetation.[30](#R30) All other locations are classified as “not in WUI,” which include both urban areas and uninhabited areas. Maps of WUI data for 1990, 2000, and 2010 are projected spatially onto the same spatial grid as the ERC data using ArcGIS. The “in WUI” locations for 2010 are densest throughout the Eastern United States and California ([Fig. S1](#SD1)).

We obtain census tract population density estimates obtained from the U.S. Geological Survey.[31](#R31) The population density estimates are projected onto the ERC grid locations using the “sp” library for R software.

#### 2.1.4. Measures of Population Fire Exposure

Population fire exposure reflectS how widely human communities experience high fire danger. We measure population fire exposure as the product of fire danger and the population density:

|  |
| --- |
| PopulationFireExposure=FireDanger×PopulationDensity. |

As before, the fire danger is measured in a grid cell using the relative (percent change from baseline) ERC value at onset for fire season. We use the population density (persons per square kilometer) of the ERC grid cell, as it represents how many people would be affected by changes in fire danger. We estimate population in the grid cell as the product of the area of the grid cell (16*km*2) and the population density from census tract estimates.

Increased population fire exposure in communities within the WUI has been shown to be strongly related to wildfire activity.[19](#R19) As such, we focus on population exposure within WUI sites. For the exposure change within the WUI over a geographic area, we calculate over all WUI grid cells inside the area the arithmetic average change and percent change in the population fire exposure between 1990 and 2010. To determine how many more people have been affected in high risk areas, we also calculate the total change and percent change in population for all WUI sites within the region.

### 2.2. Statistical Methods

### 2.3. Estimation of ERC Quantile Function and Quantile Levels

The peak fire season is defined by the 90th quantile of the ERC distribution; however, this quantile cannot be empirically observed since it is not a multiple of (1/365). Therefore, we use the estimated ERC quantile function and evaluate the 90th quantile ERC in each grid cell fore each year. To estimate the ERC quantile function, we obtain empirical quantiles[1](#FN2) for each cell in a given year. For each year, we estimate a cell’s quantile distribution function by fitting Bernstein polynomials to the empirical quantiles.[32](#R32) In each cell, we fit the following linear regression model

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| Qi=∑kϕkBkτi+ei | (1) |

where Qi is the empirical quantile associated with level τi, ϕk is the coefficient of the Bernstein polynomial Bk⋅, and ei is mean-zero random error. However, the coefficients ϕk must be nonnegative to ensure a valid, nondecreasing estimated quantile function.[33](#R33) We estimate the quantile level τ^ for a given day’s ERC value in a cell. Instead of inverting the estimated quantile function obtained from [Equation 1](#FD2), we interpolate the empirical quantiles to estimate the quantile level of a new ERC value, using the “approx” function in R’s base “stats” library. ERC quantile values are the predicted values from substituting new values of τ into [Equation 1](#FD2). The estimated quantile levels are the interpolated values obtained using the “approx” function in R.

### 2.4. Smoothing Trends in Fire Danger

We use smoothed trends in fire danger to compare long-term behaviors over the years between USFS regions. To obtain the regional smoothed trend, we estimate the smoothed trends for each ERC grid cell, then average the trends over all cells within the region. We use the notation Yst to represent the ERC in location s in year t and ϕjt to represent spline functions defined on the time domain. The expected value is regressed against a linear expansion of the spline functions:

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| μst=∑j=1mβsjϕjt | (2) |

for unknown location-specific coefficients βsj. This linear model is represented in matrix notation as

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| Ys=XBs | (3) |

where Ys is the vector of ERC quantiles, Bs is the vector of coefficients, and X is the design matrix with elements Xij=ϕjti.

Typically, [Equation 2](#FD3) can be solved with least squares estimation, but we smooth the estimation to account for potential over fitting. By adding a roughness penalty,[34](#R34),[35](#R35) we derive coefficients that minimized the penalized deviance

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| ∑tYst−μst2+λ∫μs″tdt | (4) |

for a smoothing parameter λ>0. The first part of [Equation 4](#FD5) estimates deviance of the smoothing function from the data, whereas the second part penalizes the mean function based on the second derivative. For a given value of λ, the penalized least squares estimate for the functional expansion in [Equation 3](#FD4) is calculated to be:

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| B^s=X′X+λS′S−1X′Ys | (5) |

where S is the matrix of second-derivatives of the spline functions with elements Sij=ϕj′′ti. We evaluate the overall fit given λ using the penalized sum of squared errors

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| SSEλ=∑sYs−PλYsdiagI−Pλ2 | (6) |

where Pλ=XX′X+λS′S−1X′ is the projection matrix. We optimize the fit by searching values of λ that minimizes SSEλ.

For the annual trend in ERC quantile, we select cubic i-Spline functions with knots every five years from 1980 to 2015, with boundary knots at 1979 and 2016. Other functions, such as b-Splines and m-Splines, were considered but did not significantly differ in terms of overall trends.

The smoothed danger trends are averaged over cells within USFS Administrative Regions ([Fig. S2](#SD1)). However, to control for local climate effects, we standardize the smoothed ERC values by dividing by the baseline value for the location between 1979 and 1983. This standardization enables assessment of relative changes in fire risks across spatial regions.

#### 2.4.1. Regressions of Fire Season Measures for Large Fires

To illustrate how fire danger relates to the risk of large fire occurrence, we associate each large fire from the MTBS database with the ERC at its origin. We calculate the sum frequency of large fires observed at each quantile level, rounded to nearest percentile. We assume the frequency followed a Poisson distribution:

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| FireFrequency∼Poissonλ | (7) |

with mean related linearly to ERC quantile:

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| lnλ=θ0+θ1·ERC. | (8) |

For burn acreage, we use a log-linear regression that associates the base-10 logarithm of the mean burn acreage linearly to ERC quantile:

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| logBurnacreage=ψ0+ψ1⋅ERC. | (9) |

#### 2.4.2. Association of Trends in Risks of Large Fires with Trends in Fire Danger

Fire danger represents the condition of the fuels and are used here to describe the potential for fires, whereas the risks of large fires are observed outcomes. We therefore estimate the extent to which trends in the risks can be reasonably related to contemporaneous changes in the fire danger. For each year between 1984 and 2016, we calculate annual summaries of fire danger and the risks of all large fires (at least 1000 acres in burn acreage) in the United States. We calculate from the MTBS database the annual total fire incidence and average burn acreage as the risks of large fires, and we calculate predicted fire danger as the change from baseline year in annual average peak fire season duration. The fire danger is averaged only over locations in which fires were observed.

We measure the proportion to which trends in risks of large fires can be associated with trends in fire danger using mediation analysis.[36](#R36) First, we regress the risk measure (denoted as Yi) against the calendar year (denoted as Ti) to estimate the annual trend in fire risk:

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| Yi=μ0+τTi+ei | (10) |

where τ estimates the change in risk per year and random errors (denoted as ei) are assumed to be independent with a mean of zero. For the annual trend in fire danger, we similarly regress the fire danger measure (denoted as Xi) against the calendar year to estimate the annual trend in fire danger:

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| Xi=μ1+αTi+ei | (11) |

where α estimates the change in fire danger per year.

For the association between risk and danger, we regress the measured risk against both fire danger and calendar year:

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| Yi=μ0+τ′Ti+βXi+ei. | (12) |

The estimated parameter β measures change in risk per change in fire danger, while controlling for other effects τ′ of time-varying factors related to the risk but not associated to fire danger. The product αβ quantifies the change in the risk per year that can be associated to changes in fire danger. The ratio of the product to the overall trend τ measures the percent of the fire risk trend mediated by the fire danger trend.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Regional Trends in Fire Danger

Baseline fire danger, measured by the average of peak fire season onset ERC values between 1979 and 1983, was most severe throughout the arid regions of the Southwest ([Fig. 1](#F1)). By 2016, fire danger had increased from the baseline by 6.3% on average ([Fig. 2](#F2)). Across the nine USFS Regions, the largest relative increases were observed in the Southwest, Intermountain, Pacific Southwest and Pacific Northwest regions, where the onset values of peak fire season increased on average by more than 10%. These regions further observed consistent growth in peak fire danger, whereas the other regions observed an initial decrease in fire danger before observing a growing trend in fire danger during the mid to late 1990s. Of these regions, the Eastern region did not experience a net positive change in fire danger. Similar spatial and temporal trends were observed for the onset values of the regular fire season (see [Supplemental Fig. S3](#SD1) and [S4](#SD1)).

### 3.2. Trends in Risks of Large Fires

The risks of large fires, as measured by the incidence and burn acreage of large fires, increased in the United States from 1984 to 2016 ([Fig. 4](#F4) and [Fig. 5](#F5)). The incidence of large fires increased by 22.9 fires each year, which accounted for 59% of the observed variation in fire frequency during this period R2=0.585([Table 1](#T1)). Concurrently, the overall trend in total burn acreage from large fires increased by 175,000 acres each year which accounted for 36% of the observed variation in burn acreage during this period R2=0.364.

### 3.3. Trends in Risks of Large Fires Attributable to Fire Danger

Since the respective measures concurrently increased over the study period ([Table 1](#T1)), we expected trends in the risks of large fires can be predicted by trends in the fire danger. When fire danger was measured by average peak fire season duration, fire incidence increased by 15.1 fires for every 1% increase in the average peak fire season duration. Accounting for the annual trend in peak fire season duration, changes in peak fire season duration accounted for 26.5% of the overall annual trend in fire incidence (6.1 fires per year) and for 45% of the overall annual change in total burn acreage (78,000 acres each year). Although similar mediation results were observed when danger was measured with regular fire season duration and daily ERC (see [Supplemental Table S1](#SD1)), trends in peak fire season accounted for the largest proportion of the trends in the risks of large fires.

### 3.4. Trends in Population Exposure across the Wildland-Urban Interface

Accounting for changes in fire danger and population density, population fire exposure across all WUI sites had increased between 1990 and 2010 by 40% ([Fig. 6](#F6), [Table 2](#T2)). The total population in WUI areas increased by approximately 19 million people, or 44% relative to the 1990 WUI population. Because we determined WUI status using the 2010 census data, this analysis included areas that changed from non-WUI status in 1990 to within WUI status by 2010, but excluded areas that were within WUI status in 1990 but were non-WUI by 2010.

When WUI sites were categorized by change terciles (low-exposure third, medium-exposure third, high-exposure third), the average population fire exposure in the highest category has grown by 135%, compared with 25% increase in the medium category. Although the total population changed by large margins in these categories, the percent growth in population fire exposure was larger than the population percent growth, which indicated that fire danger (which increased in all medium- and high-exposure WUI sites) substantially contributed to the increased fire exposure. WUI sites in the lowest category experienced a 6.4% decrease in population, leading to a 5.0% decrease in average population fire exposure, despite fire danger increasing in about half of the locations (51.6%).

Considering USFS Administrative Regions, Region 5 (Pacific Southwest) experienced the largest average increase in population fire exposure between 1990 and 2010, at 6.5 people/*km*2, but Region 3 (Southwest) and Region 4 (Intermountain) experienced the largest relative increases in exposure, having grown by 114% and 95% respectively. All three of these regions experienced similarly large changes in fire danger ([Fig. 2](#F2)), but the exposure was also driven by the increases in population within WUI sites. Between 1990 and 2010, the WUI populations had grown by 1.6 million for Regions 3 and 4 (109% and 107%, respectively). The WUI population for Region 5 increased by 2.0 million, translating into only a 44% increase from Region 5’s already substantial 1990 WUI population.

Region 8 (Southeast) and 9 (Eastern) experienced relatively smaller increases in population fire exposure. Despite having the largest changes in total population and largest total land area designated as WUI, the fire danger did not increase as much in the eastern regions as in the western regions, leading to lower average change in exposure. However, even though the changes for Regions 8 and 9 were comparably smaller than for other regions, the population fire exposure did increase in a majority of the WUI sites for both regions.

## 4. Discussion

By analyzing trends in fire danger and growth of human populations, we aimed to contribute understanding of a pressing research gap in long-term trends of potential population exposure to large fires. We determined that peak season fire danger has increased throughout the United States, leading to more frequent large fires and a growing exposure to people living within the WUI. There has been a differential increase in peak season fire danger (represented by the 90*th* percentile in ERC values) relative to regular season fire danger (60*th* percentile). Increases in fire danger measures were associated with the increased risks of large fires, in both frequency and burn area.

As a measure of peak season fire danger, we focused on the 90*th* annual quantiles of ERC across the United States between 1979 and 2016. We used spline regressions to smooth annual trends in the fire danger relative to a five-year baseline average from 1979 to 1983. Across the United States, we observed a 6.3% increase in fire danger from the baseline. Regionally, the Southwest, Intermountain, and Pacific Southwest (USFS Regions 3, 4, and 5, respectively) experienced the largest relative increases in fire danger, but fire dangers has increased in all USFS Regions within the contiguous United States since 1999. The increased values of fire danger was associated to the increased risks of large fires observed in the MTBS database. With respect to increases in peak fire season duration, the frequency of large fires increased on average by 6.1 fires per year between 1984 and 2016, and burn acreage increased on average by 77,924 acres per year. We concluded that increased fire danger and longer fire seasons throughout the United States have contributed to more frequent wildland fires with greater burn acreages.

Multiple studies have observed the upward trends in fire occurrence and fire season lengths.[9](#R9),[37](#R37)–[40](#R40) Most notably, Collins[40](#R40) observed increased fire season lengths using the 90*th* and 95th percentiles of ERC values from weather stations throughout the northern Sierra Nevada. Andrews et al[37](#R37) related ERC to large fire occurrence using logistic regression methods. Finney et al[38](#R38) found a strong relationship between ERC and burn acreage, using simulation models for burn spread that closely matched observed records. Westerling et al[9](#R9) observed increased fire season lengths, as measured by number of days between the first major fire’s ignition date and the last major fire’s control date, with the largest increases found in the northern Rocky Mountains, the Sierra Nevada, and the coastal areas of Northern California. Although our study did not focus exclusively on the western United States, we observed the largest increased fire danger in USFS Regions connected to these geographic areas.

As a public health concern, the population fire exposure in WUI areas has increased approximately 40% between 1990 and 2010 because of both increased populations and fire danger. Regionally, the Southwestern and Intermountain regions experienced the largest relative increase in exposure, mainly because the population within WUI areas between 1990 and 2010 had doubled. However, the Pacific Southwest experienced the largest absolute change in exposure, affecting an additional 2 million people. The relative changes in population fire exposure for the Pacific Southwest were tempered because of the large population within WUI areas and the substantial exposure value in 1990.

However, our analysis has certain limitations that affect interpretation of the results. At the fore front is the chosen measures for fire danger, fire risk, and population exposure. While we acknowledge that other measures exist,[41](#R41) the chosen measures agree with standard fire risk terminology.[42](#R42),[43](#R43) Using ERC quantiles effectively related fire danger to the relative accumulation of live and dead fuel sources in an area, which could be attributable to multiple local factors (e.g. dense vegetation in forested areas and dry buildings in desert/urban areas).[26](#R26) As such, other indices may have either stronger associations with burn acreage of large fires or clearer connections to either climate or human factors.[27](#R27),[44](#R44)–[46](#R46) Using the quantile levels for the entire calendar year enabled analysis of annual trends, but we could not predict future values because one would not know the annual quantiles until the end of the calendar year. The quantiles were smoothed in [Equation (6)](#FD7) using a single smoothing parameter over all locations, but they could have been smoothed using location-specific smoothing parameters. However, this would have required more computationally expensive spatiotemporal methods. Smoothed quantiles were standardized relative to the average between 1979 and 1983 to have consistent comparisons across years, but these baseline values become less relevant to current fire danger measures because of changes in local climates. Future work could consider comparing daily ERC values to the preceding 365 days of values to determine its quantile level, but such longitudinal analysis would also be computationally intensive over large spatial domains.

The chosen measures for the risks of large fires have certain interpretation limits. Although this study used fire frequency and average burn acreage, other fire risk assessment studies interpreted risk as the impact of fires on ecology or mortality rates.[47](#R47)–[49](#R49) However, our chosen measure relates to the risks of the overall season more than individual fires, which would more naturally connect to the chosen fire danger measures. Similarly, we focused on fires that burned more than 1000 acres, but smaller fires could be catastrophic in terms of losses. Our focus on large fires created a potential geographic bias because smaller fires often occur in more densely populated areas where they are suppressed, such as WUI areas in the Eastern U.S. However, these smaller fires would need to consider more local factors, such as socio-economics and fire response.[50](#R50)–[52](#R52) While we acknowledge these limitations, we selected measures of risks of large fires that most appropriately relate to the seasonal fire danger described.

Population fire exposure was considered relative to the total population of areas within WUI. We did not distinguish exposures among vulnerable populations, such as by age, race or income status, or control for human activity variables that have been studied on smaller geographic scales.[53](#R53),[54](#R54) Finally, because WUI status was determined by housing density data, WUI could only be calculated on decadal census years, so population fire exposure changes in WUI were not analyzed between census years.

Despite these limitations, our study confirmed that fire danger in the United States has increased over the last decades, and the danger will likely increase in the future.[55](#R55) These dangerous conditions correlated with increased frequency and burn acreage of large-scale wildfires, representing a growing risk to geographic areas with millions of houses and people throughout the United States. Risk and exposure data are needed in order to prepare for resilience in populated areas, related to both the immediate effects of wildfire and the health and economic impacts that occur during and after such fires.[56](#R56),[57](#R57) These include substantial health impacts related to smoke and ash exposure, changes to residential and natural land and water, and community impacts on sectors including labor, education, housing, tourism, and recreation, to name a few. Understanding the growing exposure can positively contribute to future environmental and public health management efforts to mitigate the risks of wildfire to communities.
