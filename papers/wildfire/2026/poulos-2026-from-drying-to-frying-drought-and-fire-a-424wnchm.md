# From drying to frying: drought and fire as interacting drivers of vegetation change in piñon-juniper-oak forests of the Chisos Mountains, Big Bend National Park, USA

## Abstract

Abstract Background Piñon-juniper forests provide important habitat, cultural resources, and ecosystem services across western North America, but increasingly severe droughts and wildfires threaten their persistence. Understanding responses to successive disturbances is critical because wildfire may affect forests already transformed by drought. We used 20 years of permanent-plot data from the Chisos Mountains, Texas, to quantify forest changes in response to a severe drought and subsequent wildfire. Results Drought caused widespread declines in tree density and basal area. In unburned plots, both declined by approximately 50% between the pre-disturbance and post-drought censuses, with little subsequent change. Fire caused additional but spatially variable mortality that ranged from limited tree death to near-stand replacement. High-severity fire plots had already lost 70.7% of tree density after drought and retained only approximately 75 trees ha⁻ 1 after the subsequent fire and cumulative basal-area loss reached 92%. Piñon declined by approximately 99% in these plots, compared with 92% for junipers and 89% for oaks. Mortality was not concentrated in particular tree sizes. Fire also significantly reduced juvenile tree abundances. Conclusions Successive drought and fire created a heterogeneous mosaic of drought-thinned forests and burned patches. As these disturbances become more common, forest persistence may depend on recovery between disturbances rather than resilience to either disturbance alone.

But today, warming temperatures and increasing wildfire activity from anthropogenic climate change are exposing these forests to the hottest, driest, and most fire-prone conditions in the last 1000 years (Williams et al. 2022). Drought is killing trees worldwide (Allen et al. 2010; McDowell et al. 2020), and despite their relatively high moisture stress tolerance compared to other conifer forests, even PJ systems are experiencing massive, landscape-scale tree death at many sites (Breshears et al. 2009; Flake and Weisberg 2019; Floyd et al. 2009). Die-off events like these are sparking concern amongst managers and the public alike about the resilience of PJ systems to disturbances in the Anthropocene (Floyd et al. 2009; Redmond et al. 2023). 

Within PJ systems themselves, piñon pines are particularly sensitive to prolonged drought compared to co-occurring junipers and oaks. Pine species, including Rocky Mountain piñon, appear more susceptible to hydraulic failure and mortality under moisture stress than co-occurring trees, like oaks and junipers. In response to increasing water stress, pines tend to shut their stomates (compared to these other species) in an effort to conserve water and prevent hydraulic breakdown. This can be an effective mechanism over the short term, but it cannot be maintained because it shuts down photosynthesis. The hydraulic machinery of pines also appears to be simply less resilient to internal drying (Adams et al. 2017; Brodribb et al. 2020). Studies have repeatedly linked such hydraulic shortcomings to mass die-offs of piñon pine, but drought survival by junipers in the Southwest (Meddens et al. 2015). In several recent droughts, however, even junipers and oaks are succumbing to extreme droughts with prolonged high vapor pressure deficits (Bowker et al. 2012; Kannenberg et al. 2021; Poulos 2014). Studies about drought-triggered PJ mortality events are largely focused on explaining the mechanisms of tree death. Much fewer are studies that document how recent landscape-scale droughts are shaping multidecadal PJ forest and woodland dynamics. 

Similarly sparse are studies that document the impacts of recent wildfires on PJ forest structure and function (Barney 1972; Board et al. 2018; Floyd et al. 2021; Huffman et al. 2012; Koniak 1985; Poulos et al. 2020). Most prior research on contemporary wildfire effects on Southwestern forests has focused on documenting its impacts on ponderosa pine, pine-oak, and mixed conifer forests, with much less attention to drier PJ savannas, woodlands, or forests. The few published studies to date have demonstrated that contemporary PJ fire effects are variable (Board et al. 2018), burning across a range of severities form low-severity surface fire in PJ woodlands and forests with abundant grasses (Poulos et al. 2020), 

to high-severity stand-replacing fire and compositional shifts from forests to shrublands (Floyd et al. 2021; Huffman et al. 2012). 

PJ woodlands and forests’ responses to repeated disturbances are varied, and they often depend on how the original disturbance reorganizes the resources or growing space from the first disturbance to the second. Selective mortality from drought can either reduce competition and enhance post-drought moisture availability for surviving trees or induce injuries from xylem cavitation that predisposes plants to mortality in subsequent drought or disturbance events (Oliver and Larson 1990; Rodman et al. 2025). Low- versus high-severity wildfires can similarly either promote post-fire growth in residual surviving trees under reduced competition or predispose trees to mortality in subsequent high-severity reburns (Bagcilar et al. 2026; Harris and Taylor 2017). Drought can magnify subsequent wildfire effects because when drought-killed trees become part of the vertical and surface fuel matrix, the same dry atmospheric conditions that kill trees during severe drought also create fuel beds that are vulnerable to ignition in extreme fire weather. This is especially true following drought-triggered tree mortality, when standing dead trees and downed woody debris accumulate across the landscape. (Seager et al. 2015, Higuera and Abatzoglou 2020). 

While frequent, low-severity fires were the historical norm in most southwestern pine forests prior to EuroAmerican settlement, PJ fire regimes vary from frequent and low-severity to infrequent, high-severity fire, where fire behavior is driven by tree canopy cover and surface fuels (Romme et al. 2009). PJ savannas, with their abundant grass cover and scattered trees have continued to experience frequent, low-severity surface fires throughout history (Margolis 2014; Poulos et al. 2020, 2009). Where PJ forest cover was higher and grass cover lower, fire was less frequent and was more severe historically (Floyd et al. 2004, 2000; Romme et al. 2009). Yet, we know little about the impacts of high-severity fire on contemporary PJ forest structure, especially in regions outside the distribution of Rocky Mountain piñon pine ( _Pinus edulis_ ). In general, piñons do not possess sufficiently thick bark or tall, self-pruned boles to survive even moderate-severity fire (Romme et al. 2009, Keely 2012), in contrast to thicker barked and more fire-resistant pine species, like ponderosa pine ( _Pinus ponderosa_ ). To date, just a few studies have explored the impacts of recent wildfire on PJ forest dynamics, signaling the need for more field studies to characterize the range of PJ postfire successional trajectories in the Anthropocene. 

The combination of drought, high temperature, and wildfire is increasingly recognized as a key combined driver of contemporary landscape-scale vegetation

change (Anderegg et al. 2019; McDowell et al. 2019). Moreover, projections call for an even a hotter, drier, and more flammable future for the American Southwest (Petrie et al. 2017, Thorne et al. 2018, O’Connor et al. 2014). Many fear that, as a result of these trajectories, forests and woodlands in this region may be approaching a tipping point that could lead to their transition from diverse, mixed species complexes to simpler droughtresistant and fire-resilient plant communities (Falk 2013; Falk et al. 2019; Gonzalez et al. 2018; Phillips et al. 2024). 

Many recent field studies and review papers document how single drought or wildfire events influence PJ dynamics, but to our knowledge no one has evaluated the combined impacts of a recent landscape-scale droughttriggered tree mortality event followed by wildfire on PJ forest change. Species composition of southwestern PJ systems vary greatly from north to south into Mexico, but studies characterizing drought or fire impacts on PJ systems beyond the Rocky Mountain piñon pine—one seed juniper ( _Juniperus osteosperma_ ) species complex are less common (but see Barton 1993, 1994; Barton and Teeri 1993; Morino et al. 2000; Poulos and Berlyn 2007; Barton and Poulos 2021a; Poulos et al. 2021; VillanuevaDíaz et al. 2022; Azpeleta Tarancón et al. 2023; Rodriguez-Robles et al. 2023 for studies about its impacts on _Pinus discolor_ and _Pinus cembroides_ ). In an effort to fill these gaps in our understanding of drought + wildfire impacts on PJ systems, we use data from a long-term plot monitoring network in the Chisos Mountains in Big Bend National Park in West Texas to quantify the impacts of a drought event in 2011 and subsequent wildfire in 2021 on Mexican piñon pine ( _Pinus cembroides_ )—Alligator juniper ( _Juniperus deppeana_ )—oak ( _Quercus grisea_ , _Q. gravesii, Q. emoryi_ ) dominated PJ forests that are common along the highlands that straddle the US-Mexico Border. 

As a baseline for the current study, Barton and Poulos (2021b, a) evaluated the impacts of the drought across the Chisos Mountains in a larger, mountain range wide study. Drought impacts in the Chisos Mountains were strongly dependent on topography and tree species identity. Mortality was highest in piñon pine and on warm, exposed, low-elevation sites with high solar radiation and low moisture availability. Here, we asked the following: (1) how did tree density, basal area, and juvenile abundance change between the pre-disturbance, post-drought, and post-fire censuses; (2) how variable were fire effects among burned plots?; and (3) within field-defined fireeffect classes, how was tree loss distributed among species and tree size classes, and how did juvenile abundance trajectories differ? Given the recent increases in both drought and wildfire incidence across the southwestern USA, assessing the impacts of multiple, successive 

disturbances on PJ forest stand dynamics is critical for informing forest and fire management, and it provides an opportunity for evaluating how recent droughts and wildfires are interacting to influence long-term PJ community dynamics. 

## **Methods** 

### **Study system** 

The Chisos Mountains (CM) are a small rhyolitic mountain range located entirely within Big Bend National Park. The CM rise to 2300 m asl. and are bound at lower elevations by deserts dominated by shrub and succulent desert flora, where tree establishment and growth are inhibited due to high temperatures and low precipitation. Soils are a mixture of mollisols and entisols. They are composed of moderately deep gravelly loam, which is well drained and non-calcareous (Miggins et al. 2008). 

PJ forests in the Chisos Mountains cover most of the mountain range. Contemporary forest structure and composition in the Chisos Mountains are driven by a combination of historical factors including climate, fire regimes, and land-use history (Moir 1982; Poulos et al. 2009). Tree density generally increases with increasing soil moisture availability in PJ systems and, in the CM, open-canopy woodlands dominate drier sites and lower elevations and closed-canopy PJ forests are most common on moister, higher elevation, and canyon locations (Poulos and Camp 2010). Dominant tree species include Mexican piñon pine ( _Pinus cembroides_ Zuccarini), alligator juniper ( _Juniperus deppeana_ vonSteudal), gray oak ( _Quercus grisea_ Liebmann), Graves oak ( _Quercus gravesii_ Sudworth), Emory oak ( _Q. emoryi_ Leibmann), and weeping juniper ( _J. flaccida_ vonSchtdl) (Poulos et al. 2013). 

The climate is arid, characterized by cool winters and warm summers. Precipitation is distributed bi-modally in late summer and winter with most precipitation falling during summer storms as part of the North American Monsoon System. Mean annual precipitation for the Chisos Basin is 49.7 cm (range 10–135 cm). Mean minimum temperatures are 1.8 °C in January and 17.0 °C in July. Mean maximum temperatures are 14.1 °C in January and 29.1 °C in July (Western Regional Climate Center 2023). 

In February 2011, the Chisos Mountains and the rest of Texas experienced the most severe 1-year drought on record in the state (Neilson-Gammon 2011). This event led to widespread tree mortality and unprecedented wildfires across Texas, although no fires occurred in the Chisos Mountains that year (Inciweb 2011). The drought killed an estimated 300 million trees statewide (Moore et al. 2016), with especially severe impacts in West Texas (National Drought Mitigation Center 2011). Arid conditions have continued to prevail in the Chisos Mountains since the 2011 drought. For example, from September

### **Field methods** 

We have maintained a permanent plot network in the Chisos Mountains since Poulos established 226 plots across the mountain range in two separate studies in 2003 and 2008 (Poulos 2010; Poulos and Camp 2010). Poulos and Barton resampled a sub-sample of 180 of these original plots in 2019 to (1) document the effects of the 2011 drought on forest dynamics and (2) estimate the effectiveness of fuel management treatments (Poulos and Barton 2021). In 2021, the South Rim 4 Fire burned through much of the plot network across piñon-juniperoak forests of the CM. 

In May 2023, we remeasured all 56 plots that had been sampled in 2019 and subsequently burned in the 2021 South Rim 4 Fire, together with 51 unburned comparison plots that had also been sampled in 2019. Since the burned plots were dominated by piñon pine, junipers, and oaks, we restricted the unburned comparison plots to previously sampled plots with similar forest composition. Of the 51 unburned comparison plots, three were located within the mapped fire perimeter but showed no evidence of burning, whereas the remaining 48 were located outside the fire perimeter in nearby high-elevation areas. In the initial censuses of these long-term plots, trees were uniquely identified with numbered brass tags, and we recorded their distances and azimuths from the plot center. We analyzed these data for changes in forest stand structure over multiple sampling intervals, from pre-disturbance in 2003/2008 to post-drought in 2019 and from post-drought in 2019 to post-fire in 2023. The post-fire sampling interval occurred 2-years post drought, so our census was designed to capture both immediate and delayed tree mortality effects, rather than initial fire effects and because the drought event occurred over the same year, we were only able to evaluate the combined impacts of those events on forest change from the pre-disturbance to post-drought sampling interval. 

In each 10-m radius fixed-area (0.0314 ha) plots, we assessed each tree > 5 cm diameter at breast height (DBH) as live or dead based on its change in condition in each plot census. Live juvenile trees that were < 5 cm 

DBH in previous censuses but surpassed that threshold by our sampling dates were measured, tagged, and added to the current tree population census. Seedlings and resprouts (0–5 cm DBH) were tallied by species as juvenile plants in 5-m radius circular plots (0.008 ha), nested inside of the larger census plots. 

Finally, we classified burned plots into field-based fireeffect classes using the percentage of pre-fire trees that experienced fire-related mortality: low (< 30%), moderate (31–60%), and high (> 60%). We made ocular estimates of fire severity by placing two technicians at 5 m distance from plot center at cardinal directions. We confirmed tree mortality by bending branches or visual inspection for heavy char or lack of needles or branches to detect tree death, as needed. In total, we sampled 17 low-, 20 moderate-, and 19 high-severity burned plots. We recognize that total tree mortality was used to define these classes. Therefore, we descriptively interpreted contrasts among classes in total overstory density, basal area, and survivorship rather than as independent causal tests of fire-severity effects. This approach offered a method for evaluating interactions between drought and fire within a fire severity class, looking at how species differed (or not) in mortality within severity classes based on all trees, and examining if there were differences in mortality across tree size distributions within fire severity classes. 

In addition to our field fire severity assessments, we also explored the utility of remotely sensed differenced normalized burn ratio (dNBR) (Miller and Thode 2007) fire severity products. However, neither the Landsat 8 nor the Sentinel-2 derived dNBR indices were well correlated with the field assessments of wildfire severity at our plots (data not shown). We therefore used our field fire severity classifications since remotely sensed dNBR did not correspond well with field-observed fire effects at the plot scale and because the field classes provided an ecologically interpretable way to distinguish lightly affected, partially affected, and stand-replacing portions of the burned plot network. 

### **Statistical analyses** 

We used linear mixed effects models (LME) to assess changes in live tree density, basal area, juvenile plant density, species-specific tree density, size, and stem abundance across the pre-disturbance, post-drought, and post-fire censuses. LMEs were fit via the glmmTMB (Brooks et al. 2017), and nlme (Pinheiro et al. 2017) packages in R (R Core Team 2026). For analyses of tree density, basal area, juvenile density, and species-specific density, models included timestep, fire-effect class, and their interaction as fixed effects, with plot included as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements. Separate models were fit for all trees and for the major

taxonomic groups: piñon, juniper, and oak. Plot was included as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements within sampling units. Post hoc LME pairwise comparisons were then estimated via estimated marginal means in the emmeans package (Lenth et al. 2019). 

For all models, we evaluated temporal and fire severity impacts by first comparing the alternative error distributions among Poisson, negative binomial, and zero-inflated negative binomial models. Final models were selected using simulation-based residual diagnostics implemented the DHARMa package (Hartig and Hartig 2017). We assessed residual uniformity, dispersion, zero inflation, and outliers by comparing observed residual patterns to those expected under the fitted model. Diagnostic results indicated that simpler models (e.g., Poisson) did not adequately meet assumptions due to overdispersion and excess zeros, whereas the negative binomial and zero-inflated negative binomial models showed no evidence of remaining violations. We therefore selected zero-inflated negative binomial models for our final analyses based on their low Akaike Information Criterion and their handling of assumptions of dispersion, distributional fit, and residual structure over the other tested models. 

Finally, we evaluated forest size structural changes and fire severity classes for all trees (stems > 5 cm DBH) and for individual taxa. Diameter distributions were visualized using kernel density plots, faceted by fire severity, and overlaid by timestep to assess changes in distribution shape among surviving stems in each sampling interval. Because density plots are normalized, they were used to evaluate relative distribution shifts rather than changes in abundance. Then, we quantified changes in tree diameter central tendency using linear mixed-effects models to test how timestep, severity, and their interaction influenced plot-level median DBH using plot as a random intercept. Significance was assessed using Type III ANOVA, and post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted via estimated marginal means. 

To test whether tree losses were size-selective from the drought and wildfire, we binned stems into 5-cm DBH classes and modeled stem counts per plot, timestep, and size-class using negative binomial generalized linear mixed-effects models. Models included timestep, size class, their interaction, fire severity as an additive fixed effect, and plot as a random intercept. The timestep × size-class interaction was used as the primary test of whether temporal changes in abundance differed among diameter classes. Models were fit separately for all trees and for each major taxonomic group. 

## **Results** 

Among the 56 burned monitoring plots, 17 were field classified as low effect (30.4%), 20 as moderate effect (35.7%), and 19 as high effect (33.9%). This distribution showed that fire effects varied substantially across the pre-existing plot network across the fire perimeter, ranging from limited overstory loss to near-stand-replacing conditions. Across both disturbances, we observed strong and consistent declines in tree abundances and basal area over the 20-year study period (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1; Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Figures S2–S3). Declines from pre-disturbance to postdrought were significant across all plots ( _p_ < 0.001), whereas subsequent additional post-fire declines occurred primarily in high-severity fire plots ( _p_ < 0.001; Table 2). The mixed-effects models showed significant timestep × severity interactions for both tree density and basal area ( _p_ < 0.001; Table 1, Supplementary Table S1), indicating that the 2011 drought reduced tree densities and stand structure across most plots, while the subsequent 2021 fire was associated with additional losses, especially after high-severity fire. Tree density and basal area declined substantially between the post-drought and post-fire censuses in burned plots, whereas unburned plots showed little additional change over the same interval. Among burned plots, the magnitude of overstory loss varied widely across the fire-effect classes, from limited change to near-complete loss of live structure. As noted in the methods, we interpreted these class-level contrasts descriptively as an interaction with the drought impacts. (Fig. 1, Table 1). These patterns were consistent for all trees as a whole and within each tree taxon. These patterns were clearly shown in the violin plots (Figs. 1, 2) through progressive downward displacement and narrowing of distributions over time, with the strongest compression in moderate- and high-severity plots. 

Drought caused widespread structural loss across all plots ( _p_ < 0.001; Table 1; Supplementary Table S2; Supplementary Figs. S1–S2). ## **Discussion** 

Taken together, the 2011 drought and the 2021 South Rim 4 Fire in the Chisos Mountains resulted in landscapescale declines in tree density and basal area. The drought alone caused substantial reductions in tree abundance, demonstrating the major impact of this event on forest structure. The 2021 South Rim 4 Fire caused additional tree mortality following the widespread losses associated with the 2011 drought, but its effects varied substantially across the burned plot network. Some burned plots experienced little additional overstory loss, whereas others underwent near-complete loss of remaining live trees. The fire’s principal effect was not uniform stand replacement across the burned area, but rather a heterogeneous post-fire pattern of additional mortality superimposed on an already drought-affected forest. The field-classed fire-effect classes describe this range of outcomes, from lightly affected plots to near-stand-replacing conditions, rather than serving as independent evidence that greater severity caused greater total mortality. 

### **Drought impacts** 

In piñon-juniper woodlands (PJ) throughout much of the western US, severe drought (and associated diseases, insects, and parasites) is the most important driver of forest dynamics, more so even than fire (Floyd et al. 2009; Meddens et al. 2018). The drought event in the CM resulted in the deaths of many thousands of trees, and the broad and consistent pattern of tree losses across a range of sizes and species groups suggests that this coupled event acted as a landscape-scale biological filter of tree survival. These results are consistent with our past reports documenting these impacts by Poulos (2014) and Tyree and Sperry (1989), as well as accounts by Waring and Schwilk (2014), for the Chisos Mountains and the state of Texas as a whole (Lawal et al. 2025). 

Plant hydraulic system failure is a primary cause of death under water stress in response to drought (Brodribb et al. 2020; Marchin et al. 2022). Tree losses over the pre-disturbance to post-drought sampling interval were therefore likely driven by hydraulic failure caused by drought stress (Sperry and Tyree 1989; Pittermann et al. 2010; Lens et al. 2013; Brodribb et al. 2020; Marchin et al. 2022), with piñon pine showing especially high vulnerability to these processes both at our study site and in the larger PJ drought tolerance literature (Breshears et al. 2009; Wion et al. 2022).

Whereas drought impacts were widespread across plots and taxa, fire acted more selectively, producing the greatest additional losses in moderate- and high-severity patches. ### **Impacts of successive drought and fire** 

Together, the drought and South Rim 4 Fire illustrate how successive disturbances can reshape pinyon-juniper forests through both broad-scale stress and spatially selective filtering. The interaction between timestep and fire severity therefore reflects a compounding disturbance effect, but not because drought mortality directly predicted subsequent burn severity. Rather, drought reduced tree density and basal area across much of the landscape, and high-severity fire then imposed an additional filter that strongly altered which stands retained live trees, basal area, and juvenile plants. 

The magnitude of these cumulative losses appears high relative to other long-term studies of piñon-juniper disturbance dynamics. Compared with similar work near Flagstaff, Arizona (Rodman et al. 2025), the Chisos Mountains experienced greater drought-associated mortality and even larger cumulative losses where drought was followed by high-severity fire. This suggests that the sequence of drought after fire may have pushed some stands beyond partial canopy thinning toward persistent structural reorganization. 

At the landscape scale, these disturbances have increased heterogeneity in forest structure and potential wildlife habitat. Avian and mammal habitat use varies significantly with canopy cover, the number of standing dead snags for nesting sites, and forest structure in PJ systems, and both drought and fire reorganize the availability of different habitats for wildlife (Gallo et al. 2016; Johnson et al. 2018). Some unburned stands remain comparatively intact and may function as seed sources for adjacent burned areas, whereas moderate- and highseverity patches now contain few or no living trees. The resulting mosaic ranges from closed-canopy piñon-juniper forest to drought-thinned woodland, open post-fire savanna, and severely burned patches with minimal live overstory. This increased structural heterogeneity is likely to influence not only tree regeneration, but also understory plant communities, wildlife habitat, and future disturbance dynamics across the CM. 

With projected increases in aridity and temperature, however, the sparser forests created by the drought and wildfire may make surviving trees more resistant to mortality by future droughts and fires because decreased competition as a result of tree mortality (McDowell et al. 2020). Our results provide an important example of how drought and fires are interacting today to trigger the post-fire recovery of sparser forests composed of more drought drought-and fire tolerant species. Low density forests could therefore be the new normal for PJ systems 

growing under chronic stress and disturbances in the Anthropocene. 

### **Forest management applications** 

Our study provides an important example of ecosystem transformation that is likely to magnify with anticipated climate warming and increased fire activity in coming decades. Forest and fire managers across the Southwestern USA are using studies such as these to identify tipping points for forest structure and composition. We recognize that a 20-year study of forest change is short compared to the long-term life cycles and forest development trajectories of PJ forests. Still, long-term forest monitoring projects like these are important barometers of forest change. Recovery in PJ systems can take many decades (Barney 1972; Huffman et al. 2012; Koniak 1985), and studies of drought subsequent to that documented here further suggest that piñon establishment in particular is declining throughout the region under increasing aridity (Floyd et al. 2015; Redmond et al. 2012). Longterm datasets like ours will continue to be critical for documenting forest recovery and shifting successional trajectories in response to global change. 

Assessments of the impacts of successive disturbances on contemporary forests are similarly important for informing forest management in a warming climate. Many ecosystems are likely to face repeated disturbances as a result of rapid environmental change in Southwest and beyond. The Resist-Accept-Direct (RAD) framework offers a mechanism for evaluating and enacting management options that range from resisting change to directing the trajectory of change (Lynch et al. 2021; Schuurman et al. 2022). The drought and fire could have either pushed the forests into a non-forest state for decades to centuries, or these disturbances could have the longer outcome of facilitating tree survival and growth in residual stands. The RAD framework is therefore an apt way to think about the future forests of the CM. 

Our results suggest that management responses in piñon-juniper-oak woodlands will need to balance resistance, acceptance, and directional strategies depending on site conditions and management goals. Efforts to resist ecosystem transformation are particularly important where maintaining ecosystem services and habitat is a priority. For example, piñon pine provides critical food resources for wildlife and cultural values for humans, making it a keystone tree species in the CM. Management could focus on maintaining remaining piñon populations by reducing competition and water stress through thinning or prescribed fire in unburned parts of the CM. However, the effectiveness of adaptive management activities like forest thinning and/or prescribed fire for bolstering PJ forest resilience remains uncertain

and context dependent (Huffman et al. 2019; Poulos et al. 2020; Redmond et al. 2018). While reducing stand density may alleviate water stress and improve tree vigor in dense forest stands and other forest types (Sankey and Tatum 2022; Sankey et al. 2025), this may not always be the case. For example, lower stand density might disrupt the benefits of nurse plants on regeneration or processes such as hydraulic lift that help redistribute soil moisture from deeper soil. The widespread drought-driven mortality we observed, especially the near-collapse of piñon populations, also indicates clear limits to resistance after both single and repeated disturbances. In areas where piñon has already declined substantially, managers may need to accept ecosystem transformation, recognizing that recovery of this species may be limited under current and future conditions. Alternatively, managers could direct ecosystem transformation by planting piñon seedlings in large high-severity patches with no nearby seed sources. Maintaining piñon-dominated systems will require site-specific strategies informed by both disturbance legacies and ecological tradeoffs. 

## **Conclusions** 

Our results have important implications for future forest management of the Chisos Mountains, specifically, and PJ systems of the southwestern USA, in general. Climate models predict a hotter and drier climate with increased fire activity in southwestern PJ forests and woodlands (Harris et al. 2024; Wasserman and Mueller 2023). This will likely lead to shrinking PJ distributions (Noel et al. 2025). Projections suggest an amplification of the impacts we identified here, with associated higher tree mortality and reduced stand density and basal area from the increasing prevalence of multiple disturbances. Drought stress may also accentuate the impacts of disease and insects, as well, exacerbating disturbance effects (Breshears et al. 2021). Time will tell, as we continue to monitor forest changes throughout the region; forests in the CM could continue to persist under successive disturbances in the future, especially at lower tree densities, but these impacts could also lead to long-term transitions to non-forest cover types, such as shrubland or grassland systems that are devoid of trees (Falk et al. 2019; Guiterman et al. 2022). Sustained tree mortality and a lack of juvenile regeneration in the wake of disturbance could represent a bottleneck for the long-term sustainability of CM PJ forests. While the resultant plant communities after two disturbances could be more resilient to drought and fire, they may also be representative of a longer-term loss of forest cover across the Southwest if trees cannot recruit in the post-fire and post-drought environment (Coop et al. 2020). Such forest losses would in turn result in loss or at least changes in the unique environmental 

services provided by PJ ecosystems to people and wildlife (e.g., wood, wildlife habitat, shaded recreation), making long-term forest monitoring ever more important for characterizing trends in PJ forest stand dynamics.
