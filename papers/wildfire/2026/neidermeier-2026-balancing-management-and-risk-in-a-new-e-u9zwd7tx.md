# Balancing Management and Risk in a New European Pyrogeography

## Abstract

Wildfires are an increasingly important challenge across Europe. Climate change is altering the conditions under which fires can ignite and spread, but wildfire risk is also shaped by how landscapes are used, managed, and planned. These choices influence vegetation and fuel availability, landscape structure, and the resilience of ecosystems and communities. Understanding these interactions is therefore important for developing landscapes that are better prepared for a changing climate. Wildfire research draws on multiple disciplines, including ecology, climate science, land-use research, and fire modelling. This broader perspective is referred to as pyrogeography—the study of how fire interacts with climate, vegetation, landscapes, and human activities across space and time. Although wildfire research in Europe is growing, fewer studies combine land-use change, fuel management, ecosystem function, and wildfire modelling to examine how future landscapes can balance multiple objectives. This dissertation addresses this challenge by investigating how land management, future land-use change, and fuel management influence wildfire risk across Europe. The research combines continental-scale assessments with a regional case study. Chapter 2 maps the suitability of three fuel-relevant management strategies—herbivory, mechanical fuel removal, and prescribed burning—across Europe. The results show that opportunities for these strategies vary considerably between regions, highlighting the importance of adapting management to local ecological and social conditions. Chapter 3 examines how wildfire management interacts with biodiversity and carbon storage. It identifies areas where reducing wildfire risk may also help protect important ecosystem functions, as well as areas where fuel management requires careful consideration of potential trade-offs. Chapter 4 focuses on central Portugal and explores how different land-use trajectories influence wildfire susceptibility. The results indicate that continued agricultural abandonment generally increases wildfire susceptibility, whereas renewed agricultural activity can reduce susceptibility by altering vegetation patterns and fuel continuity. Chapter 5 returns to the European scale and simulates how different fuel management strategies perform under alternative land-use and climate futures. Management targeting fine fuels, either alone or combined with other fuel classes, generally produces greater reductions in wildfire behaviour than strategies focused only on coarse fuels. However, responses vary considerably between regions, reinforcing the importance of adapting management to local conditions. Taken together, the findings demonstrate that wildfire in Europe is shaped not only by climate, but also by decisions about how landscapes are managed. Land-use planning, fuel management, and ecological conservation are closely connected, and effective wildfire management requires balancing multiple objectives, including reducing fire risk, conserving biodiversity, storing carbon, and supporting agriculture and forestry. Because these priorities and landscape conditions vary across Europe, management strategies cannot be applied uniformly. The modelling approaches presented in this dissertation do not predict exactly where or how future wildfires will occur. Rather, they provide ways to explore alternative futures, identify potential management opportunities and trade-offs, and examine how different land-use and fuel management pathways may influence wildfire outcomes. By bringing together wildfire science, land-use planning, ecology, and modelling across spatial scales, this dissertation contributes to a more integrated understanding of wildfire management and provides evidence to support the development of landscapes that are better prepared for a changing climate.
