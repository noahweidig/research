# Rapid soil moisture depletion during flash droughts accelerates wildfire spread in the United States

## Abstract

Abstract Fire danger can escalate rapidly when vegetation dries over days to weeks. Flash droughts, characterised by rapid root-zone soil moisture depletion under high evaporative demand, may accelerate wildfire spread. Here, we quantify relationships between flash droughts and wildfire spread dynamics across land-cover types in the conterminous United States, using a model-derived root-zone soil-moisture flash-drought metric and contrast these with conventional drought conditions defined by persistently low soil moisture without rapid onset. We find that fires following flash droughts exhibit faster early spread than fires not associated with flash droughts, with model estimates indicating about 1.3–1.8-fold faster early burning across major flammable land covers. Grasslands and croplands show the strongest acceleration, followed by open shrublands, woody savannas, savannas and deciduous broadleaf forests, consistent with faster drying and greater fuel continuity in fine-fuel ecosystems. In contrast, conventional drought conditions produce weaker and less consistent changes in fire spread. Our results demonstrate that rapid soil-moisture drawdown—rather than background dryness—appears to provide a more direct pathway linking drought to accelerated wildfire spread. We recommend that monitoring declines in root-zone soil moisture in the days to weeks preceding ignition could improve early warning of fire risk in ecosystems prone to rapid drying.

Weather extremes pose major societal and ecological risks, particularly when they interact in compound or cascading sequences that amplify impacts<sup>1–4</sup> . Anthropogenic climate change is intensifying many of the physical drivers that govern these extremes, including rising air temperatures, increasing atmospheric evaporative demand and altered precipitation regimes<sup>5–8</sup> . Together, these changes are accelerating land–atmosphere moisture exchange and increasing the likelihood that moderate dry spells rapidly escalate into more severe hydroclimatic events<sup>5–8</sup> . Flash droughts (FDs) have emerged as a prominent example of this shift, characterised by therapidintensificationofsoil-moisturedeficitsovertimescalesofweeks<sup>9–11</sup> . Because they develop quickly and often without prolonged warning, FDs leave limited time for preparedness and response, increasing risks for agricultural systems, ecosystems, and water resources<sup>10–12</sup> . 

Flash droughts arise not solely from precipitation deficits but from the combination of reduced rainfall with unusually high evaporative demand<sup>11,13–15</sup> . Elevated air temperatures, low humidity, strong winds, and enhanced solar radiation from clear skies enhance evapotranspiration (ET), causing soil moisture to decline rapidly, often in conjunction with heatwaves<sup>14</sup> . Climate change is expected to amplify these processes by 

strengthening atmospheric moisture demand and accelerating land-surface drying rates<sup>16–19</sup> . As a result, drought may increasingly be governed not only by how dry conditions become, but by how quickly drying occurs<sup>20</sup> . This rapid depletion of soil moisture can impose severe physiological stress on vegetation, reduce ecosystem productivity and modify fuel moisture dynamics in ways that potentially precondition landscapes for wildfire ignition and spread. 

Drought has long been recognised as a key driver of wildfire activity, as evidenced by palaeoecological reconstructions (e.g., tree rings, charcoal), satellite observations<sup>21</sup> and the predictive skill of meteorological drought indices for wildfire occurrence<sup>22–25</sup> . Although drought is not a prerequisite for wildfire, it increases ignition likelihood and enhances fire spread by lowering moisture content in both live and dead vegetation fuel sources<sup>26</sup> . Flash droughts may intensify this relationship because they alter the rate at which fuels dry. Rapid soil-moisture depletion during FD events can accelerate vegetation desiccation and enhance fuel flammability over short timescales<sup>14,27</sup> . Under such conditions, landscapes can transition rapidly from moderately dry to highly fire-prone states, potentially compressing the time available for fire detection, response and suppression. Operational fire danger systems may not fully capture all aspects of such rapid drought-to-fire transitions. In the United States, wildfire risk is assessed using the National Fire Danger Rating System (NFDRS), which provides daily metrics of fire potential used by federal and state agencies<sup>28</sup> . Historically, key components of NFDRS, such as the 1000-hour dead fuel moisture, which contributes directly to the Energy Release Component (ERC) and is a widely-used index of fire intensity, have relied on models driven by multi-day running averages of meteorological inputs<sup>29</sup> . While these approaches effectively represent slowly evolving drought conditions and weather-driven fuel drying, they may not explicitly represent rapid losses of root-zone plant-available water associated with flash droughts<sup>19</sup> . Although NFDRS Version 4 introduces a physically-based fuel-moisture model driven by hourly weather data<sup>28</sup> , which improves representation of short-term fuel-moisture responses to near-surface weather, rapid RZSM decline reflects depletion of plant-available water over days to weeks. These challenges are compounded by the limited predictability of FDs on subseasonal timescales<sup>30</sup> ; for example, current forecast systems capture fewer than 30% of FD events at one-week lead time<sup>31</sup> . 

Flash droughts have been widely studied at global and regional scales<sup>18,32–34</sup> . In the contiguous United States, FDs occur most frequently across central regions during the warm season<sup>35</sup> , with seasonal timing and dominant drivers varying geographically: the western United States tends to experience FD onset in spring/early summer from precipitation deficits, whereas the eastern United States more often experiences FD in fall due to soil moisture deficits from high evaporative demand<sup>16</sup> . Emerging evidence suggests that FDs may also enhance wildfire risk. Regional analyses in India haveidentifiedincreased fireincidencefollowingFDonset<sup>36</sup> ,andrecentcase studies have linked flash drought events in the United States to widespread wildfire activity<sup>37</sup> . However, these studies have primarily focused on cooccurrence or correlation rather than systematically evaluating how flash droughts influence wildfire behaviour itself. 

Despite growing recognition that flash droughts can rapidly precondition landscapes for wildfire, their influence on wildfire dynamics remains poorly quantified. In particular, it is unclear whether flash droughts primarilyaffectthelikelihoodofwildfireoccurrenceortherateatwhich fires spread once ignited. This distinction is critical because climate change is expected to increase atmospheric evaporative demand and accelerate landsurfacedryingrates,potentiallyalteringwildfirebehaviourevenwherelongterm drought conditions remain moderate. Yet to date, to the best of our knowledge, no continental-scale analysis has systematically quantified how 

flash droughts influence wildfire spread across ecosystems or whether these effects differ from those associated with conventional, slowly evolving drought. 

Here we address this gap by analysing the relationship between flash droughts and wildfire dynamics across the conterminous United States (CONUS). Specifically, we aim to: (1) characterise the spatial co-occurrence of FDs and wildfires; (2) determine whether fires igniting after FD events spread more rapidly than fires not preceded by FDs, and compare these effects with fires occurring under conventional drought conditions defined by persistently low RZSM without rapid onset; (3) assess how these relationships vary among major land-cover types; and (4) examine whether rapid pre-fire depletion of root-zone soil moisture, rather than systematic differences in final fire size, provides a mechanistic pathway linking FDs to accelerated wildfire spread. By explicitly linking rapid drying processes to wildfire spread dynamics, this study provides new insight into how flash drought can intensify wildfire impacts. 

## Results 

### Spatial distribution of flash droughts and wildfires in CONUS 

FD occurrence across CONUS exhibits pronounced spatial variability, with hotspots in parts of the north-western United States, the south-central region,andlargeareasoftheeasternCONUS(Fig. 1a).Hereandthroughout the main analysis, FDs are identified using a root-zone soil moisture (RZSM) definition that captures rapid soil-moisture depletion relative to local climatology, defined as a drop in 5-day (pentad) mean RZSM from above the 40th percentile to below the 20th percentile within three pentads (15 days). Wildfires are represented as spatio-temporally connected clusters of burning pixels, enabling analysis of fire evolution at the event-scale. Sensitivity to the FD identification method was evaluated using the Standardized Evaporative Stress Ratio (SESR) metric (Supplementary Fig. S1). Because our primary hypothesis concerns rapid soil-water depletion and its effect on fuel flammability, all main analyses use the RZSM-based FD definition, while SESR is retained only as a supplementary sensitivity test. 

In contrast to the broad distribution of FDs, wildfire clusters are concentrated primarily in the south-central United States, including Texas andneighbouringstates,aswellasinthewestern,andsouth-easternregions, with additional isolated hotspots elsewhere (Fig. 1b). Regions where fires ignite shortly after FD onset, hereafter referred to as post-FD fires, largely coincide with areas of high wildfire activity (Fig. 1c). ### Post-FD fire spread at cluster and CONUS scales 

To illustrate how flash drought conditions influence wildfire spread within individual events, we first examine an example pair of fire clusters from the south-central United States. Fire clusters are defined as spatio-temporal groupings of spatially contiguous burned grid cells that ignite in successive pentads,withtheignitionarearepresentingpixelsthatburn firstwithineach cluster (see Methods). In this example, a post-FD fire occurred in June 2008 in a mixed cropland–grassland landscape, with burned pixels composed of 55.0% croplands and 45.0% grasslands, while a second fire occurred in June 2010 at the same location under non-FD conditions, with burned pixels composed of 66.7% croplands and 33.3% grasslands (Fig. 2). 

In the post-FD case, cumulative burned area increases rapidly and reaches its final extentwithinthe firstfew pentadsafter ignition, whereas the non-FD fire expands more gradually and continues growing for several additional pentads (Fig. 2a). Instantaneous burn rates show a similar contrast: the post-FD fire rates peak earlier and decline rapidly, while the nonFD fire rate reach their maximum later and decay more slowly (Fig. 2b). 

Notably, the non-FD fire also accelerates during the event and reaches comparable burn rates later in its evolution (Fig. 2b). This is expected as the fire grows with the expansion of the burning perimeter and fire line, which can increase the rate of area growth even without FD<sup>38</sup> . In addition, the later propagation of the post-FD fire may have been constrained by breaks in combustible materials or reduced fuel continuity. Subsequent fire growth and final burned area may also be influenced by changing weather conditions, topography and suppression activity. Burn sequence maps further highlight these differences (Fig. 2c, d): under post-FD conditions, most pixels ignite within a narrow time window, indicating rapid spread across thelandscape,whereasthenon-FD fireprogressesmoreslowly,withalarger fraction of the cluster burning at later times. Together, this provides an event-scale example of the broader pattern that wildfire spread tends to accelerate following FD conditions.

### Influence of land cover type 

The frequency and spatial extent of post-FD fires vary across land cover types. The land-cover distribution across CONUS (Fig. 4a) shows that grasslands and croplands occupy the largest areas, followed by deciduous broadleaf forests, open shrublands and woody savannas. Based on the postFD fire sample, we focus the remaining analysis on six land-cover types that account for most post-FD fire clusters and burned area: croplands, grasslands, savannas, woody savannas, open shrublands, and deciduous broadleaf forests. Other land-cover classes are excluded because they contain few or no post-FD fire clusters or have too few clusters and therefore do not allow robust comparison. 

The annual proportional burned area of post-FD fires, defined as the percentage of each land-cover class that burns per year, shows systematic differences across these ecosystems (Fig. 4b). Typical values remain below 

~3% for all fires(Supplementary Fig. S3) and below ~0.3% for post-FD fires. Woody savannas, grasslands and croplands consistently exhibit the highest proportional burned areas, while other land-cover types show smaller but still notable burned fractions. For example, savannas and deciduous broadleaf forests occasionally exceed 0.1–0.2% burned area during post-FD events, although their smaller spatial extent results in lower absolute burned area than in grasslands or croplands. Total burned area statistics (Supplementary Fig. S4) similarly indicate that grasslands and croplands dominate overall burned areas, with open shrublands and woody savannas also contributing substantially. 

These results highlight strong differences in FD-fire interactions across land-cover types. Grasslands and croplands dominate burned area totals, while deciduous broadleaf, open shrublands and woody savannas also contribute meaningfully to post-FD fire activity. Under the RZSM definition, croplands often exceed grasslands in proportional area burned during FD events, whereas savannas, open shrublands and woody savannas contribute episodically but significantly. These contrasts indicate that the apparent strength and spatial distribution of FD–fire co-occurrence over CONUS depends on both land cover and the FD definition, motivating a closer examination of fire evolution across these six land-cover types. 

Temporal composites of mean burned area as a function of time since fire onset reveal broadly similar fire evolution across land-cover types (Fig. 4c, d). In both post-FD and non-FD fires, mean burned area peaks sharply during the first pentad after ignition. Grasslands exhibit the largest early burned areas, reaching approximately 5 km² in non-FD fires and slightly lower values in post-FD fires. Burned area declines rapidly within the first ten pentads and approaches near-zero values after approximately 20 pentads. 

Total and cumulative burned area statistics further confirm that most burning occurs in the first few pentads across all land-cover types (Supplementary Fig. S5). Grasslands and croplands account for the largest mean, total and cumulative burned area, followed by open shrublands. ### Time to burn of FD and non-FD fire clusters 

Across all six analysed land-cover types, wildfire spread is systematically faster following FD conditions than under non-FD conditions. Kaplan–Meier survival curves show a more rapid decline in probability of grid cells remaining unburned after ignition for post-FD fires compared with non-FD fires across all six land-cover types (Fig. 5a–f). Corresponding Cox proportional hazards models confirm this pattern, with hazard ratios (HRs) exceeding one across all land-cover classes. HR values are highest in open shrublands (HR ≈ 1.84), followed by grasslands (≈1.45), croplands (≈1.33), savannas (≈1.22), deciduous broadleaf forests (≈1.19) and woody savannas (≈1.17). These results indicate consistently faster ignition of grid cells within fire clusters when fires occur following FD events. The proportional-hazards model assumes a time-invariant burn rate, though diagnostics indicate possibleviolations in four of the sixanalysed land-cover classes: croplands, grasslands, open shrublands and woody savannas (Supplementary Table S1). No clear violation was detected for deciduous broadleaf forests or savannas. We therefore interpret Cox hazard ratios as summarymeasuresof relativeburningratesover the analysed period,rather than as strictly time-invariant effects, and place greater emphasis on the Kaplan–Meier survival curves when comparing land-cover-specific firespread behaviour. 

In contrast, when drought is defined solely by persistently low soil moisture (20th-percentile) but without requiring rapid intensification from 

FD, the relationship between conventional drought conditions and wildfire spread becomes weak or inconsistent. Survival curves for conventional droughtandnon-droughtconditionslargelyoverlapacrossmostland-cover classes, with hazard ratios near or below one (Supplementary Fig. S6a–f). Fine-fuel ecosystems concentrate much of their burnedarea during the early stages of fire development. In grasslands, croplands, open shrublands and woody savannas, survival curves correspond to a pronounced spike in burning during the first few pentads after ignition, indicating rapid initial spread that consumes a large fraction of the eventual burned area soon after ignition<sup>40,41</sup> . Fires in open, fuel-rich landscapes such as grasslands and open shrublands can propagate rapidly through continuous dry fuels until fuels are exhausted<sup>42,43</sup> . In contrast, woody savanna fires tend to spread more slowly because fuels are patchier, while cropland fires generally remain smaller but can still spread quickly through dry crop residues<sup>43</sup> .

### Does SESR-defined Flash Drought produce similar results?

## Discussion 

FDs are increasingly recognised as rapid-onset hydroclimatic extremes capable of producing cascading impacts across ecosystems and society. Our analysis links FD occurrence to wildfire dynamics across CONUS and demonstrates that rapid soil-moisture depletion is more strongly associated with accelerated wildfire spread than background drought conditions alone. These findings highlight the important contribution of the rate of landsurface drying, alongside drought severity, in shaping wildfire behaviour. 

FDs impair plant physiological function, alter canopy structure and reduce productivity, thereby weakening ecosystem resilience and, in some regions, threatening food security when they coincide with key crop growth stages<sup>2,11,50,51</sup> . Stressed, desiccated vegetation also supplies highly combustible fuels and can reinforce land-atmosphere feedbacks through enhanced evaporative demand and soil-moisture depletion, further intensifying surfaceheatingand fire activity<sup>1,37,46,52</sup> .FDsmayalso preconditionfuelsthrough a two-stage process: initially favourable moisture conditions can sustain vegetation activity and fuel availability, while rapid subsequent RZSM depletion dries this biomass<sup>11</sup> ; this potentially contrasts with conventional

Against this backdrop, our results show that FD occurrence across CONUS is spatially heterogeneous, with hotspots across parts of the Northwest, the south-central region and large areas of the eastern United States. Fire activity, by contrast, is concentrated in the western, southeastern and south-central United States. Post-FD fires tend to occur in regions where wildfire activity is already common, particularly across the central and southeastern United States, indicating that FD-fire co-occurrence is strongest in landscapes already prone to burning. However, these spatial patterns provide little evidence of fire occurrence directly attributable to FD, suggesting that the primary influence of FD is on wildfire behaviour rather than ignition probability. 

Instead, our results show that FD is consistently associated with early fire spread, as demonstrated using Kaplan–Meier survival curves that indicate faster fire spread following RZSM-defined FD events across all six focal land-cover classes. In contrast, when drought is defined solely by low soil-moisture percentiles without requiring rapid intensification, differences in fire spread become weak or inconsistent. This comparison should be interpreted within the drought definitions used here: conventional drought is treated as persistently low RZSM without a constraint for rapid onset, rather than as a complete representation of long-term drought effects such as cumulative fuel drying, atmospheric evaporative demand or vegetation stress. 

Fire-size diagnostics reinforce this conclusion. Typical fire sizesremain broadly similar between post-FD and non-FD fires, while differences in the 

upper tail vary by ecosystem. Post-FD fires are therefore not consistently larger or smaller than non-FD fires, and their faster spread cannot be attributed to differences in final fire size. 

Further analysis linking burn rate to pre-fire soil-moisture deficits shows that the fastest-burning clusters exhibit substantially larger RZSM deficits in the pentads preceding ignition. This metric captures both sustaineddrynessandrapidsoil-moisturedrawdown,suggestingthatsustained drying can lead to accelerated fire spread, but the rapid depletion of rootzone soil moisture provides a pathway toward accelerated fire spread earlier in its lifetime. These findings align with previous work demonstrating that low soil moisture reduces live and dead fuel moisture and increases burn probability and fire spread potential<sup>16,42–47</sup> . 

As this study is based on observational data, these results should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than strict causal effects. Wildfire spread is influenced by multiple interacting factors, including short-term weather conditions, fuel continuity and structure, topography, ignition characteristics and human suppression activities. Our land-coverstratified analysis accounts for broad ecosystem differences in vegetation and fuel type, but these additional meteorological, landscape and anthropogenic factors are not fully controlled for here and may contribute to variability in fire spread. Nevertheless, the consistent contrast between postFD, non-FD and conventional drought conditions suggests that rapid prefire RZSM depletion is an important indicator of faster early fire spread. An additional source of uncertainty is the aggregation of 500 m burned-area detections to the 0.25° analysis grid and pentad resolution, which may smooth fire boundaries and affect estimated fire duration and spread metrics, particularly for small fires. 

Results also indicate the critical role of land cover type in shaping these FD–fire interactions. Grasslands and croplands consistently emerge as the most vulnerable ecosystems: they account for the largest burned area totals and show short FD-to-ignition delays, reflecting shallow rooting depths, limited soil-water storage and highly continuous fine fuels that cure rapidly under high evaporative demand<sup>48</sup> . The cropland and grassland results, however, should be interpreted with some caution. Burned-area detections in human-managed landscapes may include managed or prescribed burning, which can differ from unmanaged wildfire in ignition source, fuel structure, seasonality and suppression or management practices. Our landcover classification identifies cropland-dominated landscapes, but it does not distinguish agricultural burning from wildfire within those landscapes. Therefore, the faster spread signal in cropland- and grassland-dominated landscapes should be interpreted as evidence that post-FD conditions are associated with more rapid burning in these landscapes, rather than as a direct attribution to unmanaged wildfire spread alone. 

Open shrublands show similarly rapid responses due to sparse canopy cover and limited soil-moisture buffering. ## Methods 

The data used, processing steps, and overall methodological framework applied in this study for drought identification, fire processing, and fireclassification workflow are summarised in Supplementary Fig. S10. 

### Data 

Flash drought (FD) identification can be based on a range of hydroclimatic variables that capture rapid soil moisture depletion<sup>9</sup> . Among the most widely used approaches are definitions utilizing model-derived Root Zone Soil Moisture (RZSM) and Standardized Evaporative Stress Ratio (SESR), both of which have beenapplied in regional and global FD studies<sup>16,27,49</sup> . The SESR metric is derived from the Evaporative Stress Ratio (ESR), defined as the ratio of actual evapotranspiration (ET) to potential evapotranspiration (PET)<sup>13</sup> .Additionally,weusepentad(5-day)meansbecauseFDdevelopson sub-monthly timescales, and pentads capture rapid onset and intensification without the smoothing inherent to monthly averages. Pentad aggregation also reduces day-to-day noise in soil-moisture variability while retaining event-scale timing relevant for FD detection<sup>9,13,15,18</sup> . 

Atmospheric and land surface variables were obtained from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) fifthgeneration reanalysis (ERA5). The dataset spans 2001-2024 with a spatial resolution of 0.25° × 0.25°. ERA5 provides physically consistent estimates of land–atmosphere variables and has been widely used for flash-drought detection<sup>27,49,53</sup> and compareswell with other modern reanalysis datasets<sup>12,27</sup> . 

Daily RZSM values were extracted for three ERA5 soil layers: 0–7 cm, 7–28 cm, and 28–100 cm<sup>54</sup> . Integratedroot-zone soil moisture (0 to 100 cm) was computed as a depth-weighted average of these layers. Although actual rooting depths vary among vegetation types, the upper 1 m is commonly used as a standardised large-scale representation of root-zone water availability and enables consistent comparison across ecosystems<sup>11,18,49</sup> . The resulting daily values were aggregated to pentad (5-day) means for subsequentFDidentification.ESRwascalculatedastheratioofETtoPETusing ERA5 evapotranspiration fields. The method used to calculate Potential evapotranspiration in ERA5 was computed using the Penman-Monteith formulation. 

Fire occurrence and land-cover information were obtained from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) satellite products. Burned-area data were derived from the MCD64A1 Collection 6.1 product, which provides global burned-area information at 500 m resolution. Land-cover classifications were obtained from the MCD12Q1 Collection6.1dataset,whichprovidesgloballandcoverdataat500 mresolution annually from 2001 to 2022, following the International GeosphereBiosphere Programme (IGBP) classification scheme with 17 land cover categories<sup>55</sup> . 

The MODIS burned-area product identifies burned pixels using a hybrid algorithm that combines surface reflectance changes with active-fire detections<sup>56</sup> . Burn dates are reported as the ordinal day of the calendar year for each grid cell, and were converted to calendar dates to enable temporal aggregation. 

Both MODIS products were re-gridded to 0.25° × 0.25° spatial resolution to match the ERA5 fields. Burned-area data were subsequently aggregated to pentads to ensure consistency with the temporal resolution used for FD identification. Additionally, burned area from 500 m MODIS pixels was summed within each 0.25° analysis grid cell, so burned area is preserved at the coarse-grid scale. However, this aggregation does not retain sub-grid fire geometry, and fire-cluster boundaries and spread metrics 

shouldthereforebeinterpretedatthe0.25°gridandpentadresolutionrather than as fine-scale fire perimeters or daily fire-front propagation. Land-cover data were also aggregated to the 0.25° analysis grid. For each coarse grid cell, the underlying 500 m MODIS land-cover pixels were examined after excluding fillvaluesandinvalid classes.Aland-coverclasswasassignedonly when it occupiedatleast 50%of the valid 500 mpixels within thecoarse grid cell; otherwise, the cell was treated as having no dominant land-cover class. This approach reduces uncertainty from mixed land-cover pixels, but the resulting classes should be interpreted as dominant coarse-grid land-cover types rather than homogeneous vegetation units. 

### FD and conventional drought identification methods 

Asthereisnouniversallyaccepteddefinitionof flashdrought<sup>57,58</sup> ,weemploy two widely used and conceptually distinctmetrics to identify FD events: one based on rapid depletion of root-zone soil moisture (RZSM) and one based on atmospheric evaporative stress. These approaches have been applied in numerous regional and global studies<sup>12,16,34,53,58,59</sup> and represent complementary perspectives on rapid land-surface drying. The dual-metric framework allows us to assess the sensitivity of FD–fire relationships to the FD definition and to distinguish soil-driven from atmosphere-driven drought development. 

### Root-zone soil-moisture–based flash droughts 

RZSM-based flash droughts are identified following the framework of Yuan etal.<sup>18</sup> ,whichhasbeenwidelyadoptedinpreviousstudies<sup>12,34,53</sup> .Ateachgrid cell, pentad-mean RZSM values were converted to percentile ranks relative to the historical distribution for the corresponding calendar pentad over the full record. Under this definition, an FD event must satisfy three criteria: 

- (1). Pentad-mean RZSM is at or above the 40th percentile, declines by at least 5 percentile points in the following pentad to below the 40th percentile, and subsequently falls below the 20th percentile within the following three pentads; 

- (2). The event persists until RZSM recovers above the 20th percentile; 

- (3). Theminimumdurationoftheeventis fivepentads(25days)toexclude short-lived dry spells. 

To avoid conflating flash droughts with slowly evolving seasonal drought conditions, the maximum FD duration is limited to 18 pentads (90 days). This definition isolates events characterised by rapid and sustained depletion of root-zone soil moisture, which directly influences live and dead fuel moisture and therefore wildfire behaviour.

### Conventional drought definition 

To isolate the influence of soil-moisture deficits independent of rapid drought intensification, we also defined conventional drought events using RZSM percentile thresholds. At each grid cell, a drought event begins when the RZSM percentile falls below a fixed threshold and persists for at least four consecutive pentads. To prevent artificial splitting of events, one temporary above-threshold pentad is permitted within an otherwise continuous below-threshold period. The event terminates when RZSM rises above the threshold for a second time following any allowable exception. 

Two drought thresholds were evaluated to assess sensitivity: the 20th percentile and the 10th percentile of the climatological RZSM distribution. 

### Spatio-temporal clustering of fire and flash drought events 

The MODIS burned-area product provides the locations and dates of daily fire detections. To ensure consistency with the flash-drought diagnostics, daily fire detections were aggregated to pentads. A fire event was recorded for a pentad if burning occurred on any day within that 5-day period. 

To analyse event-scale behaviour, both fire and FD occurrences were represented as spatio-temporal clusters. Each cluster consists of spatially contiguous grid cells exhibiting temporal continuity across successive pentads. Isolated burned pixels were retained as individual clusters because even small fires can have ecological and operational significance. 

A post-FD fire event was defined as a fire cluster whose ignition area overlapped with an active FD cluster after FD onset. The ignition area corresponds to the set of pixels that burn first within each fire cluster, and at leastonegrid cellinthisignitionareahadtobepartofanongoingFDcluster for the fire to be classified as post-FD. Fires were therefore not classified as post-FD solely because an FD had occurred earlierat the same location. FDto-ignition delay was calculated as the number of pentads from the first pentad at which the associatedFD event started to the ignition pentad of the fire cluster. FD status was assignedat the cluster level based on conditions in the ignition area prior to onset, so clusters classified as non-FD can later spread into FD-affected grid cells but retain their original classification. Land-cover classification for each cluster was determined using an areaweighted approach based on the dominant land-cover type within the cluster footprint. 

For each fire cluster, we calculated metrics describing pre-fire soil moisture conditions and fire spread intensity. Pre-fire soil moisture deficit was defined as the cumulative root-zone soil-moisture (RZSM) shortfall below the 20th-percentile threshold over the four pentads preceding ignition. For each pentad, the deficit was calculated as the positive difference between the 20th-percentile climatological RZSM threshold and observed RZSMacrossallgridcellswithintheclusterandthensummedoverthefourpentad period. 

Firespread intensitywasquantified usingburnrate,defined as thetotal burned area of a fire cluster divided by its duration, yielding the mean burned area per pentad.
