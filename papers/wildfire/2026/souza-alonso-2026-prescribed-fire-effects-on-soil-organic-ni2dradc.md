# Prescribed fire effects on soil organic matter in a eucalypt forest previously affected by severe wildfire

## Abstract

Wildfires are integral to the dynamics of many ecosystems yet shifting fire regimes around the world pose challenges for biodiversity, carbon (C) cycling, and soil conservation. Prescribed fires (PF) are widely used to mitigate wildfire risk, yet their ecological consequences in areas previously affected by wildfires remain poorly understood. This study examines quantitative and qualitative impacts of a low-to-moderate severity PF on soil organic matter (SOM) in a dry eucalypt forest of the Greater Sydney area (Australia), 13 years after a severe wildfire. During the PF, temperatures in the litter layer and Oa horizon reached ∼900 °C, exceeding 300 °C for up to 4 min. In contrast, in the Ah horizon (0–2 cm) of the mineral soil, temperatures remained below 60 °C. The PF strongly affected soil surface SOM quantity, reducing the C stock by ∼87% (9.1 to 1.1 Mg ha −1 ). Thermal analysis (TG-DSC) and 13 C NMR spectroscopy revealed qualitative SOM transformations once the litter and the Oa horizon became ash, increasing thermal recalcitrance, reducing C-labile fractions, and a dominance of aromatic C, indicating incomplete combustion and pyrogenic C formation. By contrast, the Ah horizon (0–2 cm) showed limited changes but presented a highly recalcitrant SOM, probably reflecting the legacy of the previous severe wildfire. These findings highlight a critical trade-off presented by using PF in fire-prone eucalypt forests: while effectively reducing fuel loads, PF significantly diminished SOM stocks, but also increased the thermal recalcitrance of remaining SOM. These changes can be expected influence post-fire C cycling and enhance the persistence of this C pool in surface soil horizons. By examining both immediate PF effects and the legacy of a previous wildfire, this study contributes to a better understanding of SOM dynamics in fire-prone ecosystems and highlights the importance of considering soil C transformations when using PF as a fire-prevention tool.

## **1. Introduction** 

In naturally fire-prone ecosystems, wildfires within their typical fire regimes represent an important ecological process, playing a key role in landscape dynamics, influencing biogeochemical cycling, maintaining biodiversity and supporting overall ecosystem health (Ambrosia and Brass, 1988; Neary et al., 2005; Kelly et al., 2020). Fires are one of the key nutrient recycling pathways, as they break down plant biomass, promoting the release of nutrients back into the soil, where they become 

available again for plant and microorganism uptake (Pausas and Bond, 2020). 

As a consequence of human-driven global change, fire regimes are being altered around the world, with wildfire frequency and severity increasing in recent years in many regions, especially in extratropical forests (Jones et al., 2024; Cunningham et al., 2024). This has multidimensional effects on nutrient cycling, carbon (C) sequestration, and soil conservation (Moghli et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2021; Tran et al., 2020). forests have co-evolved with fire and display diverse fire-adaptation strategies (Prendergast-Miller et al., 2017). Nevertheless, despite being fire-adapted, increasingly severe wildfires can lead to ecological degradation, affecting plant and fauna communities, as well as soils and waters (Bowd et al., 2019; Driscoll et al., 2024). During wildfires, many soil properties can be altered, including enhanced water repellency, reduced infiltration rates and increased susceptibility to erosion (Doerr et al., 2006; Prosser, 1990; Shakesby et al., 2007; Olivares-Martinez et al., 2023). Such changes can contribute to soil degradation through several mechanisms such as soil organic matter losses, soil nutrient depletion, disruption of soil structure and decrease of microbial activity, especially in the upper soil layers (Prendergast-Miller et al., 2017; Merino et al., 2021; Tulau et al., 2021). 

The severity of fire impacts on soils is determined by many interlinked variables, and fire effects vary substantially depending on biome, fire severity, soil depth, slope, vegetation type, and fire recurrence (Knicker, 2007; Kelly et al., 2020; Pellegrini et al., 2020; Fontúrbel et al., 2021; Oliveira et al., 2023). This complex interaction emphasizes the need for holistic fire management and mitigation strategies that take soil conservation into account (Badía et al., 2017; Bowman et al., 2013; Keeley, 2009; Scharenbroch et al., 2012). The impact of wildfire on litter and SOM content and quality is one of the most important aspects affecting soil conservation, since the organic component of soil drives many aspects of the post-fire soil recovery, including physical (e.g. structure, infiltration) and biochemical (e.g. fertility and microbial activity) parameters (Merino et al., 2016). In addition to the soil C or SOM content, it is also important to consider soil C in qualitative terms. Fire altered SOM tends to be more recalcitrant, with a lower presence of carbohydrates, higher content of aromatic groups and higher C/N ratios (Knicker, 2007), especially where fire has been recurrent (Fontúrbel et al., 2021). Such changes are generally limited to the upper few soil centimeters (Santín and Doerr, 2019) and are caused by the incomplete burning of SOM and consequent pyrogenic C formation, together with inputs of charred biomass (Brye, 2006). 

In order to reduce wildfire risk and mitigate its impacts, fuel management strategies such as mechanical treatments, grazing and prescribed fire (PF) are increasingly used to reduce fuel loads. Although prescribed fire is used in many countries around the world (Bonner et al., 2021; Diniz et al., 2023; Fernandes et al., 2013), there are some regions where its use has a longer tradition and is particularly relevant, as is the case in southeastern Australia. In Australian fire-prone forests, PF is the most widely used management tool to reduce fuel loads and the likelihood of large-scale wildfires (National Burning Project, 2021). Prescribed fire is usually carried out here under low-moderate burn severity (i.e. with severity indicating the degree of damage to vegetation and or/ soil), while attempting to emulate natural fire regimes and promote ecosystem health (Burrows, 2009; Trappe et al., 2006). When carried out under appropriate conditions, PF has usually a lower impact on soil properties than high severity wildfire (Agbeshie et al., 2022; Fontúrbel et al., 2021; Merino et al., 2019; Pereira et al., 2021). Nevertheless, PF impacts can be spatially highly variable and, in some cases, be as substantial as those recorded for wildfires (Pressler et al., 2019; Roces-Díaz et al., 2022). 

In Australia, several studies have addressed the short- and long-term effects of PF on SOM content, including total and pyrogenic C (Krishnaraj et al., 2016; Sawyer et al., 2018;), or soluble and labile C (Hobley et al., 2019; Muqaddas et al., 2019) and related parameters such as soil heterotrophic respiration (Butler et al., 2019). The studies available indicate that changes in soil C depend on fire intensity and season. For example, the higher fire intensity reached in PFs carried out in autumn relative to PFs conducted in spring (due to higher fuel loads and lower fuel moisture) usually leads to consumption of greater amounts of SOM and litter (Bennett et al., 2014). Overall, the application of PF has been found to decrease C, N and P stocks in Australian forests (Bennett et al., 2014; Muqaddas et al., 2015, 2016; Santín et al., 2018). 

Regarding the potential impacts of PF outlined above, substantial uncertainty remains about the time needed for soil to recover between PFs (Muqaddas et al., 2016) or the consequences of the repeated application of prescribed fire on SOM quality. It seems clear that the consideration of fire interval is essential, with increased PF frequencies driving a reduction in C, while reduced fire frequency yields minimal or no impact on C quantity in dry eucalypt forests of SE-Australia (Bennett et al., 2014; Guinto et al., 2001; Hopmans, 2003). In these very fireprone ecosystems, it is relatively common to find a PF carried out in areas that have previously been affected by wildfires with a relatively short return interval (some years to a couple of decades between fires). However, apart from some modelling simulation (Chick et al., 2019), studies analyzing the combined effects of wildfires and subsequent PF on soil properties are scarce (Bennett et al., 2017). The present study addresses this knowledge gap by capitalising on the rare opportunity of examining the impact of PF through pre- and postfire sampling as well as in-fire temperature monitoring in a dry mixed-sclerophyll eucalyptus forest in SE Australia, burned 13 years previously by a severe wildfire. We focus on the quantity and quality of the organic matter of the litter layer and the near surface mineral soil given that these are the layers that experience the greatest perturbations through heating and combustion during fires (Certini, 2005; Santín and Doerr, 2019; Doerr et al., 2025). 

## **2. Materials and methods** 

## _2.1. Study area_ 

The study was conducted in a dry eucalypt forest in the Hawkesbury Sandstone Tablelands, adjacent to Lake Burragorang and directly bordering the Blue Mountains National Park, approximately 60 km west of Sydney, New South Wales, SE Australia (Wollondilly Shire, Greater Sydney; 33<sup>◦</sup> 52′16″ S, 150<sup>◦</sup> 36′04″ E, WGS84; 200 m a.s.l.; Fig. 1). Lake Burragorang is the main reservoir among Sydney's water supply catchments. The landscape is characterized by deep canyons and gently sloping plateaus. The bedrock consists mainly of quartzitic Hawkesbury Sandstone with shale outcrops, and the dominant soils are Kandosols according to the Australian Soil Classification (Australian Government, 2018). These sandy to sandy-clay loams are widespread in Australia and typical of eucalypt forests on sandstone plateaus in SE Australia (Doerr et al., 2006; Australian Government, 2018). They are characterized by a thin, organic rich surface Oa horizon, typically 1 to 2 cm thick (Santín et al., 2018). The climate is humid temperate, with an average annual rainfall of 900–1000 mm, warm summers and cool winters. Open, dry sclerophyll forest is the dominant type of vegetation cover (Keith, 2004). Most of the trees (reaching 10–30 m in height) belong to the genus _Eucalyptus_ , including ironbark ( _Eucalyptus fibrosa_ ), stringybarks ( _Eucalyptus eugenioides_ , _E. oblonga_ ) and scribbly gum ( _Eucalyptus sclerophylla_ ). The shrubby understorey (up to 4 m high) is dominated by _Banksia_ sp., _Leptospernum_ sp., _Acacia_ sp. and _Petrophile_ sp. These forests are dominated by fire-adapted resprouter species that recover rapidly (5–7 years) after fire (Caccamo et al., 2015; Heath et al., 2016). The wildfire return interval is around 14–21 years (Enright et al., 2012), and prescribed fires are widely used for hazardous fuel management at shorter intervals ( _<_ 10 years) (Guinto et al., 2001).

The prescribed fire was carried out on 23 September 2014, in an area previously affected by a severe wildfire in 2001 (Chafer et al., 2004), with the objective of reducing the substantial fuel (up to 25 t ha<sup>−1</sup> ) that had accumulated occurred over the 13 years since the wildfire (Santín et al., 2018). 

An experimental plot (~2000 m<sup>2</sup> ) was established for research purposes within the area to be burned (Fig. 1). The plot was located on a flat ridge top and had the highest and most homogenous fuel load within

## _2.3. Fire monitoring and sampling procedure_ 

Before the fire, three transects (T1-T3), each 30 m long and spaced 30 m apart) were set in the experimental plot. Ten sampling points were established in each transect (one every 3 m, resulting in a total of 30 sampling points, Fig. 1a). K-type thermocouples were placed at all sampling points to record the temperature during the fire (every 10 s) using Easylog (Lascar) dataloggers. One thermocouple was placed in the litter layer (~1 cm depth) ( _n_ = 30) at all sampling points, while at selected sampling points thermocouples were placed on the surface of the Oa soil horizon ( _n_ = 9) and at 1 cm depth within the Ah soil horizon ( _n_ = 4) (Table S1). Prior to the fire, two additional parallel 30 m transects (CA and CB) were laid out between the three post-fire sampling transects and control samples collected (Fig. 1b). Sampling points were established every 3 m (20 sampling points in total) and at each sampling 

point, the entire litter layer within a sampling square of 20 × 20 cm was collected. The Oa horizon (i.e. uppermost surface soil layer, dark, of low cohesion and mostly consisting of well decomposed organic matter; Fig. 1b) was also quantitatively sampled in the same 20 × 20 cm square. Finally, the surface Ah horizon below the Oa layer was also sampled to a depth of 2 cm in the centre of the square with a 5 cm diameter sampling core. 

The fire did not affect the tree canopy, but it consumed most of the understory vegetation (pre-fire load ~2.6 t ha<sup>−1</sup> ; García-Carmona et al., 2025) and the litter layer, leaving an ash layer that was collected a few hours after the fire, using a 20 × 20 cm sampling square at each sampling point of T1-T3, with the aid of a soft brush (Fig. 1c). No uncharred O horizon remained at any of the sampling points, and the charred material resulting from the burning of the O horizon had become part of the collected ash layer (Fig. 1). Subsequently, samples of the Ah soil horizon were collected in the centre of the 20 × 20 cm square, with a 5 cm diameter sampling core to a depth of 2 cm (i.e. 0–2 cm). A field-based visual classification of fire severity, based on the degree of vegetation destruction, was also carried out at each sampling point, following the classification developed for this region by Chafer et al. (2004). For the

## _2.4. Laboratory analysis_ 

All samples were air-dried and weighed on a top-loading balance. In total, 20 pre-fire litter samples, 20 pre-fire Oa horizon samples, 20 prefire Ah horizon samples (0–2 cm depth), 30 post-fire ash samples and 30 post-fire Ah horizon samples (0–2 cm depth) were collected. To reduce analyses of the litter samples to a manageable number, 20 samples were pooled into a single composite sample, from which three replicates were used for further analyses. Of the 30 ash samples, seven were excluded because the corresponding temperature records were unavailable due to thermologger malfunction. For soil analyses, 10 pre-fire Oa samples, 10 pre-fire Ah samples, and 9 post-fire Ah samples were selected to represent a broad range of fire impacts based on the maximum temperatures and heating durations recorded during the prescribed fire (Table S1). 

## _2.4.1. Sample preparation and, total organic C and N analyses_ 

All soil samples were air-dried and passed through a 2 mm sieve and the ash samples through a 1 cm sieve to remove charcoal fragments and unburnt vegetal material. Subsamples of all collected materials (i.e. ash, litter, Oa horizon and Ah mineral soil) were ground before further analysis. Elemental analysis and thermogravimetry-differential scanning calorimetry analyses (TG-DSC) were carried out on all of these subsamples. In addition, a selection of samples was used for solid-state 13C Nuclear Magnetic Resonance spectroscopy (13C NMR CP-MAS). Litter, ash, Oa and Ah layer samples were analyzed for organic C and N concentrations using an automated C analyzer (LECO Elemental Analyzer). Carbon stocks (Mg C ha<sup>−1</sup> ) were calculated from the dry mass of each layer and its corresponding C concentration determined by elemental analysis. For the Ah horizon (0–2 cm), C stocks were estimated considering soil bulk density and C concentration and expressed on an area basis (García-Carmona et al., 2025). 

## _2.4.2. Thermogravimetry-differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC)_ 

Samples were analyzed by TG-DSC in a thermogravimetric analyzer (Mettler Toledo). Soil samples (4 mg) were placed in aluminium pans under dry air (O2 flux rate: 50 mL<sup>−1</sup> ) and heated from 50 to 600<sup>◦</sup> C (heating rate of 10<sup>◦</sup> C min<sup>−1</sup> ). The heat of combustion (Q, in J per gram) was determined by integrating the TG-DSC curves (in Wg<sup>−1</sup> ) over the exothermic region (150–600<sup>◦</sup> C). For each TG-DSC thermograph, the area in the 150–600<sup>◦</sup> C region, where combustion of SOM occurs, was divided into three temperature sections that correspond to exotherms (W1, W2, W3) representing the different levels of resistance to thermal oxidation (Merino et al., 2015, 2023): labile SOM, mainly comprising carbohydrates, proteins and other labile aliphatic compounds (150 _<_ T1 _<_ 375<sup>◦</sup> C); recalcitrant SOM, such as lignin and other polyphenols (375 _<_ T2 _<_ 475<sup>◦</sup> C); and highly recalcitrant SOM, such as polycondensed aromatic forms (475 _<_ T3 _<_ 600<sup>◦</sup> C). The resulting partial heats of combustion representing these three regions were calculated as Q1, Q2 and Q3, respectively. 

## _2.4.3. Solid state_<sup>_13_</sup> _C CP-MAS NMR spectroscopy_ 

A selected subset of representative samples (i.e. covering a range of variability within each of the sample types studied here) were analyzed by solid state<sup>13</sup> C NMR analysis using an Agilent Varian VNMRS-500-WB spectrometer. The equipment was operated at a proton resonance frequency of 500 MHz, with a zirconia rotor volume of 160 μL. Carbon chemical shifts were expressed relative to the C methylene signal of solid 

adamantane at 28.92 ppm. Cross Polarization Magic Angle Spinning (1D CPMAS) analysis was carried out under the following conditions: contact time 1 ms, inter-scan delay 1 s (a proton T1 experiment was performed to check the suitability of this time), and MAS rate 12 kHz. The number of scans ranged from approximately 10,000-35,000. The crosspolarization time was set at 1 ms. Spectra processing and integration were performed using MestreNova software 8.1.0 (Mestrelab Research Inc., University of Santiago de Compostela). For quantification, each spectrum was divided into four regions representing different chemical environments of the<sup>13</sup> C nucleus: alkyl C (0–45 ppm), O-alkyl C (45–110 ppm), aromatic C (110–160 ppm), and carbonyl C (160–210 ppm). Signal intensities were corrected for spinning sidebands according to Knicker et al. (2005). 

## _2.5. Statistical analyses_ 

The normality and homoscedasticity of the data were first analyzed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene's test, respectively. After this, the effect of prescribed burning on the independent variables (total C and N, C/N, W1, W2, W3, T50w) was evaluated by comparing means using oneway ANOVA in the case of more than 2 levels (litter, Oa horizon and ash) or _t_ -test in the case of only 2 levels (Ah horizon). Tukey's test was selected as the post-hoc test for pairwise comparison, and significance was detected at the _P <_ 0.05 level. Data obtained by integration of the areas of interest in the<sup>13</sup> C NMR spectra were then compared through hierarchical clustering, using the Euclidean distance as the construction measure. Thermogravimetic data was represented using the first derivative of the TG curves (dTG), generated with the STARe Evaluation Software (Mettler Toledo, v17.0). 

## **3. Results** 

## _3.1. Fire-induced temperatures and duration, and fire severity_ 

In the litter layer, the PF generated maximum temperatures between 500 and 750<sup>◦</sup> C at most sampling points (range 148–889<sup>◦</sup> C; average 567 ◦C) (Table S1). These high temperatures were of short duration, with temperatures above 300<sup>◦</sup> C lasting less than 4 min at most points (range 0–680 s; average 171 s). Relatively high peak temperatures were also recorded in the Oa horizon, and, although lower than in the litter layer (range 94–781<sup>◦</sup> C; average 474<sup>◦</sup> C), the duration of temperatures above 300<sup>◦</sup> C was longer here than in the litter layer (range 0–787 s; average 291 s). By contrast, temperatures recorded in the Ah horizon remained below 52<sup>◦</sup> C in all cases (Table S1). 

Fire severity in our experimental plot was classified as high in 21 of the 30 sampling points and as moderate in the remaining nine points, according to the effect on vegetation (Chafer et al., 2004) (Table S1). High-severity points were characterized by complete combustion of ground and understorey fuels ( _<_ 4 m height), with partial canopy scorching, whereas moderate severity points showed less extensive vegetation damage. The fire resulted in the formation of a continuous ash layer, consisting of burnt residues from ground fuels (litter) and understorey vegetation, together with the combusted Oa horizon. This ash layer was consistently darker and more wettable than the underlying uncharred mineral soil, which was brighter in colour and water repellent at all sampling points (Santín et al., 2018) (Fig. 1).

The C/N ratios were higher in the unburnt litter (72.5) than in the Oa horizon (39.0). After the fire, the C and N concentrations in the resulting ash were 28.7 and 0.64%, respectively, and the C/N ratio was 44.2. ## _3.3. Prescribed fire impact on SOM quality - thermal analysis (TG-DSC)_ 

## _3.3.1. Pre-fire: Litter and Oa horizon_ 

TG-DSC analysis of the unburnt litter revealed two dominant peaks of contrasting thermal stability: a prominent peak at 320<sup>◦</sup> C and a smaller secondary peak at 430<sup>◦</sup> C (Fig. 4a). Here, most of the OM (53%) can therefore be considered thermolabile, combusting at temperatures below 375<sup>◦</sup> C. The Oa horizon yielded peaks in the same regions, although much less pronounced. In this case, the secondary peak at 430<sup>◦</sup> C was more prominent than the first at 320<sup>◦</sup> C, indicating a loss of labile compounds during the decomposition process of the OM from the litter to the Oa horizon. The proportion of compounds consumed at intermediate temperatures (300–400<sup>◦</sup> C), also increased in the Oa layer compared to the litter. Correspondingly, the T50w, the temperature at 

which 50% of the OM is lost (considered an index of thermal recalcitrance; Merino et al., 2018), also increased, from 356<sup>◦</sup> C in the litter to 384<sup>◦</sup> C in the Oa layer (Table 1). 

## _3.3.2. Post-fire: Ash (litter and Oa layer residue)_ 

The shape of the TG curves of ash samples was very different to those of the pre-PF litter and Oa horizon, and also showed greater heterogeneity due to the different types of source material and their temperatures reached during the PF (Fig. 5). Considering the average values (Fig. 4a), the thermolabile peak (320<sup>◦</sup> C) observed in the litter and Oa samples was not present in the ash, and there was an increase in the area corresponding to the region between 400 and 570<sup>◦</sup> C, with a prominent peak at 430<sup>◦</sup> C. In this case, the relationships between the fractions were reversed, with a notable reduction in W1 (27.5%), a moderate increase in W2 (51%) and a significant increase in W3 (21.5%), due to transformation of the OM during burning (Table 1). After the prescribed fire, the<sup>13</sup> C NMR spectra of the different ash samples produced at different temperatures exhibited a very distinctive pattern. The alkyl C and O-alkyl C fractions were greatly reduced in comparison with the pre-fire organic layers, whereas the proportion of aromatic-C fraction almost tripled, clearly dominating the spectra. On average, the aromatic signals represented 51% of the ash spectra, followed by O-alkyl C, alkyl C and carboxyl-C compounds, with values of 28.5%, 17.1% and 3.4%, respectively. Within the ash samples formed at the higher recorded temperatures (498–816.5<sup>◦</sup> C), the predominant signal was generally that of aromatic C at 130 ppm (pyrogenic aromatic compounds). Even the ash samples produced at the lower recorded maximum temperature (498<sup>◦</sup> C) showed noticeable significant signals in the aromatic-C region (54%) (Fig. 6). Despite the change in the proportions of alkyl C, O-alkyl C and aromatic-C fractions, no clear trend relating the maximum temperature recorded during the fire and the intensity of the aromatic signal was detected. 

Before the PF, the<sup>13</sup> C NMR spectra of the Ah horizon showed relatively homogeneous proportions of the different functional C groups: 23%, 37%, and 36% for the alkyl C, O-alkyl C and aromatic-C fractions, respectively. After the PF, changes were limited to a slight increase in the O-alkyl C signal, and a slight decrease in the aromatic-C (Fig. 6). 

In detail, the hierarchical classification based on the similarity in profile (ratio) of the different groups of C compounds clearly separated samples into 3 main clusters (Fig. S1). Thus, the analysis distinguished post-fire samples (ash) from pre-fire (litter and Oa horizon) samples and Ah horizon samples, although the latter two appear within the same branch. Ah horizon samples, characterized by an intermediate C-compound profile, appeared to be more related to pre-fire organic samples. The only exception was the M60 sample (ash), which exceptionally appeared linked to mineral soils, likely because its C-compound profile was less aromatic and more dominated by alkyl C and O-alkyl compounds than the other ash samples. 

Finally, the data from thermologgers, elemental analysis, thermogravimetry and NMR spectroscopy enabled several linear relationships to be established. Thus, in the litter and Oa samples, the alkyl C / O-alkyl C ratio was positively related to the C/N ratio after the prescribed fire (R<sup>2</sup> = 0.89; Fig. S2a). The limited number of NMR samples for the mineral soil precluded the establishment of a robust relationship between these two ratios (data not shown). In addition, the data from the thermogravimetric analysis and NMR were significantly related. Thermal stability, measured as T50w, was significantly correlated with the degree of aromaticity measured by<sup>13</sup> C NMR (R<sup>2</sup> = 0.74; Fig. S2b). 

## **4. Discussion** 

The use of controlled fire to mitigate the impacts of wildfires has a long tradition in Australia, ranging from its ancestral Aboriginal uses to its increasing popularity over the past century in the form of prescribed fires (Grant and Wouters, 1993), and culminating in its high demand today (National Burning Project, 2021). When assessing the benefits and limitations of PF, it is important to bear in mind their effects on soils; especially in areas like the studied here, dominated by thin, nutrient poor soils, which are subjected to frequent fires. Considering the date of the previous wildfire in the study site (13 years before), the PF was applied slightly below the reported wildfire return interval of around 14–21 years (Enright et al., 2012). Thus, the PF might represent an additional perturbation with potential effects on soil quality, the focus of this study, as or other impacts (e.g. on plant and faunal communities or hydrological processes that are outside of the scope of the present study), which are beyond the scope of the present study. 

## _4.1. Burn severity in fire-adapted eucalyptus ecosystems_ 

The temperatures and fire severities recorded in our study showed a high level of spatial variability, with moderate to high fire severities recorded at some points. Although the spatial variability may not have been fully captured, temperature data, along with the visual damage assessment showed that the PF was of overall intermediate fire severity (García-Carmona et al., 2025). 

Before the PF, and 13 years after the last wildfire, the soil had a welldeveloped litter layer and Oa horizon (33.1 Mg ha<sup>−1</sup> combined organic layer), as a result of a fast recovery typical of these fire-adapted ecosystems with pyrophytic vegetation. The loss of C mass (litter + Oa) observed for the different burn severities were within the wide range (18–100%) reported by Nave et al. (2011) and Merino et al. (2015) for different ecosystems. According to these studies, consumption of the organic layer reflects variations in the flammability of different types of litter and fire characteristics. After the PF, a layer of ash, of up to 35 Mg ha<sup>−1</sup> , was present, originating predominantly from the combustion of litter layer and the Oa horizon, with only a minor contribution from burned understory vegetation (García-Carmona et al., 2025). Despite this severe level of combustion of the litter layer and the Oa horizon, the temperatures recorded at the surface of the Ah horizon were low, in all cases below 60<sup>◦</sup> C. 

## _4.2. Soil C and N in the surface (litter_ + _Oa) soil and Ah horizon_ 

Due to the prescribed nature of the fire, high C losses in the Ah horizon of the mineral soil were not expected, as these fires are usually timed and performed to have minimal impacts on soils. With increasing depth, the proportion of SOM associated with the mineral fraction (MAOM) becomes more dominant, so most of the SOM is protected by mineral interactions (Merino et al., 2021), making the OM less susceptible to combustion thanks to this physical protection. This may be particularly important in sandy soils (as the quartzitic sandy soils of our study), which contain fewer fine particles that provide protection for SOM. In this sense, shorter periods were considered to have modified soil chemical properties and forest floor nutrients in eucalypt forests of

south-east Queensland, Australia (Guinto et al., 2001). Considering the key role of fine particle size in MAOM protection and its importance for the conservation of SOM in the long-term (Lavallee et al., 2020), reduced time intervals between fires, due to wildfire/prescribed fire alternation such as that observed in this study, may therefore be detrimental. In addition, although PF led to substantial C losses from the Oa layer, temperatures in the Ah horizon remained too low (lower than 60 ◦C) for SOM combustion. 

PF under low-moderate severity conditions is generally considered to cause the redistribution of a substantial fraction of the fire-affected C between different ecosystem compartments, rather than its complete loss into the atmosphere. This is associated with the production and transfer of pyrogenic OM to the surface mineral soil (Volkova and Weston, 2013; García-Carmona et al., 2025). This process is mostly due to the deposition of ash and charred vegetation derived from burnt surface vegetation (Knicker et al., 2005; Krishnaraj et al., 2016; Santín et al., 2020; Vega et al., 2013). Considering the low temperatures reached in the Ah horizon and the fine nature of the ash produced, this mechanism seems to provide a reasonable explanation for the pattern observed. The C stock of the sum of the litter and Oa horizon and mineral Ah horizon (0–2 cm) was thus reduced by 37% (from 17.7 Mg C ha<sup>−1</sup> to 11.2 Mg C ha<sup>−1</sup> ). 

In the present study, the N concentrations in the ash layer increased with increasing temperature, reflecting a relative enrichment of N- containing compounds after burning. The analyses of ash showed that C/N ratios were similar to one of their organic precursors (Oa horizon), but significantly lower than the litter ( _P_ ≤ 0.001), probably due to the higher volatilization of C than of N (Santín et al., 2016a). Nevertheless, high C/N ratios observed in the uppermost Ah horizon before the PF were much higher than usually observed in pre-fire soils (Vega et al., 2013), indicating the accumulation of recalcitrant organic forms in charred material from previous fires. 

## _4.3. SOM quality in the surface soil (litter, Oa and Ah horizon (0_ – _2 cm))_ 

The<sup>13</sup> C NMR spectra showed that the upper Ah horizon (0–2 cm), both before and after the PF, had a substantial proportion of aromatic C (54–66%) and comparatively low proportion of O-alkyl C compounds (19–22%). This is very similar to SOM quality previously reported in soils burned at medium to high severity in eucalypt plantations in the Iberian Peninsula (Merino et al., 2015). Thermal analysis and NMR spectroscopy revealed a high degree of carbonization/aromatization in the ash samples collected after PF. The high spatial variability observed in the ash samples can be attributed to the variability in the fire behavior and associated temperatures reached during ash formation (between 147<sup>◦</sup> C and 889<sup>◦</sup> C), reflecting the heterogeneity in different factors, such as fuel load, soil moisture, and fire residence time (Bodí et al., 2014). The abundance of recalcitrant OM in the ash layer would be expected to make it more resistant to microbial decomposition than their non-burned precursors (Fontúrbel et al., 2021). As previously observed in other studies, even for highest temperatures observed during the fire, the organic component of the ash is less recalcitrant (aromaticity _<_ 60; T50w _<_ 450<sup>◦</sup> C) than woody biochar generated by pyrolysis, which is richer in highly polycondensed aromatic compounds (Keiluweit et al., 2010; Leifeld, 2007). This is partially due to the chemical composition of the original materials (i.e. lower degree of aromatization in charred residues generated by low lignified vegetation (Keiluweit et al., 2010; McBeath et al., 2013) and, also, to the wildfire-derived pyrogenic matter being generally less recalcitrant than pyrogenic materials produced through industrial pyrolysis (Santín et al., 2017; Snihur et al., 2025). This difference is mostly attributed to a combination of higher maximum temperatures, shorter fire residence times and more oxygen availability during wildfire compared to laboratory burning and industrial pyrolysis (Merino et al., 2015; Santín et al., 2016b). These pyrogenic materials generally exhibit a lower potential for long-term C sequestration than those generated by industrial pyrolysis (Santín et al., 2017), and they 

also differ in the mobility and transport behavior of their associated chemical elements (Snihur et al., 2025). 

In contrast, the SOM in the Ah horizon showed little alterations in OM quality after PF. Previous research has shown that PFs can induce variable effects on mineral SOM content and quality, ranging from losses to slight increases, depending on the biome, fuel and soil characteristics, and fire behavior and frequency (Alcaniz et al., 2018˜ ; Fontúrbel et al., 2021; Merino et al., 2019). In this case, the burn was carried out in very early (Austral) spring, when mineral soil moisture levels are typically higher than in summer, which limits downward heat transfer (Fontúrbel et al., 2021). Although high temperatures ( _>_ 500<sup>◦</sup> C) were reached in the surface of the organic soil layer (Oa), the temperature decreased sharply in the Ah mineral horizon ( _<_ 60<sup>◦</sup> C) and consequently, slight differences observed are most likely attributable to inherent variability among samples rather than to changes in the SOM induced by PF. Also, dTG curves showed a high degree of thermal recalcitrance even in the pre-fire samples. As the PF effects on the Ah horizon can be considered limited, this relatively high degree of SOM recalcitrance is likely attributable to fire-derived OM remaining after the 2001 wildfire or even previous fires. 

A previous study examining the effects of the 2001 wildfire on soil properties in a nearby area within the same catchment studied here, estimated that temperatures of at least 250–350<sup>◦</sup> C had been reached during the wildfire in the top 0.5–5 cm mineral soil layer, based on the thermally sensitive elimination of the soil water repellency (Doerr et al., 2006). Thus, it is possible that the previous severe wildfire led to in situ transformation of SOM through combustion, increasing its recalcitrance and reducing the proportion of labile OM compounds (Fern´andez et al., 2011; Gonzalez-P´ ´erez et al., 2004; Knicker, 2007). In addition, the high recalcitrance of the OM in the Ah horizon may also reflect the vertical movement into the mineral soil of pyrogenic organic materials, including charcoal fragments and ash generated during the wildfire (Krishnaraj et al., 2016; Moreno-Roso et al., 2023). Although this is supported by the relatively high rates of soil bioturbation in this region (Richards et al., 2011), our study only considers the upper layer of the mineral soil (0–2 cm) and thus, further studies would be necessary to explore changes in deeper layers of the soil. 

## _4.4. Ecological implications of PF on soils already affected by wildfires_ 

Fire leads to a rapid release of nutrients from vegetation and soils, both via volatilization in the form of gases and airborne particles and, also, via ash generation. Ash is also highly mobile by wind and water erosion (Bodí et al., 2014; Lewis et al., 2021; Thurman et al., 2023) what, together with nutrient volatilization during fire (Barkley et al., 2019) can result in a depletion of nutrient pools in the fire-affected ecosystem. This is especially relevant in oligotrophic and recurrently burned ecosystems such as the dry sclerophyll eucalyptus forest studied here. In addition, we observed an increasing recalcitrance of SOM quality due to fire, what can lead to an even higher nutrient limitation. This recalcitrant SOM has a lower proportion of carbohydrates, which can potentially alter soil structure and reduce infiltration (Brye, 2006 Merino et al., 2021), while enhancing the already high values of cation exchange capacity (CEC) and base saturation, decreasing soil fertility (Dymov et al., 2021). Although the accumulated recalcitrant OM can be considered as an important C sink, it may slow down the cycling of some other nutrients, such as P and N (Santín et al., 2018; Souza-Alonso et al., 2024). 

Moreover, fire increases the relative contribution of persistent/pyrogenic SOM inputs that remain in soil for years to decades (Leuthold et al., 2025). Biologically, a higher proportion of recalcitrant C fractions influences also the SOM stability by modifying the environmental drivers of decomposition (Pellegrini et al., 2020), since increasing thermal stability of SOM negatively affects microbial activity (Harvey et al., 2012; Kurganova et al., 2019). Although not directly assessed in this study, the increase in chemically condensed and thermally

While fire is clearly a key driver for nutrient cycling in this oligotrophic “fire driven system” (Pausas and Bond, 2020) and PF represents a powerful management tool, it demands careful practical calibration (timing, intensity, and frequency) to balance wildfire mitigation needs with long-term soil and ecosystem health. Considering this trade-off between fire risk reduction and potential long-term soil degradation and associated ecosystem health impacts, the cumulative impact of fires on SOM stability deserves careful attention to prevent or reduce fireinduced soil physical and chemical degradation. Given that PF is an important tool for fire management and ecosystem maintenance in many Australian eucalyptus forests, subjected also to frequent wildfires, PF frequency and severity should be carefully balanced in order to not compromise long-term soil functionality and ecosystem health. These insights are increasingly important as fire activity, especially in forests, intensifies due to climate change (Jones et al., 2024). Indeed, a high vulnerability to fire regime change is projected for all pyroregions across Australia (Cunningham et al., 2024). 

## **5. Conclusions** 

In this study we took advantage on the rare opportunity to examine the combined impact of a past severe wildfire and a new prescribed fire (via pre- and postfire sampling and in-fire temperature monitoring) on soil organic matter characteristics in a fire-prone eucalypt forest typical of southeast Australia. While effective for fuel reduction, the PF caused an 87% loss of surface soil carbon (litter and Oa horizon) and transformed residual SOM into more recalcitrant forms, as evidenced by a noticeable increase in aromatic compounds and higher thermal stability. While this stability may enhance long-term soil C storage, it can also have a limiting effect on nutrient availability and can thereby impact soil health. In contrast, the mineral surface soil (0–2 cm of the Ah horizon) retained its pre-fire SOM characteristics likely due to limited heating ( _<_ 60<sup>◦</sup> C), suggesting that the prior high-severity wildfire or cumulative past fires overall had already established a pool of recalcitrant SOM that the PF alone did not significantly alter. 

While our findings raise ecological concerns for nutrient cycling in oligotrophic ecosystems, such as those examined in this study, it is important to recognize that the lack of ecosystem-scale replication represents a limitation that constrains the ecological interpretation and extrapolation of the findings to other dry eucalypt forests. 

The cumulative effects of both PF and wildfire found here highlight the need of careful consideration of the timing, intensity, and frequency of PF to mitigate unintended consequences while achieving wildfire prevention objectives. Critically, our results provide valuable insights for fire management strategies that account for legacy wildfire impacts that also address changes in soils. Examining changes in SOM quality, including PyC content and thermal stability, can help to define ecological thresholds for sustainable fire frequency. As climate change intensifies wildfire regimes, such insights will provide a framework for balancing immediate risk reduction with the preservation of soil ecological functions.
