# CAL FIRE and Intersectional Inequality: Gender, LGBTQIA2S+ and the Governance of Incarcerated Labor

## Abstract

This article examines California’s CAL FIRE conservation camps as a contemporary system of coerced labor enabled by the 13th Amendment’s punishment clause. While the state publicly frames incarcerated firefighting as voluntary rehabilitation, document analysis reveals that participation is structured through coercive conditions, disciplinary threats, and economic incentives that mirror historical systems of racialized forced labor such as Black Codes and convict leasing. Drawing on state documents, CDCR policies, CAL FIRE training materials, legislative hearings, audits, and media reports, this study demonstrates how gender and sexuality shape access to labor assignments within the camps. Women and LGBTQIA2S+ incarcerated people are systematically excluded from masculinized, higher‑status firefighting roles that offer greater pay and double sentence‑reduction credits, and are instead funneled into feminized, low‑wage, surveillance‑heavy work. Using frameworks from racial capitalism, feminist criminology, queer criminology, gendered organizations theory, and intersectionality, the analysis shows how CAL FIRE reproduces patriarchal and heteronormative labor hierarchies inside a carceral setting. Findings reveal that the state’s economic dependence on incarcerated firefighters—saving an estimated $100 million annually—reinforces a labor regime in which coercion, discipline, and inequality are embedded in institutional design. This article argues that CAL FIRE conservation camps represent a modern iteration of coerced labor that extends the historical lineage of racialized and gendered exploitation in the post‑Emancipation United States, and highlights the need to confront the constitutional and economic structures that sustain this system.
