# Heather management modifies climate sensitivity of surface peat moisture in UK upland peatlands

## Abstract

Abstract. UK upland peatlands are important carbon stores, often dominated by heather, and are increasingly threatened by climate change, which promotes peat drying and wildfire risk. While water table depth (WTD) is often monitored to assess peatland hydrology, many key ecohydrological processes depend on surface peat moisture (SPM), which directly regulates surface carbon cycling, vegetation dynamics and peat fire risk, yet SPM remains poorly understood. We analysed seasonal SPM measurements under unmanaged, mown and burnt heather over 12 consecutive years post management from three replicated deep peat sites, together with measurements over five weeks of wet to dry conditions at a shallow peat site spanning three years post management. Across the deep peat sites, climate primarily controlled SPM, while heather management buffered moisture losses, particularly during warm and dry conditions. This buffering effect was strongest soon after management and weakened during vegetation recovery as heather height increased. After excluding low-rainfall events, SPM was strongly related to WTD, and the relationship remained relatively stable when the water table was within ~35 cm of the peat surface. At the shallow peat site, management substantially increased SPM during the first year after treatment relative to old heather, while mowing maintained higher SPM three years post-management, particularly under very dry conditions. Overall, heather management increased SPM across both peatland types, creating wetter near-surface conditions and reducing the sensitivity of surface-moisture to climatic drying. While mowing generally produced more consistent increases in SPM than burning, retained brash may increase wildfire risk under future climate change. The relative benefits of management types are likely to depend on site-specific conditions and time since management.

# **1. Introduction** 

- 37 Peatlands cover only around 3% of the global land surface but store approximately 30% of 

- 38 global soil carbon (Yu et al., 2010). These carbon stocks have accumulated over millennia 

- 39 under waterlogged, often cold and acidic conditions that suppress organic matter 

- 40 decomposition (Heinemeyer et al., 2010). However, climate change and human disturbance 

- 41 increasingly threaten peatland hydrological functioning, potentially increasing peat drying, 

- 42 carbon loss and wildfire risk (de Waard et al., 2024; Harenda et al., 2018; Loisel et al., 2021). 

- 43 Protecting and restoring peatlands is therefore of global importance for conserving existing 

- 44 carbon stocks (Andersen et al., 2017; Humpenöder et al., 2020), and supporting climate- 

- 45 change mitigation (Leifeld & Menichetti, 2018). 

- 46 Blanket bogs are the dominant peatland type in the UK, covering more than half of the 

- 47 uplands (IUCN, 2018) and representing around 13% of the global blanket bog resource 

- 48 (IUCN, 2011). Many are dominated by _Calluna vulgaris_ (heather) and have historically often 

- 49 been managed by rotational prescribed burning for livestock grazing and grouse moor 

- 50 management (Davies et al., 2016). However, approximately 80% of peatlands are claimed to 

- 51 be degraded (IUCN, 2025), and less than 20% of UK blanket bogs are considered either 

- 52 undamaged (IUCN, 2011) or in “good” condition (IUCN, 2024). While the extent and causes 

- 53 of blanket bog degradation remain contested (Davies et al., 2016; IUCN, 2024, 2025; Tallis, 

- 54 1998), drainage, vegetation change and severe fire can impair peatland long-term functioning 

- 55 (Harper et al., 2018; Heinemeyer et al., 2023a, 2025). Management should therefore be 

- 56 evaluated using direct measurements of ecosystem processes and functions rather than binary 

- 57 pass/fail criteria based on vegetation or management condition alone (Davies et al., 2016; 

- 58 Heinemeyer et al., 2019a, 2025). An important unresolved question is whether heather 

- 59 management alters the capacity of peatlands to retain near-surface water under changing 

- 60 climatic conditions, thereby influencing the resilience of key ecohydrological functions.

- 61 Hydrological assessments of heather management have primarily focused on water table 

- 62 depth (WTD) (Holden et al., 2015; Worrall et al., 2013), infiltration (Holden, 2009; Jordán et 

- 63 al., 2010; Mallik & Rahman, 1985), hydraulic conductivity (Holden et al., 2014), water 

- 64 storage and flow (Brown et al., 2015; Robinson, 1985), and water quality (Worrall et al., 

- 65 2007). While WTD is widely used as an indicator of peatland hydrological status, it does not 

- 66 necessarily represent moisture conditions within the biologically active surface peat. Surface 

- 67 peat moisture (SPM) responds directly to rainfall, plant and atmospheric water demand, and 

- 68 regulates vegetation recovery (Mallik et al., 1988), microbial activity (Peltoniemi et al., 

- 69 2015), carbon cycling (Charman et al., 2013; Räsänen et al., 2021), smouldering fire 

- 70 behaviour (Huang & Rein, 2017) and peatland food webs (Carroll et al., 2015). Satellite- 

- 71 derived SPM has recently been used to assess drought resilience (Lees et al., 2021) and 

- 72 methane emissions (Räsänen et al., 2021), but direct field measurements remain scarce. Only 

- 73 one unpublished UK blanket bog study has directly compared SPM between burnt and 

- 74 unburnt plots over five months following prescribed burning (Meyles, 2002); SPM changed 

- 75 little during burning but was higher in burnt plots under subsequent dry summer conditions, 

- 76 suggesting that management effects may become most apparent during climatic 

- 77 drying. However, that study was short-term, considered only burning and did not assess 

- 78 whether management effects changed as vegetation recovered. Most peatland hydrological 

- 79 studies have also focused on deep peat, leaving responses in shallow peat (i.e., <40 cm) 

- 80 poorly understood. 

- 81 The relationship between WTD and SPM is crucial for predicting carbon emissions (Räsänen 

- 82 et al., 2021), food web interactions (Carroll et al., 2015), and peat-fire risk (Kartiwa et al., 

- 83 2023). In raised bogs and lowland peatlands, deeper water tables are generally associated 

- 84 with lower SPM (Liu et al., 2019; Price, 1997), although this relationship may weaken when 

- 85 WTD falls ~30-40 cm below the peat surface (Price & Whitehead, 2001; Strack & Price, 

4 

https://doi.org/10.5194/egusphere-2026-4967 Preprint. Discussion started: 5 October 2026 _⃝_ c Author(s) 2026. CC BY 4.0 License. 





- 86 2009). Antecedent rainfall may further modify this relationship as small rainfall events can 

- 87 rewet surface peat without producing a response in deeper water table. Such decoupling may 

- 88 be especially important in spatially heterogeneous upland peatlands, which typically include 

- 89 steeper slopes (Bonn et al., 2016) and often contain shallow peat. Determining whether 

- 90 heather management alters the sensitivity of this relationship to contrasting climatic 

- 91 conditions is therefore essential for understanding peatland ecohydrological resilience and 

- 92 wildfire risk under future climate change. 

- 93 Prescribed burning and mowing may modify the climatic sensitivity of SPM by altering 

- 94 vegetation structure, evapotranspiration and near-surface water retention. Removing mature 

- 95 heather may initially reduce transpiration and root water uptake, thereby limiting moisture 

- 96 loss during warm and dry conditions (Heinemeyer et al., 2023b; Worrall et al., 2013). These 

- 97 effects are likely to weaken as heather regenerates and vegetation-mediated water loss 

- 98 increases. Burning and mowing may also differ in their effects: mowing can leave a brash 

- 99 layer that likely reduces direct evaporation and surface runoff, whereas brash removal 

- 100 exposes the peat surface, and burning may also alter surface properties and infiltration 

- 101 (Heinemeyer et al., 2023b; Holden et al., 2014; Mallik & Rahman, 1985). While mowing has 

- 102 been promoted as an alternative to burning (Heinemeyer et al., 2019a), its ecohydrological 

- 103 effects and long-term trajectories are even less well understood (Harper et al., 2018; 

- 104 Heinemeyer et al., 2019a, 2019b, 2025). Management effects should therefore be examined 

- 105 in relation to peatland type, climatic conditions and time since treatment. 

- 106 To address these knowledge gaps, we measured SPM (i.e., top 8 cm) under prescribed 

- 107 burning, mowing with brash left or removed, and unmanaged heather. We combined seasonal 

- 108 measurements collected over 12 consecutive years post management at three replicated deep 

- 109 peat blanket bog sites with five-week measurements spanning wet to very dry summer 

- 110 conditions over three years post management at a shallow peat site to answer four questions:

- 111 (1) How do site characteristics like climate, vegetation, peat depth, and topography influence 

- 112 SPM across deep-peat sites; (2) How do heather management strategies affect SPM and do 

- 113 their effects vary with climatic conditions and time since management; (3) how does WTD 

- 114 relate to SPM under different heather managements in deep peat; (4) how does heather 

- 115 management affect SPM in shallow peat under variable moisture conditions?

- 409 **4. Discussion** 

- 410 **4.1 Environmental and site-specific controls on surface peat moisture variability** 

- 411 The results suggest that climatic factors have the strongest overall effects on SPM 

- 412 across deep peat sites, followed by heather management, with heather height and peat 413 depth having ecologically meaningful but not overall statistically significant 

- 414 influences (Table 1a). However, after excluding management from the BEINF model, 

- 415 heather height effects became highly significant (Table 1b). This could be explained 

- 416 by the higher evaporation rates from unmanaged tall heather biomass (Heinemeyer et 

- 417 al., 2023b; Worrall et al., 2013). Importantly, these findings indicate that climate 

- 418 determines the overall moisture regime, whereas heather management modifies how

|---|---|
|420|control of SPM, management alters vegetation structure (Whitehead et al., 2026) and|
|421|associated evapotranspiration (Worrall et al., 2013), thereby changing the sensitivity|
|422|of surface peat moisture to rainfall deficits and atmospheric drying (Figure 4).|
|423|Application of different timescales of climatic factors also had considerable overall|
|424|impacts (Table 1). This is not surprising as substantial climatic differences, especially|
|425|in rainfall, existed across the three deep peat sites (Figures S1-S3). The comparatively|
|426|weaker effects of heather height and peat depth likely reflect their localized and|
|427|gradual influence on SPM, which is masked by the more pronounced variability|
|428|associated with climate. As measurements were usually conducted during relatively|
|429|stable and sunny periods, the preceding three days were also likely to have had dry|
|430|weather in many cases. However, over longer timescales (e.g., more than five days|
|431|before measurements), the likelihood of encountering periods with rainfall increased.|
|432|The likely effects of rainfall, therefore, became stronger with period length, while the|
|433|contributions of other factors were fairly stable. Nevertheless, vegetation height and|
|434|peat depth (including litter) showed moderate negative and positive correlations with|

|441|facilitate upward water movement via capillary action (Dixon et al., 2017), helping to|

|516|align with those of Mallik et al. (1984) and Meyles (2002), who reported higher SPM|
|517|under burnt than unburnt plots following short-term heather burning experiments.|
|518|These patterns can largely be explained again by reduced evapotranspiration and root|
|519|water uptake after the removal of aboveground vegetation (Heinemeyer et al., 2023b;|
|520|Liu, 2023; Worrall et al., 2013). This mechanism likely also underlies the highly|

|522|was excluded (Table 1b). In summer, these effects peak due to limited rainfall and|
|523|high temperatures, thereby maximizing SPM differences between managed and DN|
|524|plots. In winter, however, abundant rainfall and lower evapotranspiration rates|
|525|saturate the peat and mask the effects of vegetation, resulting in minimal SPM|

|528|period 1 under similar climatic conditions (Figure 1b). By contrast, period 2 was|

|532|overall lower during period 1 (Figure 1a). This may reflect period-specific brash|

|534|reaching the ground (Figure 4). While brash can also reduce evaporation by|
|535|protecting the peat surface, the reduced water recharge seems to have dominated,|
|536|resulting in drier SPM in LB. As compaction and decomposition progressed, the|
|537|balance shifted, and after approximately three years post-burn, recharge and|
|538|protective effects likely began to outweigh interception losses. Consequently, SPM in|
|539|LB exceeded that in BR and FI during periods 2 and 3. Additionally, the slightly|

|543|2016; DeBano, 2000). However, considering climate change and more frequent|
|544|extreme drought events in spring and summer, brash layers resulting from mowing|
|545|may pose an additional wildfire risk from fuel loads (Heinemeyer & Ashby, 2023a).|
|546|At the shallow peat site, significantly higher SPM in managed than in DN plots was|
|547|found only within one year following treatment under very dry conditions (Figure 3a).|
|548|It is unsurprising as shallow peat generally has deeper WTD (in our site mostly within|
|549|the bedrock) and drier SPM than deep peat (Wilkinson et al., 2020). Under very dry|
|550|conditions, vegetation in DN plots cannot access sufficient water from underground,|
|551|leading to a rapid decrease in SPM due to evapotranspiration. However, under wetter|
|552|conditions, SPM differences between managed and DN plots can be reduced by|
|553|increased recharge and lower evapotranspiration. In any case, these differences from|
|554|unmanaged areas are expected to diminish with vegetation regrowth for the reasons|
|555|outlined above, though the process tends to be faster and more pronounced in the|
|556|comparatively drier shallow peat system, where conditions favour the rapid regrowth|
|557|of drought-adapted plants such as heather. Notably, SPM in LB was higher than in|
|558|both FI and DN three years after management under very dry conditions but lower|
|559|under wetter conditions; however, within one-year, median SPM in FI was higher|
|560|than in LB (Figure 3a). This may indicate that mowing with brash left can maintain|
|561|more water than burning or mowing with brash removed in particularly dry conditions|

|563|deep peat sites (Figure 1a), further highlighting the period-specific brash effects.|
|564|**4.4 Management implications, limitations and future research**|



29 

https://doi.org/10.5194/egusphere-2026-4967 Preprint. Discussion started: 5 October 2026 _⃝_ c Author(s) 2026. CC BY 4.0 License. 





|565|This study shows that heather management reduced the susceptibility of surface peat|
|---|---|
|566|to climatic drying by maintaining wetter near-surface conditions, particularly during|
|567|warm and dry periods when ecohydrological functioning is most vulnerable. Such|
|568|changes are therefore likely to create favourable conditions for the regeneration of|
|569|hydrophilic vegetation, such as_Sphagnum_mosses, grasses and shrubs, especially in|
|570|the early stages of recovery as around 50% of fine roots in similar blanket bog were|
|571|found in the top 5 cm of the measured 20 cm peat profile, and more than 95% of soil|
|572|respiration took place in the upper 10 cm of peat (Heinemeyer et al., 2011). The driest|
|573|unmanaged plots in turn highlight the benefits of removing old, dense and tall heather|
|574|toward supporting rewetting and reducing carbon loss from increased decomposition|
|575|in drier peat and mitigating the potential rising wildfire risks associated with climate|
|576|change (Heinemeyer et al., 2025; Liu, 2023), especially considering dead standing|
|577|heather biomass (Lewis et al., 2024). Additionally, the lower vegetation height and|
|578|increased SPM likely benefits many moorland birds (e.g., golden plover) by|
|579|supporting breeding grounds and higher cranefly populations, a key food source,|
|580|which were observed to prefer a moisture range of 85% to 95% (Carroll et al., 2011;|
|581|Heinemeyer et al., 2019a). Regarding the optimal management strategy, current|
|582|evidence remains insufficient to draw firm conclusions, but potential options are|
|583|likely site-dependent: prescribed burning may be preferred in wetter sites and where|
|584|mowing is not accessible, whereas mowing with brash left is likely better in drier|
|585|sites.|
|586|Overall, our findings indicate that heather management modifies the climate|
|587|sensitivity of SPM by buffering moisture losses during dry conditions rather than|
|588|uniformly increasing peat wetness throughout the year. This buffering effect was|
|589|strongest shortly after management and gradually weakened as vegetation regrew,|



30 

https://doi.org/10.5194/egusphere-2026-4967 Preprint. Discussion started: 5 October 2026 _⃝_ c Author(s) 2026. CC BY 4.0 License. 





|590|demonstrating that climate sensitivity of SPM changes dynamically during ecosystem|
|---|---|
|591|recovery. While these findings were revealed by the long-term experimental design,|
|592|several limitations should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of|
|593|management effects. The wider experimental design and pre-management WTD|
|594|records helped identify baseline hydrological differences, but the absence of pre-|
|595|management SPM measurements limits attribution of all post-treatment SPM|
|596|differences exclusively to management. These limitations are also important when|
|597|comparing our findings with previous studies. For example, Holden et al. (2015)|
|598|reported the lowest WTD following prescribed burning, but also a gradual increase in|
|599|WTD (and thus wetness), with the highest WTD occurring in plots more than 10|
|600|years post burning. However,these results were derived from a space-for-time|
|601|approach, which is inherently subject to uncertainty arising from pre-existing|
|602|environmental differences among sites (Ashby & Heinemeyer, 2020). The contrasting|
|603|conclusions between studies therefore likely reflect methodological differences, pre-|
|604|existing environmental variation among sites, differing climatic and ecohydrological|
|605|conditions, the use of environmentally contrasting control sites, and genuine|
|606|differences between short- and long-term responses to heather management (Ashby &|
|607|Heinemeyer, 2020; Heinemeyer et al., 2025). These findings also raise several|
|608|important questions. What constitutes an appropriate reference condition for|
|609|unmanaged peatlands? Is the recovery trajectory site-dependent? Do both|
|610|managements support sustainable practice and recovery of ecohydrological functions?|
|611|Collectively, these findings suggest that vegetation management should be viewed not|
|612|simply as a means of altering average peat wetness but as a mechanism for enhancing|
|613|the resilience of near-surface peat moisture to climatic drying under future climate|
|614|change. Further research is needed to address these questions and improve the|



31 

https://doi.org/10.5194/egusphere-2026-4967 Preprint. Discussion started: 5 October 2026 _⃝_ c Author(s) 2026. CC BY 4.0 License. 





|615|mechanistic understanding of SPM dynamics across peatland types and management|
|---|---|
|616|strategies. In particular, measurements of peat hydraulic properties, including|
|617|saturated and unsaturated hydraulic conductivity, infiltration capacity, soil water|
|618|retention characteristics and near-surface runoff (Holden et al., 2014, 2015), would|
|619|help disentangle the relative contributions of evapotranspiration and water transport|
|620|processes to the observed management effects on SPM. These measurements would|
|621|improve understanding of how prescribed burning and mowing influence peat|
|622|hydrology and provide better parameterisation of ecohydrological models. Future|
|623|studies should also quantify the rewetting potential and topographic constraints (e.g.,|
|624|slope and aspect) governing SPM across contrasting peatland types, including "leaky|
|625|slope" and "soup bowl" systems (Glatzel et al., 2024). In addition, measurements of|
|626|peat moisture throughout the peat profile, together with long-term monitoring ideally|
|627|spanning at least one complete management rotation (approximately 5–25 years|
|628|depending on peatland type), and adequate replication at both plot and site levels, are|
|629|required to improve model predictions and support evidence-based, sustainable|
|630|heather management under future climate change.|
