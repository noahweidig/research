# Compound and cascading climate risks in urban infrastructure governance and resilience across North America

## Abstract

North American cities are increasingly exposed to climate-related hazards that do not occur in isolation. Extreme heat, drought, flooding, hurricanes, wildfires, wildfire smoke, and sea-level rise interact with aging infrastructure, uneven urbanization, social vulnerability, and fragmented governance systems. As a result, an initial climate shock can trigger cascading failures across energy, water, transport, telecommunications, housing, health, and emergency response systems. This article develops a conceptual and comparative framework for understanding compound and cascading climate risks in urban systems, with a focus on cities in Canada, the United States, and Mexico. It argues that urban climate risk cannot be understood through single-hazard or sectoral approaches alone. Instead, cities must be analyzed as socio-technical systems in which infrastructure interdependence, governance arrangements, fiscal capacity, and social inequality shape the scale and distribution of climate impacts. Drawing on selected urban cases, including Los Angeles and New Orleans in the United States, Toronto and Vancouver in Canada, and Mexico City, Monterrey, Acapulco, and Veracruz–Boca del Río in Mexico, the article identifies common patterns and differentiated challenges in North American urban resilience. The reviewed evidence identifies a mismatch between the complexity of cascading risks and the fragmented governance and funding mechanisms available to manage them, although its form and severity vary across national and urban contexts. The article concludes that strengthening urban resilience in North America requires moving from isolated adaptation measures toward integrated risk governance, stable resilience financing, intersectoral metropolitan coordination, and equity-centered infrastructure planning. Stronger disaster-response networks, interagency cooperation, and urban-resilience institutions can support more coordinated preparedness and recovery from climate-related disasters with the potential for severe human, infrastructural, and economic impacts.

#### **1 Introduction** 

Cities have become central arenas of climate risk. Across North America, urban areas are exposed to extreme heat, prolonged drought, flooding, hurricanes, wildfires, wildfire smoke, coastal storms, and sea-level rise [1]. The most serious urban climate impacts rarely result from a single hazard acting alone; instead, climate-related events interact with infrastructure systems, land-use patterns, social inequalities, and governance arrangements in ways that can amplify disruption far beyond the initial shock. A hurricane may begin as a meteorological event but quickly become a crisis of electricity, water, telecommunications, housing, mobility, tourism, and public health. A drought may appear first as a hydrological problem but evolve into a broader urban crisis involving water rationing, energy demand, social conflict, public-health risks, and institutional legitimacy. A heatwave may strain electricity grids, increase mortality, worsen air quality, reduce labor productivity, and expose inequalities in access to cooling [2]. 

This article argues that such dynamics require a shift in how urban climate risk is understood and governed. Traditional approaches to urban risk management have often treated hazards such as floods, heatwaves, storms, droughts, and wildfires as separate categories of analysis. While this approach remains useful for specific planning purposes, it is increasingly insufficient for understanding how climate-related crises unfold in complex urban systems. Cities are not passive recipients of climate hazards [3]. They are dense socio-technical systems in which physical infrastructure, economic activity, ecological conditions, social vulnerability, and institutional decision-making are deeply interconnected. As a result, a disruption in one system can propagate across others, generating cascading failures that transform a localized event into a systemic urban crisis [4]. 

The concept of compound and cascading climate risk is becoming a key consideration for urban studies and policy [5]. Compound risks emerge when multiple climatic drivers, hazards, or vulnerabilities interact to produce impacts greater than those expected from each factor alone. Cascading risks occur when an initial disturbance triggers secondary failures across interconnected systems. In the urban context, these processes are especially significant because energy, water, drainage, transport, telecommunications, food supply, health services, and emergency response systems are highly interdependent [6]. This paper identifies this as a central problem: urban systems depend on interconnected physical infrastructure and social-governance arrangements, while disruptions in energy, water, or transport can generate effects across the wider urban infrastructure nexus. 

North American cities provide a particularly relevant context for examining these dynamics. Canada, the United States, and Mexico share continental-scale climate risks, highly urbanized populations, and deeply interconnected economies. At the same time, they differ significantly in infrastructure quality, social inequality, federal arrangements, municipal capacity, adaptation policy, and resilience financing. These differences make North America a useful comparative space for analyzing how compound and cascading risks are shaped not only by climate hazards but also by governance systems and patterns of urban development. 

The United States illustrates the tension between strong subnational innovation and unstable federal climate policy [8, 36]. Canada offers an example of a country with a relatively advanced national adaptation framework but important implementation gaps at

the municipal and infrastructure levels. Mexico reveals how cascading climate risks can be intensified by uneven urbanization, water insecurity, infrastructure deficits, informality, and fragmented metropolitan governance. Taken together, these cases show that urban resilience is not simply a technical challenge of protecting infrastructure assets; it is also a governance, finance, and equity challenge. 

Community-centered approaches and the co-design of problem-solving processes can strengthen urban climate-risk management and adaptation programs [66]. Interagency coordination, transdisciplinary research, and interdisciplinary policy analysis are also needed to develop interventions suited to local risks, barriers, and capacities. Infrastructure stress testing and scenario planning can help cities evaluate compound-risk pathways and identify adaptation options, particularly where global mitigation remains insufficient to avoid further climate impacts [40, 49, 50]. 

This review makes three related contributions. First, it develops a comparative North American perspective that examines urban cases in Canada, the United States, and Mexico within a common analytical framework, while retaining the institutional and socio-spatial differences among them. Second, it integrates compound hazards, infrastructure interdependence, social vulnerability, governance capacity, and resilience finance through a structured evidence matrix. Third, it uses that matrix to identify both recurring cascading-risk mechanisms and differences in their operational governance across the three countries. The article does not rank cities; its originality lies in providing a transferable qualitative framework for examining how climate hazards become systemic urban crises under different governance and capacity conditions. 

The article proceeds as follows. Section  2 explains the methodological approach and comparative review design. Section 3 develops the conceptual framework for understanding compound climate risks, cascading failures, and urban socio-technical systems. Section 4 reports the findings from the selected urban contexts in the United States, Canada, and Mexico. Section  5 compares the cross-case patterns identified through the evidence matrix and discusses their implications for governance, finance, infrastructure interdependence, and equity. Section  6 concludes by identifying the contribution, limitations, and implications of the review. 

#### **2 Methodological approaches** 

This article uses a targeted comparative scoping-review design combined with qualitative policy-document analysis. The approach was selected because the objective is to examine how compound and cascading climate risks are conceptualized and operationalized across different North American urban-governance settings, rather than to estimate effect sizes, produce a comprehensive systematic inventory, or rank cities. The review is guided by the question: how do compound and cascading climate risks expose the limits of current urban resilience governance in Canada, the United States, and Mexico? 

Targeted searches covered academic and grey literature published primarily between January 2020 and June 2026. Earlier foundational studies were retained when necessary to define core concepts, document major focusing events, or establish the development of urban resilience and infrastructure-interdependence research. Academic sources were identified through searches in Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, supplemented by backward reference checking. Policy and institutional documents were

identified through official federal, state or provincial, and municipal websites and the repositories of international and specialist organizations. Searches were conducted in English and Spanish using combinations of the terms “compound climate risk,” “cascading climate risk,” “systemic risk,” “multi-hazard risk,” “urban climate risk,” “critical infrastructure,” “infrastructure interdependence,” “urban resilience,” “climate adaptation,” “governance,” and “resilience finance,” together with the country or city name. Spanishlanguage equivalents included _“riesgos climáticos compuestos_ , _” “riesgos en cascada_ , _” “riesgo sistémico_ , _” “infraestructura crítica_ , _” “resiliencia urbana_ , _” and “adaptación climática.”_ 

Sources were included when they met at least one substantive criterion: explicit analysis of compound, cascading, systemic, or multi-hazard climate risk; examination of interactions between climate hazards and urban infrastructure or essential services; analysis of social vulnerability or unequal exposure in an urban climate-risk context; or assessment of governance, planning, financing, preparedness, or adaptation responses relevant to urban resilience. Sources were excluded when they addressed a single hazard without an urban-system, infrastructure, governance, or vulnerability dimension, or when they lacked relevance to Canada, the United States, or Mexico. The final evidentiary corpus comprised 81 cited sources: 54 academic publications and 27 policy, governmental, or institutional documents. All sources in the evidentiary corpus are listed in the numbered reference section; methodological references are reported separately. The targeted scoping-review procedure was informed by Mak and Thomas (2022a, 2022b). 

Document selection proceeded in two stages. Titles, abstracts, executive summaries, and document descriptions were first screened for thematic and geographical relevance. The retained full texts were then assessed against the inclusion criteria and their capacity to inform at least one dimension of the analytical matrix. Because the study is a targeted scoping review, selection sought analytical diversity and cross-case comparability rather than statistical representativeness. The urban cases comprise New Orleans and Los Angeles in the United States; Toronto, Vancouver, Halifax, Calgary, and Edmonton in Canada; and Mexico City, Monterrey, Acapulco, and Veracruz–Boca del Río in Mexico. Cases were selected purposively to capture contrasting combinations of coastal storms and recovery, heat and wildfire smoke, drought and water insecurity, flooding, sea-level rise, infrastructure stress, metropolitan fragmentation, social inequality, and institutional and fiscal capacity. 

Each source was coded qualitatively using the evidence matrix presented in Table 1. The matrix contains eight dimensions: hazard profile; compound interaction; urban system affected; cascading pathway; social amplification; governance challenge; funding challenge; and policy response. Coding recorded whether a source treated systemic risk conceptually, diagnosed a specific risk pathway, translated it into a planning instrument, or documented implementation, monitoring, or funded action. No numerical scores were assigned. The synthesis followed three stages: within-case coding of hazards, dependencies, populations, and responses; country-level aggregation of recurring patterns; and cross-case comparison to identify common mechanisms, contextual differences, and gaps between risk recognition and implementation. Section 4 reports the coded findings by country and urban context, whereas Sect.  5 interprets the cross-case patterns.

The design has limitations. The targeted strategy does not claim exhaustive coverage of every North American city or policy instrument, and the evidence base is uneven across countries and cities. The searches were iterative rather than preregistered, and a sufficiently reliable deduplicated count of all records initially retrieved across databases, backward searches, and institutional websites was not preserved; a PRISMA flow diagram is therefore not reported. Academic studies, government strategies, and institutional reports also differ in purpose and evidentiary detail, limiting direct equivalence. The findings should therefore be interpreted as an analytically structured synthesis of the reviewed literature, not as a statistical assessment of urban performance or causal effectiveness. These limits are addressed by reporting claims cautiously, grounding comparisons in the common matrix, and distinguishing documented implementation from policy intention. 

#### **3 Conceptualizing compound and cascading climate risks in North American cities** 

Climate change is altering not only the frequency and intensity of urban hazards but also the ways in which those hazards interact across space, time, and infrastructure systems [10, 13, 21, 40]. Such interactions have the potential to produce severe human, infrastructural, and economic impacts. Compound climate risk is therefore important for understanding how urban communities experience climate-related disruption. Many severe urban crises arise from interactions among climatic drivers, exposure patterns, and system vulnerabilities that produce impacts greater than those expected from each factor alone [10, 13]. This perspective is particularly relevant in cities, where dense concentrations of people, infrastructure, and economic activity allow disturbances to propagate rapidly across sectors and territories. Recent studies project increases in the frequency and duration of compound dry-hot extremes across major U.S. cities under the combined effects of anthropogenic climate change and urban development [4]. Research on compound inland flooding likewise shows that multiple hydrometeorological processes

can converge and challenge risk assessments organized around single hazards [11, 15] These findings support analyzing cities as spaces where interacting climatic stressors are filtered through infrastructure interdependence, urbanization, and socially uneven exposure [12, 17]. 

A holistic perspective is therefore important because changing and variable climate conditions require resilience policies, planning, and management strategies that can adjust to evolving threats rather than rely solely on static predictions [12, 13]. Urban systems are complex socio-ecological-technical systems in which social, ecological, and technological components are intertwined, requiring systems-based approaches to risk assessment and resilience planning [14, 17]. 

In the urban context, integrated risk assessment that combines climatological, engineering, urban-planning, and social-science perspectives can improve understanding of compound hazards and possible responses [22, 39, 49, 50]. Transdisciplinary research, interdisciplinary policy analysis, and stronger interagency cooperation are needed to address effects that move across sectors and to reduce siloed management. Conflicts over mandates and resources can slow interministerial coordination. A comprehensive approach can therefore support adaptive governance structures suited to the multidimensional and uncertain character of urban climate risk in North America [25, 32]. 

This integrated perspective also acknowledges that strategies intended to enhance resilience to one event may inadvertently reduce resilience to another concurrent hazard [16]. Prospective analysis can help identify and minimize unintended consequences across different implementation scenarios. For example, anticipating sea-level rise can inform flood-protection and land-use measures for people, housing, urban infrastructure, power plants, and transport systems in coastal communities [28, 65]. A deeper understanding of compound and cascading risks can help decision-makers design policies that anticipate crisis pathways, maintain core urban functions, and provide timely support to affected communities. When combined with lessons from prior events and resilience standards for reconstruction, such institutional improvements can support more coordinated recovery [17–19]. 

**3.1 Theoretical elements for compound and cascading climate-risk preparedness in cities** This section introduces the principal elements of the theoretical framework for analyzing compound climate risks, cascading disruptions, and urban-system failures. Simultaneous or sequential risks can interact and reinforce underlying vulnerabilities. Within complex systems, these relationships may obscure feedback loops and disruption pathways, making cascading failures difficult to anticipate. The challenge is particularly significant in urban settings, where critical-infrastructure interdependencies can transmit disruption across systems during compound events [7, 10, 17, 50]. 

As shown in Fig. 1, compound climate risk refers to interactions among two or more climatic drivers, hazards, exposures, or vulnerabilities that amplify overall impacts. These interactions may be simultaneous, such as heat and drought occurring together, or sequential, such as prolonged drought followed by intense rainfall. Cascading risk describes the propagation of impacts when an initial climatic shock generates secondary disruption across environmental, infrastructural, and social systems [6, 10, 13]. Multihazard risk refers to the presence or assessment of multiple hazards without necessarily establishing interaction; systemic risk refers to consequences affecting the wider urban

In cities, extreme heat can coincide with peak electricity demand, strain transmission networks, reduce access to cooling, worsen air quality, and increase morbidity and mortality. Research on dry-hot extremes in U.S. cities shows that urban development can intensify climatic stress [4]. Multi-hazard evidence also demonstrates that overlapping hazards and social vulnerability can be spatially concentrated [20, 34]. Cities therefore do not simply receive hazards; their built environments, infrastructure networks, and governance arrangements can transform and amplify them. 

Urban areas can also be understood as socio-technical systems composed of interconnected infrastructures, institutions, and social relations. Electricity, water supply, transport, telecommunications, public health, drainage, and food-distribution systems are deeply interdependent. A disruption in one system can therefore affect others and produce cascading failures beyond the initial hazard [17, 50]. Legacy development patterns and evolving climatic conditions make integrated and flexible local governance particularly important. The Mexico City megalopolis illustrates this interaction. Vargas and Magaña (2020) show that rapid and insufficiently planned urbanization has intensified the urban heat-island effect, altered precipitation patterns, and increased risks associated with warm spells and urban flooding [23, 26]. 

An important implication of the socio-technical perspective is that cities are vulnerable to cascading failures across interconnected urban systems. An initial shock in one domain can generate knock-on effects in others and produce broader disruption than the triggering event alone would suggest. The reviewed evidence indicates incomplete alignment between existing risk-management arrangements and the complexity of compound and cascading risks [7, 10, 17, 50]. Institutional and governance structures may therefore need greater flexibility and coordination for preparedness and crisis response [27, 29] For example, extreme heat can increase electricity demand and contribute to grid stress or outages. Power disruption can then affect cooling, water pumping, refrigeration, traffic signals, communications, and health services. Flooding can similarly disrupt mobility, isolate neighborhoods, damage substations or drainage infrastructure, and interrupt access to hospitals and emergency services [28]. 

Research on compound inland flooding in North America shows that interacting hydrometeorological processes can make flood impacts more complex under climate change [11]. Evidence on multi-hazard risk in socially vulnerable U.S. communities similarly demonstrates that overlapping hazards and vulnerability require spatially integrated analysis [34]. Together, these studies support the argument that systemic vulnerability is shaped not simply by exposure but also by relationships among infrastructures and populations [17, 30, 50]. Risk is amplified when infrastructure lacks redundancy, governance is fragmented, or vulnerable populations depend on services that can fail simultaneously. Cities with different hazards may therefore experience similar cascading crises when tightly coupled systems are exposed to climatic stress. Risk-reduction strategies nevertheless require close attention to the context in which they will be implemented [1, 16]. 

The integrated framing also helps to reposition urban-resilience debates. Rather than asking only how cities can adapt separately to floods, heatwaves, droughts, or smoke exposure, the relevant question is how they can govern interacting hazards that travel through interdependent infrastructures and disproportionately affect vulnerable communities. North American cities offer an important setting for examining this shift because they combine advanced infrastructure networks with marked social inequalities, varied climate profiles, and frequently fragmented governance systems. Under these conditions, governing compound and cascading climate risks is an important challenge for twenty-first-century urban resilience [9, 27, 51].

North American cities use diverse technological, managerial, policy, and social innovations to address urban climate risk. Incorporating compound and cascading risks into governmental decision-making requires better understanding of risk pathways and more integrated approaches suited to specific implementation contexts [31, 32]. Electoral cycles and changes in national priorities can alter the policy and funding environment. Canada and Mexico remain formally engaged in international climate action, while recent U.S. federal priorities have placed greater emphasis on energy security; nevertheless, many state, provincial, regional, and municipal jurisdictions continue to develop climate-risk and resilience initiatives [8, 33, 36, 40]. 

##### **4.1 The united states** 

The U.S. evidence is organized around two city cases: New Orleans, Louisiana, and Los Angeles, California. Both cities face multiple interacting hazards and depend on federal and state frameworks, but they display different ecological exposures, institutional arrangements, and fiscal capacities. New Orleans illustrates hurricane, flood, subsidence, power, and displacement cascades. Los Angeles illustrates interactions among heat, drought, wildfire and smoke, flooding, water dependence, and electricity stress. Social vulnerability is central in both cases: multi-hazard exposure is disproportionately concentrated among lower-income and otherwise vulnerable populations [34]. Donoghoe (2026) further observes that state and municipal resilience planning continues despite federal retrenchment, although reduced access to federal resources and decision-support tools can weaken local implementation [36]. 

##### **_4.1.1 New orleans_** 

New Orleans demonstrates how a coastal hazard can become a systemic urban crisis. Hurricane Katrina combined storm surge and flooding with levee failure, electricity and communications disruption, constrained emergency access, housing loss, prolonged displacement, and racialized recovery outcomes [37]. The relevant city architecture is distributed across the Hazard Mitigation Office, the Office of Sustainability, NOLA Ready, the Sewerage and Water Board, and emergency-management agencies. The city’s Hazard Mitigation Plan and Climate Action Plan recognize that flood protection, drainage, energy, housing, public health, and social vulnerability cannot be managed independently [74]. 

The policy relationship is multilevel. The municipal Hazard Mitigation Plan is designed to meet Louisiana and federal FEMA requirements, enabling access to federal mitigation funding; city climate and equity priorities are layered onto that hazard-management framework [74]. This relationship can support adaptation when federal and state resources finance drainage, mitigation, and recovery. It can constrain implementation when program continuity, cost-sharing requirements, or fragmented responsibility separate infrastructure investment from housing and social protection. New Orleans therefore has relatively mature risk recognition, while institutional coordination and equitable implementation remain continuing challenges [37, 74]. 

##### **_4.1.2 Los Angeles, California_** 

Los Angeles is used as the urban unit of analysis, with California retained as the higherlevel policy environment. The city faces extreme heat, drought, wildfire and smoke,

intense rainfall and flooding, and dependencies among electricity, water, transport, housing, and public health. The Los Angeles Climate Vulnerability Assessment identifies geographically differentiated exposure and adaptive capacity [73]. The Emergency Management Department maintains the Local Hazard Mitigation Plan, while other responsibilities are distributed across City Planning, LA Sanitation and Environment, the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, the Fire Department, and public-health authorities. 

California policy strengthens the city’s capacity through the California Climate Adaptation Strategy, the Integrated Climate Adaptation and Resiliency Program, state planning requirements, and financing mechanisms such as climate-resilience districts under Senate Bill 852 [35, 41, 42]. The Cal-Adapt program also provides data-driven tools for community, research, and policy applications. California’s experience with earthquake preparedness has contributed to an established emergency-management culture that can inform recovery and adaptation planning for climate events. The Los Angeles Local Hazard Mitigation Plan aligns with federal and state requirements and establishes eligibility for FEMA mitigation funds [73]. This nested architecture can connect local vulnerability analysis with state guidance and external finance. Its limitation is implementation fragmentation: responsibility for heat, wildfire, water, drainage, energy, and housing remains divided among agencies, while the availability of state and federal funds varies across programs and political cycles. 

##### **4.2 Canada: national adaptation architecture and the urban implementation gap** 

Canada combines extensive federal adaptation planning and relatively high technical capacity with urban exposure to extreme heat, riverine and pluvial flooding, wildfire smoke, coastal storm surge, and disruption of transport, energy, water, and health systems [38, 43–47]. These risks vary with geography, infrastructure age, social inequality, Indigenous rights and governance, and municipal fiscal capacity. The national framework is therefore important, but it does not produce uniform city-level outcomes. 

Canada’s National Adaptation Strategy establishes shared goals for disaster resilience, health, nature, infrastructure, and the economy [69, 72]. The Government of Canada Adaptation Action Plan and federal infrastructure programs provide implementation and financing channels, while provinces retain major responsibilities for municipalities, land-use systems, health, infrastructure standards, and emergency management [38, 43–45, 72]. The National Issues Report on cities and towns concludes that local governments are moving from planning toward implementation, but that action is not keeping pace with present and projected risks [45]. Municipal outcomes therefore depend on whether national priorities are translated through provincial authority, infrastructure funding, asset-management systems, and local budgets. 

Toronto’s Climate Change Risk Management Policy, resilience work, TransformTO, and recent climate-risk assessment connect heat, flooding, wildfire smoke, infrastructure, and vulnerable populations [43–46]. These instruments benefit from federal and Ontario frameworks, but responsibility remains distributed across Environment, Climate and Forestry, Toronto Water, Public Health, Transportation Services, the Toronto Transit Commission, and emergency management. Vancouver’s Climate Change Adaptation Strategy similarly integrates sea-level rise, heat, heavy rainfall, and coastal

exposure, yet major protective-infrastructure and land-use decisions require coordination with British Columbia, regional bodies, utilities, and federal funding [43–45, 75]. 

Halifax’s HalifACT links coastal flooding, storms, infrastructure protection, and decarbonization within a regional municipality, while Calgary’s Climate Strategy and Adaptation Action Plan address river flooding, hail, heat, drought, and severe storms. Edmonton’s Climate Resilient Edmonton strategy connects climate science with land use, infrastructure, emergency planning, and public health [43–45, 76–78]. These cities display comparatively strong planning capacity, but different ecological profiles and provincial arrangements produce different implementation needs. The recurring deficit is not the absence of plans; it is incomplete integration of municipal instruments with infrastructure ownership, provincial authority, long-term finance, Indigenous governance, and operational performance indicators. 

##### **4.3 Mexico: urban inequality, water insecurity, hurricanes, and fragmented metropolitan governance** 

Mexican cities combine tightly interconnected infrastructure with uneven urbanization, substantial social vulnerability, and fragmented metropolitan governance [48, 54, 56– 63]. Federal climate and civil-protection frameworks establish general responsibilities, but implementation depends heavily on state institutions, municipal planning, utilities, and emergency-management capacity [62, 63]. The selected cases consequently reveal larger differences in local operational capacity than those observed in the Canadian cases. 

In the Mexico City Metropolitan Area, drought and flooding are interconnected rather than opposing risks. The Cutzamala system transfers water from basins west of the metropolitan area and supplements heavily exploited aquifers. Low reservoir levels, groundwater depletion, subsidence, leakage, unequal household access, and limited stormwater retention can convert climatic drought into a wider service and governance crisis, while intense rainfall can overwhelm drainage and mobility systems [24, 26, 48, 52, 59, 62, 63]. The city’s climate action program and water, risk-management, and land-use institutions provide an advanced municipal framework, but implementation is divided among Mexico City, federal water authorities, the State of Mexico, metropolitan municipalities, and multiple service providers. 

Monterrey’s 2022 water crisis combined prolonged drought, low precipitation, reservoir depletion, infrastructure deficiencies, and unequal household access [60, 68, 71, 82]. The municipal Climate Change Regulation and emerging Climate Action Programme align local action with Nuevo León’s state climate framework and federal law [62, 63, 79]. This vertical alignment creates a formal basis for action, but authority over water supply and metropolitan development extends beyond the municipality. The case therefore illustrates coordination challenges among municipal climate planning, state water governance, industrial demand, social policy, and metropolitan growth [68, 79]. 

Acapulco represents a different profile. Hurricane Otis in 2023 damaged housing, hotels, roads, electricity, water, telecommunications, and livelihoods, turning a rapidly intensifying cyclone into an urban economic and social crisis [53, 54, 58, 65]. Municipal Civil Protection maintains the Risk Atlas [80], while the Municipal Development Plan includes sustainability, climate, drainage, and emergency actions. These instruments operate within Guerrero and federal civil-protection and reconstruction arrangements.

The reviewed evidence documents strong post-disaster recognition but also indicates a continuing gap among hazard identification, land-use enforcement, infrastructure maintenance, early warning, and resources for anticipatory implementation [54, 55, 58, 80]. 

The Veracruz–Boca del Río conurbation shares coastal storms, flooding, drainage systems, mobility networks, and economic activity, but responsibility is divided between two municipalities and the State of Veracruz [61, 62, 81]. Separate municipal plans and civil-protection structures do not automatically create conurbation-wide risk governance. This case illustrates horizontal fragmentation: the ecological and infrastructure system is metropolitan, whereas planning authority and budgets remain institutionally divided [61, 81, 83]. 

Across the four Mexican cases, the principal deficit identified in the reviewed evidence is not simply limited planning but uneven connections among municipal instruments, metropolitan infrastructure, state and federal authority, financing, and enforcement. Mexico City has comparatively developed technical and policy capacity but complex cross-jurisdictional dependencies; Monterrey has formal climate alignment but divided metropolitan water authority; Acapulco has risk instruments but significant preparedness and implementation gaps; and Veracruz–Boca del Río faces coordination problems created by a shared risk system governed through separate municipalities [48, 54, 58–63, 68, 79–81]. 

#### **5 Comparative discussion: city profiles, multilevel governance, and implementation deficits** 

The city-level comparison distinguishes four profiles. New Orleans and Acapulco are coastal disaster-and-recovery cities, but New Orleans has a longer-established municipal–federal mitigation architecture, whereas the reviewed evidence for Acapulco indicates a larger gap between hazard identification and anticipatory implementation. Los Angeles and Mexico City are large metropolitan systems with comparatively strong analytical capacity, yet their principal risks cross agency and jurisdictional boundaries: heat–wildfire–energy–water interactions in Los Angeles and drought–aquifer–subsidence–flood interactions in Mexico City. Calgary and Edmonton are inland cities with formal adaptation strategies and relatively strong municipal planning capacity, while Toronto and Vancouver combine dense infrastructure with heat, flood, smoke, and coastal dependencies. Veracruz–Boca del Río and Monterrey demonstrate metropolitan risks for which ecological and infrastructure systems extend beyond the authority of a single municipality. These profiles are evidence-based syntheses of the sources reported in Table 2 rather than comparative rankings.

Ecological context affects which institutional relationships matter most. Coastal cities require coordination among land-use planning, drainage, coastal protection, housing, emergency response, and recovery. Water-stressed metropolitan areas require governance across basins, utilities, industry, land subsidence, demand management, and household access. Heat- and smoke-exposed cities require links among public health, housing quality, electricity reliability, emergency communication, and protection for outdoor workers and residents without adequate cooling. The same evidence matrix can therefore be replicated across cities, but its interpretation must remain sensitive to each city’s hazard pathways, infrastructure ownership, fiscal capacity, and social geography. 

Institutional capacity is consequently better evaluated through implementation relationships than through the mere presence of a strategy. Toronto, Vancouver, Calgary, Edmonton, and Los Angeles possess relatively developed planning instruments, yet coordination and financing gaps remain. New Orleans combines mature hazard planning with persistent equity and infrastructure challenges. Mexico City has strong technical diagnosis but fragmented metropolitan authority. The evidence reviewed for Monterrey, Acapulco, and Veracruz–Boca del Río indicates more pronounced gaps between municipal responsibility and the scale of the systems that must be governed. Across the cases, three practical indicators emerge for future comparison: whether city plans map infrastructure interdependencies; whether multilevel responsibilities and finance are formally assigned; and whether implementation is monitored through funded actions and outcome indicators.

#### **6 Conclusion** 

The reviewed literature suggests that cascading climate risks are shaped by common systemic mechanisms as well as differentiated national and urban contexts. In the United States, the selected evidence points to federal policy volatility, uneven local capacity, and the need for durable funding, alongside subnational innovation and community-centered resilience planning [8, 36]. In Canada, the national adaptation architecture provides an important foundation, while municipal implementation, infrastructure interdependence, Indigenous governance, and long-term finance remain prominent challenges [38, 43–47, 64, 72, 75–78]. In Mexico, the reviewed cases indicate that urban inequality, water insecurity, infrastructure deficits, informality, and fragmented metropolitan governance can intensify cascading effects [48, 54, 58–63, 67, 68, 79–81]. 

Compound and cascading climate risks are therefore an important challenge for the urban resilience agenda in North America. Cities are exposed not only to individual hazards but also to hazards that interact with one another and with the infrastructure, social, and governance systems that sustain urban life. A heatwave can become an energy and public-health crisis; a drought can become a water, economic, and governance crisis; and a hurricane can become a housing, tourism, telecommunications, transport, energy, food-supply, and social-protection crisis. These interactions can produce severe impacts and demonstrate the systemic character of urban climate risk. 

Across the three countries, the synthesis indicates that isolated adaptation measures are unlikely to address the full range of cascading relationships. More integrated risk governance can help connect infrastructure dependencies, social vulnerability, institutional responsibilities, and anticipatory finance. Without prospective scenario planning, urban-infrastructure stress testing, well-informed and integrated policies, and stable implementation mechanisms co-designed for the needs and capacities of affected communities, cities may remain dependent on reactive cycles of emergency response and reconstruction rather than sustained risk reduction. 

This article contributes a structured qualitative framework for examining compound and cascading climate risks as environmental, infrastructural, social, and institutional processes. Its comparative value lies in showing how a shared set of analytical dimensions can reveal both recurring mechanisms and context-specific governance constraints. Because the review is targeted and the evidence base is uneven, the findings should not be interpreted as a comprehensive ranking or causal assessment of the selected cities. Future research could test the matrix through systematic city-level datasets, policy-implementation indicators, and longitudinal analysis of infrastructure disruptions and recovery. Within these limits, the review suggests that urban resilience will increasingly depend on cities’ capacity to govern relationships among infrastructure, inequality, finance, and climate uncertainty in coordinated and equitable ways.
