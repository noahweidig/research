# Making sense of wildfire prevention in the southwestern United States: Workforce perspectives on organizational challenges in federal land management agencies

## Abstract

Abstract Wildfire prevention yields a high return on investment, yet research suggests that efforts to modernize prevention resources, programs, and funding within U.S. federal land management agencies are scarce despite rising ignition risk. Investigating the experiences and needs of fire prevention staff tasked with undertaking risk reduction while interacting with a rapidly diversifying portfolio of public land users, including current challenges and opportunities that would benefit from additional support, is critical for advancing holistic wildfire adaptation on public lands. We conducted semi-structured interviews with 31 federal agency employees for whom fire prevention was a primary job responsibility in the U.S. states of Arizona and New Mexico to document the current state of fire prevention in federal land management agencies. Interviews uncovered three core challenges: a lack of clarity about what prevention entails and its role within federal agencies, inability to demonstrate prevention “success,” and a lack of capacity for innovation. Promotion opportunities under these constraints were limited, resulting in a wildfire prevention ‘brain drain’ to other agencies or regions of the U.S. that retention of local knowledge not yet captured by data or decision support tools. These systemic and localized barriers motivated interest in a more defined balance between position responsibilities and role flexibility. We use organizational sensemaking and sensegiving as a theoretical lens to provide actionable insights for agency decision makers, emphasizing a need for strategic resource coordination and greater clarity surrounding what prevention entails to demonstrate programmatic value and encourage collective action.

Investigating the experiences and needs of fire prevention staff tasked with undertaking risk reduction while interacting with a rapidly diversifying portfolio of public land users, including current challenges and opportunities that would benefit from additional support, is critical for advancing holistic wildfire adaptation on public lands. Unwanted human-caused wildfire ignitions on public lands continue to increase, underscoring the need for adaptive management action that can meld local social and ecological knowledge with federal land management missions to inform effective risk reduction (Abrams et al. 2015, p. 5; Balch et al. 2017, p. 2947; Nagy et al. 2018, p. 1). U.S. federal land management agencies tasked with wildfire risk reduction employ staff to conduct activities that support wildfire prevention, typically distributed across local units like U.S. Forest Service (USFS) ranger districts or Bureau of Land Management (BLM) field offices (Christiansen et al. 1976, p. 3; Edgeley et al. 2025, p. 494). Prevention staff must enact unique, anticipatory combinations of education, engineering, enforcement, and administration activities

as dictated by available funding, staff capacity, and placebased conditions to progress risk reduction (Kohler and Evans 2021, NIFC 2017, p. 2) Return on investment for these activities is high; one study found that for every dollar spent on prevention education, $35 in wildfire damages was saved (Prestemon et al. 2010, p. 188). Although prevention has become a foundational component of federal wildfire risk mitigation, funding, staffing, and resources to support this work receive limited attention in broader agency discussions about wildfire (Kohler and Evans 2021). 

Federal prevention staff are under pressure to demonstrate the impact of their work amidst turbulent financial and political contexts surrounding wildfire (Doolittle 1980; Schultz et al. 2019, p. 13). Research suggests that the tools and science available to these employees to accomplish risk reduction are outdated and underdeveloped; many studies regarded as best available science were conducted between 1950 and 1980 and focused on education strategies, leaving uncertainty about the most suitable engineering and enforcement interventions and the utility of technological innovations like the internet, cell phones, and social media on outreach strategies (Edgeley et al. 2025, p. 513). Furthermore, these efforts tended to focus on individual prevention activities (e.g., classroom visits, signage), limiting comprehensive understandings of how prevention staff design and implement comprehensive programs that leverage a broad suite of activities for their jurisdictions and retroactively assess their impact (Edgeley et al. 2025, p. 515). Characterizing how prevention staff engage in and respond to evolving wildfire management and policy across scales, and the extent to which they influence and are influenced by adjacent processes such as suppression, hazardous fuels management, and regulative policy is important for understanding the place and impact of wildfire prevention strategies (Devenport and Edgeley 2025; Hesseln 2018, p. 183). 

The catalysts and barriers that enable or inhibit wildfire prevention are not well articulated, inviting empirical examination of the experiences and knowledge that prevention staff bring to federal land management (Stanley et al. 2021, p. 226). Documenting how prevention staff act and interact at the nexus between federal land management agency structures and local landscapes to reduce humancaused ignitions represents a logical next step for identifying factors affecting inertia and motivating progress in wildfire risk reduction programing and capacity. We conducted semi-structured interviews with 31 federal wildfire prevention staff in the Southwestern U.S. states of Arizona and New Mexico, investigating how participants characterized their management roles and responsibilities, the factors enabling or constraining their work, and the extent to which place-based prevention in large organizations is integrated and advanced. We use the intertwined lenses of 

sensemaking (Weick 1995a) and sensegiving (Gioia and Chittipeddi 1991) to explore cohesion and fragmentation within the federal fire prevention workforce, highlighting areas where attention is needed to improve the efficacy and impact of prevention work on public lands through identity construction. Social science designed to untangle how inertia and innovation emerge and intermingle within the federal wildfire management workforce offers valuable context to inform progress towards cohesive wildfire adaptation (Edgeley 2023, p. 1657). Furthermore, investigation of the relationship between sensemaking and sensegiving under uncertainty or ambiguity in natural resource contexts offers insights for other fields such as hazard management that are currently also undergoing stochastic periods of organizational change. 

## **2 Literature review** 

### **2.1 The federal wildfire prevention workforce** 

Federal wildfire prevention in the United States has evolved from an informal practice to a formal organization-based network of positions, programs, and partnerships over the past century. Ignition prevention emerged as a shared responsibility in early federal documents like the first Forest Service Use Book (Pinchot 1905, p. 1). Staff positions with prevention responsibilities were established in the 1950– 1960s; the USFS began hiring fire prevention technicians, guards, and patrolmen while the BLM introduced fire prevention patrol positions at select field offices in high fire risk areas (Alexander 1974, p. 4; Muhn and Stuart 1988). Agencies later began developing or revising internal policies to support regulatory prevention activities that necessitated increased prevention staffing for effective implementation (e.g., 36 CFR 261, the USFS Code of Federal Regulations). Current prevention staffing structures were established in the 1990s. Prevention staff today are responsible for implementing and sustaining activities across three categories: education, which entails efforts such as classroom visits and interactions with recreationists, engineering, which involves infrastructure that reduces ignition risk such as campfire rings, and enforcement, including fines and citations as well as stage restrictions and campfire bans (NIFC 2017, p. 2; Riebold 1957, p. 66). These efforts are overseen by administrative support and collectively referred to as the fire prevention triangle<sup>1</sup> .

While each federal land management agency enacts a similar prevention mission to reduce human-caused fires, responsibility for this work is internally housed in different ways. For instance, USFS hires prevention specialists and technicians who prioritize prevention activities like public education and patrolling in their day-to-day responsibilities. The BLM assigns prevention responsibilities as one component of a broader public-facing position focused on mitigation, education, and trespass that supports visitor management and safety. The National Park Service (NPS) has no dedicated roles, instead weaving wildfire prevention into its educational mission via signage, enforcement, and visitor programming. The Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) began explicitly engaging in prevention in the early 2000s, hiring prevention staff to both educate about, and strengthen distinctions between, cultural use of fire and unwanted human-caused fires. Despite the universal necessity of human-caused fire prevention on public lands, prevention staff represent a small percentage of federal agency staffing; for instance, they represent less than 4% of the USFS fire workforce (Kohler and Evans 2021). 

The geography, people, and contexts that prevention staff must address have grown as access to public lands increases, conditions conducive for fire ignition and spread expand to higher elevations and non-fire adapted ecosystems, and recreationists using public lands diversify (Jenkins et al. 2023, p. 9; Narayanaraj and Wimberly 2012, p. 878). Prevention staff must also navigate growing risk from ignitions on adjacent private property that burn onto public land, further complicating public education and implementation of regulatory risk reduction approaches (Ager et al. 2017, p. 12; Downing et al. 2022, p. 3). With these conditions comes the need for greater staff presence to support law enforcement and education, development of modified rules and regulations related to human-caused ignitions (e.g., designating shooting areas and allowing propane fires during stage restrictions), and time and capacity to comprehensively document the rising number of human-caused ignitions to inform more effective prevention programming (Dolman et al. 2026, p. 9; Edgeley et al. 2026, p. 5; Prestemon et al. 2019, p. 408). Expanded responsibilities among existing prevention staff is contrasted against stagnant or declining funding support and reductions in permanent and seasonal staffing, and further complicated by the absence of standardized measures to track program outcomes (Kohler and Evans 2021; Thomas et al. 2013, p. 105). Such conditions restrict the work that place-based prevention staff can conduct and their ability to innovate within their local unit to improve risk reduction. 

Centralized and interagency programs that gather data regarding number, cause, location, and timing of ignitions among other characteristics have become commonplace 

to better articulate the prevention landscape, supporting reflexive program adjustments that address local drivers of human-caused ignitions. These data influence funding allocation for prevention and provide a baseline from which to measure both changes in risk and the effectiveness of prevention interventions, yet variations in organizational culture and funding have produced inconsistent documentation between and within agencies (Thomas and Butry 2012, p. 40; Thomas et al. 2013, p. 104). Furthermore, studies suggest that evaluating prevention programs using fire occurrence data may not reflect the full scope or success of staff efforts and retain difficulty estimating fires "avoided” (Edgeley et al. 2025, p. 512; Wetherill 1980). Tools like the Wildfire Prevention Spatial Assessment and Planning Strategies (WPSAPS) software have been introduced across federal agencies to begin building connections between ignition risk assessments and prevention activities, allowing the user to input characteristics of their program (e.g., number of staff, funding available) and local conditions (e.g., fire history, roads) to generate high-level wildfire prevention plans for more efficient allocation of resources based on risk (USDA 2021). However, it remains unclear what factors influence planning and implementation of prevention practices, the extent to which such data affect dayto-day activities, or the impact that decision support tools like WPSAPS have on longstanding prevention challenges. 

Concerns about the structure and capacity of prevention programming in U.S. federal agencies are not novel; low staffing levels, unclear job expectations, limited training and development opportunities, absence of successful evaluation tools, and finite opportunities for career advancement have been documented for more than 40 years (Christiansen et al. 1976, p. 9; Donoghue and Main 1985, p. 88; Simard and Donoghue 1987, p. 188; Wetherill 1982, p. 4). Regional capacity often depends on how fire risk is prioritized in funding decisions or agency leadership, meaning that investment and innovation in fire prevention can vary widely across the U.S. (Kohler and Evans 2021). To minimize the consequences of these conditions, collaboration and partnerships have emerged as one way to expand prevention capacity and increase effectiveness through repeated contacts with consistent messaging. This includes the use of interagency Fire Prevention and Education Teams (FPETs) and strategic partnerships with community leaders, businesses or non-profits, and volunteer groups (Devenport and Edgeley 2026, p. 16; Dickerson 1969, p. 20; McDaniel et al. 2021, p. 9). Siloed operations and inconsistent practices often hinder collaboration; past work has therefore stressed the need for clear prevention roles and responsibilities that support interagency cooperation (Bradshaw 1988, p. 199; Christiansen 1976, p. 12).

### **2.2 Sensemaking and sensegiving in federal land management agencies** 

Sensemaking refers to the process of establishing collective understandings of events or issues and their related uncertainty and ambiguity that often emerge from retroactive examination of surprising or discrepant observations and experiences to provide a shared framework for creating purpose, meaning, and direction (Maitlis and Christianson 2014, p. 58; Weick 1995a, p. 4). This “frame of reference” is a product of seven concurrent properties (Weick 1995a, p. 17). First, it is grounded in identity construction, meaning that a person interprets things through the lens of their organizational, professional, and social identities, which in turn shapes their observations and actions. Second, it is retrospective, revealing that people often come to understand things only by reflecting on them afterwards. Third, it requires the “enactment of sensible environments”–that is, people creating their own environments through routines, actions, and interactions that they then interpret. Fourth, it is a product of social activity, engaging collective action to co-construct understandings that emerge through and encourage shared actions and problem solving. Fifth, sensemaking is an ongoing process that evolves through continuous adjustment triggered by new events, conditions, or cues. Sixth, because there are so many concurrent cues that influence sensemaking, people and organizations often extract a manageable subset that embody their understandings to anchor meanings. Finally, selection and organization of a subset of cues under broader sensemaking conditions produce the most plausible understanding, rather than the most accurate one, to allow feasible and tangible progress forward. Best characterized as a process, sensemaking is often depicted as cyclical, further reinforcing understanding or evolving to hold new meaning over time (Bajwa et al. 2020, p. 97; Weick 1988, p. 308). This means that these frameworks (e.g., roles, rules, procedures, activities, authority) and meanings can both build and remove one another (Weick 1993, p. 636). 

Sensemaking is often studied within government organizations like the U.S. Forest Service because the conditions for co-creating and acting upon shared meanings are already inherent and parameterized in these spaces (for examples, see: Nowell and Stutler 2020; Weick 1993, Weick 1995b, 1996). While impactful events, disasters, or crises are common triggers for intentional organizational sensemaking, ambiguity or uncertainty surrounding a problem are also motivators. This can include instances where there is difficulty measuring success, absence of clarity regarding roles or responsibilities, poor availability and reliability of data, and conflicting interpretations of the problem using those data (Maitlis and Sonenshein 2010, p. 551; Weick 1988, 

p. 305, 1995a, p. 95). While the issues that governmental entities are mission-obligated to address continue to complexify (e.g., drought, climate change), their responses and structures can drift towards “architectures of simplicity” that often fail to address root causes or produce incohesive action to address the problem over time and space (Colville et al. 2012, p. 6; Miller 1993, p. 116; Paveglio 2021, p. 620). This is particularly true within federal land management agencies where best practices are typically built on retrospective observations and assessments, yet “unprecedented” conditions perpetuate that negate their effectiveness (Nowell and Stutler 2020, p. 133). An organization can be good at decision making yet still experience events or issues because of deficient sensemaking, indicating the importance of shared understandings that parameterize the problem within different roles and facets of an organization to produce aligned actions to address it (Jensen and Mahmud 2023, p. 1; Weick 1993, p. 636). Emphasis on sensemaking within organizations can improve organizational capacity for unprecedented events and evolving social and ecological conditions (Maitlis and Sonenshein 2010, p. 567). 

Investigations of sensemaking to understand federal land management agency responses and learning are most common after notable wildfire fatality incidents the 1949 Man Gulch, 1994 South Canyon, and 2013 Yarnell Hill Fires demonstrate how shared understandings (or lack thereof) influence subsequent organizational action, often driven by retrospective examination of how these events unfolded to provide a potential explanation for what happened (Parrish et al. 2020, p. 86; Weick 1993, p. 630, 1996, p. 309; Williams and Ishak 2018, p. 303). Across these studies, organizational structure both enabled and inhibited sensemaking. For instance, examination of the 2016 Chimney Tops 2 Fire suggests that structural stagnation and rigidity within federal agencies prevented proactive responses to unusual cues associated with unprecedented environmental conditions, affecting suppression activity (Nowell and Stutler 2020, p. 132). Researchers have begun to explore sensemaking in other fire-related contexts, including resident-agency tensions surrounding fuel treatments (e.g., Jahn et al. 2020), risk communication regarding wildfire smoke (e.g., Thomas et al. 2022), post-fire recovery (e.g., Daurio 2026), and evacuation (e.g., Taylor et al. 2007. This growing body of literature indicates that sensemaking is an effective framework for investigating inertia and innovation around varied wildfire risk reduction processes, and highlights how multidirectional interaction across organizational scales supports identity formation among agency staff. 

Sensemaking is often tied to “sensegiving,” the process of influencing how others understand and interpret ambiguous or uncertain situations through meaning construction and reconstruction toward a preferred interpretation of

Organizational sensegiving often occurs through symbolic (inter)actions by leadership to signal a vision, identity, or change in approach to members or employees (Gioia et al. 1994, pp. 364–366; Rouleau 2005, p. 1424). Sensegiving is most visible during phases of instability, ambiguity, or uncertainty (e.g., leadership turnover, during unprecedented events) as a way to signal organizational change and stimulate or influence sensemaking as organization members seek to translate new guidance or purpose to their role (Balogun and Johnson 2004, p. 536). Organizational sensegiving in wildfire contexts is often initiated through written communication such as letters of intent from the U.S. Forest Service Chief and organization-wide plans such as the 2022 "Wildfire Crisis Strategy" for reducing wildfire risk nationwide. However, it is interesting to note that many sensegiving efforts from federal agency leadership rarely mention the role of prevention, instead focusing on fire suppression and fuels management. Sensegiving and sensemaking often occur simultaneously and iteratively as members interpret and re-interpret one another’s values, allowing organizations to emerge with a refined vision and shared understandings of how to enact it. However, this process can also exacerbate ambiguity or introduce conflict (Gioia and Chittipeddi 1991, p. 434; Maitlis 2005, p. 21). Existing wildfire communication studies often examine elements that align with sensegiving, including how meaning is introduced and then reinforced through organization messaging to the public about wildfire risk (e.g., Paveglio et al. 2009; Brenkert-Smith et al. 2013) or how agencies may be able to reduce uncertainty after wildfire by considering the timing and content of communication (e.g., Taylor et al. 2007), but do not explicitly tie these observations to sensegiving. Melding examinations of sensemaking and sensegiving provides an opportunity to explore the multi-directionality of identity creation within federal land management agencies. 

The experiences of wildfire prevention staff in federal land management agencies have yet to be examined through the lens of organizational sensemaking, positioning this study to proactively explore current conditions for shared meanings and collective action among this workforce. Observations about the relationship between sensegiving and sensemaking made here are positioned to inform strategic guidance for the natural resource management workforce more broadly under growing uncertainty and ambiguity. Here, we seek to investigate how prevention staff understand their role and responsibilities relative to their organizational structure and mission, with a focus on identity construction within the sensemaking process. This provides opportunities to identify potential discrepancies in understanding and opportunities for co-defining prevention, 

including how these shared or separate meanings influence programmatic activities and interventions to reduce human-caused wildfire ignitions. Existing scientific inquiry appears to focus on gaps and needs in prevention spaces as separate from the staff responsible for enacting this work, further emphasizing the importance of documenting firsthand experiences and observations among prevention staff. We explore the following research questions in response to these needs: 

1. What factors shape prevention sensemaking and sensegiving in federal land management agencies? 

2. What barriers and opportunities exist for advancing fire prevention? 

## **3 Methods** 

We conducted 30 semi-structured interviews with 31 federal employees in Arizona and New Mexico for whom wildfire prevention was their primary job responsibility<sup>2</sup> . This area, referred to as “Region 3” among USFS staff, was the focus of a broader study to understand fire prevention in the Southwestern U.S., selected because of a recent uptick in human-caused ignitions. The Southwest is often acknowledged as a pioneering region for federal wildfire prevention, particularly related to prevention education given the New Mexican origins of Smokey Bear, providing a rich history of prevention activity to draw from in interviews (Caro 2024). We met with three key informants in leadership positions within the USFS and BLM prior to data collection to inform study design and interview protocol development. Semi-structured interviews were best suited for this study because we could capture nuances between jurisdictions and agencies while ensuring confidentiality for interviewees identifying challenges and offering critiques of current prevention programming. This approach also fills a methodological niche; research on prevention staff to date has focused exclusively on surveying, limiting more nuanced understandings. No studies of prevention staff in the U.S. have occurred since 1980 to our knowledge (for the most recent examples, see Doolittle et al. 1975; Christiansen et al. 1976; Doolittle 1980). 

We developed a semi-structured interview protocol to allow enough flexibility to follow the interviewee’s line of thought and investigate emergent topics, supporting comprehensive, inductive development of study themes. ## **4 Findings** 

### **4.1 Factors affecting prevention sensemaking** 

No two interviewees gave the same definition of “prevention” as it pertained to their role, noting that their position descriptions did not offer clarity on what prevention was or the responsibilities associated with it. While some characterized prevention as encompassing all wildfire risk reduction 

activities (e.g., education, enforcement, and engineering activities as well as broader actions such as fuels treatments, home hardening on private property, and wildfire suppression), others offered narrower visions that focused education or enforcement. This dissensus appeared to stem from ambiguity about the difference between prevention and mitigation, a distinction that interviewees described as blurred across all scales of government, from local land management units to Congress. An interviewee summarized: 

_Prevention is stopping the fire before it starts_ , _okay. Mitigation is reducing the likelihood of that ignition either one_ , _occurring_ , _or two_ , _having severe detrimental effects. So_ , _they’re different. With the prevention side of things_ , _I’ve heard safety guys say_ , _“why mitigate when you can eliminate?” and prevention is the elimination portion of that; mitigation is lessening the blow_ , _you might say. And in some respects_ , _mitigation–this is where it gets fuzzy–mitigation can be prevention because people start talking about a spark arrestor. Is a spark arrestor prevention or mitigation? I don’t know._ 

Inconsistency surrounding what prevention entailed led to wide ranging articulation of personal roles and responsibilities as interviewees engaged in self-guided interpretation of their positions within their local units. Interviewees described themselves as generalists, emphasizing that they were expected to adapt to local and agency needs depending on whether there were active fires, upticks in recreation, or changes in staff capacity by drawing on other skillsets such as past suppression experience or fuels knowledge. One interviewee shared that prevention staff _“pretty much have the freedom to do whatever they want in the name of land management.”_ However, there was disagreement among interviewees about whether such a wide diversity of responsibilities was beneficial, underscoring nuanced interpretations of prevention plausibility and difficulty placing themselves within larger organizational missions. While some enjoyed the blended nature of prevention and the flexibility it provided in crafting unit-level programs, others saw the wide-ranging variation in prevention interpretation and enactment as a fragmenting force, weakening their collective ability to demonstrate the value of prevention within broader agency contexts. Amidst nebulous understandings and applications of prevention upheld by a perceived lack of organizational sensegiving, unit-level prevention programming characteristics were typically shaped by a combination of three factors: the interviewee’s career prior to prevention, their direct supervisor’s understanding of prevention, and how prevention related to agency mission. First, the interviewee’s background prior to entering prevention heavily informed local programming (e.g., an individual coming from fire suppression often retained some suppression-related responsibilities), acting as a waypoint for individuals to situate themselves within their organization’s culture. Program flexibility meant that prevention staff often prioritized the fire prevention element (i.e., education, enforcement, or engineering) that they were most comfortable with, regardless of whether associated activities were responsive to prevention needs within their jurisdiction. This flexibility also allowed prevention staff to be pulled away from core prevention activities to support other agency operations – an outcome some interviewees felt demonstrated that prevention was seen as a lower priority. While some saw being “on loan” to other agency efforts as a strength of their program and appreciated the variety because it supported integration of prevention into other activities to support more cooperative outcomes, others described how having infinite potential foci weakened their program impact by fracturing their capacity. An interviewee described the effect of these collateral duties as follows: 

_I’ve been on fires and I have an education program coming up that’s very important to me and I’m very passionate about. They’re like_ , _“Oh_ , _well_ , _is that really [more] important over this fire?” And I’m like_ , _“Yeah_ , _because I could have probably prevented this fire if I’d been out there doing more education.”_ 

Difficulty understanding what prevention entailed and the place of prevention staff in agency operations among colleagues was further bolstered by misconceptions about how prevention staff came to be employed in their current positions. This included widely held stereotypes described by interviewees as coloring how other staff interacted with them and valued their work, including resentment from suppression staff that good prevention work could put them “out of a job.” When asked about barriers to integrating prevention into agency functioning, one interviewee described how misconceptions isolated prevention staff: 

_Enforcement_ , _Education_ , _Education_ , _Administration_ , _and add a C to that: culture. Prevention has traditionally been a place where old firefighters go to retire and firefighters that can’t hack it in suppression go. And that’s our cultural reality. And if we don’t talk about cultural barriers_ , _and we don’t address them and we don’t dig into them_ , _we’re not doing ourselves any favors. When you work on a district in prevention_ , _you are considered sort of the weakest link in the culture._ 

This culture reflected what interviewees described as a consistent preference across federal land management agencies for investment and capacity in suppression over prevention. Some provided examples of how they felt stereotyping prevention staff led to their exclusion from conversations or decision-making spaces where they could have added significant value such as meetings about public outreach on fuels projects. Those in supervisory positions saw increased internal literacy about prevention as the most productive pathway to address and dissolve these assumptions. Interviewees were also quick to highlight the disconnect between priorities and actions this created as agency leadership increasingly focus on wildfire risk reduction: that investing in prevention would lessen the need for costly suppression activities in the first place by reducing the number of unwanted ignitions. Misconceptions surrounding prevention programs within federal agencies, paired with difficulty communicating purpose and value across government scales, prevented proactivity and fueled a deference to suppression, further reducing the plausibility of prevention as a salient and sustained value in land management. 

Second, programming appeared connected to the understanding an interviewees’ supervisor had about what prevention entailed and how it fit into agency operations at the local level. This could dictate funding (e.g., seasonal staffing, education supplies, and equipment to aid patrolling), program flexibility and goals, and broader support for prevention activities for each prevention person. Interviewees most often described success requesting additional funding and capacity for their programs in instances where their supervisor had a fire-focused background or good working knowledge of prevention. When supervisors were unfamiliar, interviewees described being siloed from other agency operations and had difficulty maintaining or improving their program without an advocate who could both engage other unit staff and communicate to the regional level about the value that prevention investments had. One interviewee summarized: “ _I was asking my boss_ , _“Well_ , _what do you expect of me?” and he’s like_ , _“Oh_ , _I don’t really know what you’re expected to do in prevention_ , _just take it and run with it…”_ District or field office level interviewees looked to their direct supervisor for purpose and direction, but the

### **4.2 Barriers to prevention progress** 

Interviewees described an absence of financial investment from agency leadership as a major driver of inertia in prevention. Unit-level prevention staff shared a perceived need to demonstrate “success” in prevention in order to counter this and attract greater buy-in. However, ascertaining that a human-caused ignition ceased to occur because of the actions of prevention staff proved extremely difficult. Interviewees instead shared proxies for determining impact, including observing children informing their parents of campfire safety after participating in education programs and documenting inferred relationships between actions and ignition data. One interviewee shared “ _If I’m providing chain covers for free to people coming in_ , _and I’m starting_ 

_to show less fires on the freeway because of it_ , _that’s how we’re going to measure it.”_ 

Interviewees framed ignition data as a tool that prevention staff could use to initiate conversation and action related to sensemaking with supervisors and higher-level organizational leadership. The accuracy of these data were considered paramount to communicating prevention’s value and helping other employees develop shared meaning around prevention efforts. Interviewees in leadership positions noted a recent uptick in undetermined ignitions that was seen as related to a scarcity of qualified fire investigators available to conduct formal investigations and make an official cause determination. Encouraging prevention staff to complete trainings and associated task books to become fire investigators was therefore a priority for ensuring improved ignition data quality. This was of greatest importance to BLM interviewees, whose agency funding structure partially relied on restitution associated with successful prosecution related to human-caused wildfires to fund prevention activities. Prioritizing investment in fire investigator trainings was seen as more fallible among USFS interviewees, who articulated an imbalance between training quality, compensation, and responsibility. One interviewee summarized: 

_Someone that’s paid $15 an hour_ , _that goes to a weeklong class and goes on a couple of fire investigations and now is qualified as a fire investigator–ask yourself_ , _is that person really a trained professional in the science of fire investigation? I would say it takes half of a lifetime to actually become a proficient fire investigator_ , _and probably a much higher salary._ 

Instead, USFS interviewees emphasized strategic assignment of available fire investigators to fires where restitution and legal proceedings would likely occur while encouraging those tasked with making decisions about ignition cause to provide an educated guess in cases without legal action to better the data available for decision-making. Given the high demand for fire investigators, interviewees across agencies noted the value of establishing dedicated fire investigator staff positions to support expertise development while acknowledging the unique nature of tasks associated with legal proceedings. 

Absence of data and inability to directly demonstrate success associated with prevention programming had cascading consequences for program support, creating a paradox surrounding financial investment in prevention success and funding. Some interviewees described how improved staffing led to greater capacity for documenting ignitions, resulting in an increase in the number of ignitions recorded on their unit that caused misguided assumptions that prevention was not effective. ### **4.3 Creating opportunities for sensemaking and sensegiving in federal fire prevention** 

Interviewees saw a multitude of opportunities to build shared identity and purpose across varied aspects of prevention work within their agencies and units in response to the challenges outlined above. Over the duration of each interview, participants described a core set of activities that were seen as foundational to prevention (e.g., patrolling, signage, and visiting classrooms) as becoming outdated or inadequate to address the changing pace and scope of humancaused wildfires. Not only was there a belief that message content was outdated, but also that the audiences targeted 

for these communications were not reflective of those causing unwanted ignitions. For example, one interviewee considered education focused on children as over-prioritized relative to other groups who were driving ignition patterns and dynamics like hunters or off-road vehicle users, stating: _“this idea on the education front of fire prevention that we target these audiences that have nothing to do with the fires that are starting in our forests is just asinine.”_ Others felt that urban visitors to rural units brought the greatest ignition risk but had no funding or mechanisms available to intercept these audiences prior to visitation. As the kinds of people visiting public lands diversified and communication approaches evolved, interviewees argued that the resources available to them for fire prevention could benefit from thoughtful modernization. One often-identified pathway to address this lack of connection was the integration of placebased messages and content into standard sign templates and education materials. One interviewee provided: 

_I think people are generally proud of where they’re from and proud of what makes their respective home or community kind of unique or different. And I think if we were able to appeal to that local flavor_ , _that local sense of pride… it’s the same with sports teams and where you live and who you cheer for and maybe how involved you are personally. So_ , _I think if forests were able to have some signage that was more locality based_ , _it could potentially be more effective._ 

Investment in prevention was often synonymous with investment in staffing. However, interviewees pointed to the current structure of prevention within federal agencies as a driver of turnover and difficulty retaining local knowledge, leaving local units with a prevention knowledge deficit that had real consequences related to wildfire occurrence and impacts on their districts. ## **5 Discussion** 

We sought to renew and deepen social understandings of human-caused wildfire prevention by interviewing federal land management staff with prevention responsibilities across Arizona and New Mexico to document the organizational, social, and personal contexts they operate within. We uncovered three factors that limited the impact of wildfire prevention work: insufficient clarity or standardization (i.e. sensegiving) about what prevention entails and its role within federal land management agencies, difficulty demonstrating prevention “success,” and a lack of capacity for progress or transformation. These challenges were systemically created and reenforced by federal agency structures and organizational culture, complicating the ability of unitlevel staff to actively engage in sensemaking. Our study reveals a perceived lack of interaction between federal employees, from district or field office to national program offices, that restricts the social construction and reinforcement of a plausible understanding of the meaning, role, and value of prevention. Below, we explore these findings by research question and provide suggestions for more intentional incorporation of prevention into federal land management to support cohesive sensemaking and sensegiving.

### **5.1 Prevention sensemaking and sensegiving in federal land management agencies** 

Our study extends documentation presenting a persistent absence of organizational clarity about prevention in federal land management. Studies of the prevention workforce conducted more than 40 years ago reveal similar challenges related to staff capacity, job expectations, siloed prevention efforts, program evaluation, and career advancement (Christiansen et al. 1976, p. 9; Donoghue and Main 1985, p. 88; Simard and Donoghue 1987, p. 188; Wetherill 1982, p. 4). This pattern indicates an inherent absence of conditions for productive sensemaking among local-level prevention staff, and a lack of plausibility regarding prevention at the organizational level. Federal land management agencies may therefore lack the mechanisms necessary to transform local prevention action and meaning into a shared organizational narrative, leaving prevention staff to construct meaning through local experience (typically via interactions with the public and non-prevention staff) rather than sensegiving provided by leadership or prevention peers. The prevailing organizational model of prevention staff as lone staff members at their field office or district in Arizona and New Mexico further restricts identity construction, as there are fewer opportunities to engage in the social interactions necessary to forge meanings and plausibility that are central to identity and subsequent sensemaking without a state- or forest-level prevention lead or colleagues with nuanced understandings of prevention. If prevention is to remain a core element of federal land management, agencies may need to reconsider how they structure, support, and interact with prevention staff to increase opportunities for sensemaking processes. 

Sensemaking is grounded in self-referential identity construction, meaning that a person interprets experiences through the lens of their organizational, professional, and social identities which in turn shapes their own observations and actions (Weick 1995a, p. 23). Interviewees exemplified this as they described tensions between agency mission, the place and plausibility of prevention across organizational scales, and their own backgrounds as influencing their prevention programming, using interactions with peers and supervisors to form their own understandings and applications of prevention at the unit level. Notably, demand for sensemaking appeared highest among USFS interviewees, who saw their prevention identity as less supported and understood relative to other agencies like the NPS, perhaps because their agency’s mission was more complex and staff roles spanned a wider range of efforts. Interviewee’s identities were also constructed by peers and supervisors who assigned stereotypes to prevention staff to inform their inclusion and engagement in other agency efforts or who had misguided understandings of what prevention entailed. This 

external identity construction reflects how identity is often developed through cues from others about how to act and react–what Weick (1995a) calls the enactment of sensible environments–and reinforces the importance of improving opportunities for prevention staff across units and agencies to interact with one another to support sensemaking. Each interviewee’s unit-level program was therefore a microcosm of what they interpreted prevention to be based on their own routines, actions, and interactions, situated within and influenced by local contexts, place attachment, and relationships. Searching for commonalities in prevention enactment and construction of meaning as prevention staff forge their own localized identity offers an initial step toward identifying what should become routine structure and what should remain flexible if agencies are interested in engaging with sensegiving. 

Disjointed understandings about what prevention is and how it is operationalized resulted in difficulty articulating its value across organizational scales, affecting the perceived legitimacy of prevention staff and programming. Without a unified, well-defined approach, interviewees felt that prevention was consistently overlooked, driven in part by difficulty identifying the “place” of prevention relative to suppression and other agency foci. Our research demonstrates the value of sensegiving for informing prevention sensemaking, and how the absence of the former affects the latter. It also reveals overlooked elements of prevention sensemaking that limit more cohesive action and interaction: namely, that there is less evidence of enactment, retrospective construction of meaning, and other two-way processes that support the creation and reinforcement of plausible narratives linking prevention to broader organizational identities, routines, and purposes. This asymmetry has persisted in part because prevention has become decentralized as efforts increasingly focus on localized, place-based contexts to infer meaning and determine action, reducing opportunities to contribute to shared organizational understandings. Sensemaking might now occur more readily at the local level, leaving questions about the scale(s) at which sensegiving could or should occur. Interviewees appeared to seek local level sensegiving from their direct supervisors, but expected their agency’s national level leadership to articulate the identity of prevention staff within the organization as a whole. This multi-level chain of sensegiving and respective sensemaking was not congruent because leaders at different levels ascribed different meanings to prevention work, resulting in variation across districts and field offices. The relationship between sensemaking and sensegiving in federal wildfire prevention is evidently multi-scalar, and therefore vulnerable to fragmentation in different ways depending on the organizational level.

Prevention, by our participants’ interpretations and enactments, is in and of itself a boundary spanning activity that cannot be compartmentalized to one area of land management, further complicating multi-directional identity creation. Research on collaborative wildfire risk management has repeatedly demonstrated the importance of “boundary spanners”–individuals who can advocate and influence how decisions are made at levels above or below their own– for motivating grassroots change and more inclusive outcomes (Davis et al. 2021; Goodrich et al. 2020, p. 45; Hoffman et al. 2024, p. 2; Edgeley and Paveglio 2024, p. 4; Paveglio 2021, p. 624). However, at present, it appears that prevention staff are restricted to operating and interacting at a singular local “scale” with few avenues for spanning organizational scales. These observations parallel existing studies that demonstrate how the rigidity of organizational structures prevent cultural and systemic change despite efforts to undo the legacy of U.S. suppression policies over the past century (Franz et al. 2024, p. 2; Schultz et al. 2019). Reenvisioning prevention staff as boundary spanners positions them to play a critical role in removing silos between internal groups and offices within land management agencies at the local level while also refining their organizational identity. 

### **5.2 Barriers and opportunities for prevention progress** 

Organizational design has been shown to prevent wildfirerelated sensemaking (Nowell and Stutler 2020, p. 134). Perhaps the most notable barrier interviewees described was the organizational staff structure within their respective agencies that prevented shared meanings and collective action. Building a clearer, more incremental vertical staffing structure was often referred to as central to strengthening federal fire prevention programming, particularly for overcoming gaps in internal communication across scales. As efforts to refine understandings and enactment of prevention progress, finding a balance between developing hierarchical, prescriptive structures and decentralized flexibility appears fundamental to support adaptive management strategies at the unit level while remaining responsive to overarching agency missions. This balance across interacting scales aligns with prevention studies that emphasize the importance of unit-level flexibility to support local program adaptation (Devenport and Edgeley 2025) and wildfire adaptation literature more broadly (Byerly et al. 2020; Monroe et al. 2013, p. 182; Paveglio and Shriner-Beaton 2025, p. 14). Where capacity allows, adding more seasonal or entry level staff, employing forest-level prevention staff who could coordinate unit-level prevention activities, and the establishment of specialized fire investigation positions could help improve existing disjointed staff structures while 

simultaneously improving place-based knowledge retention, opportunities for shared meaning construction, and refining position-based identities. 

Public interactions are uniquely positioned to inform prevention sensegiving in the absence of organizational guidance, as interviewees frequently described them as a motivator of prevention success and program structure. Specifically, using public interactions to support the development of shared elements of identity among prevention staff within and between agencies could expand capacity for prevention progress in outreach messaging and activities–an area that was repeatedly identified by interviewees as requiring modernization. Uncertainty about the “correct” audience for current prevention messaging indicates misalignment between the scale that prevention is currently being operationalized at (i.e., the local unit level) and the geography of humans introducing ignition risk to that unit. Collaboration and strategic pooling of resources between units or agenices that target larger areas to capture hubs of ingress for recreation (e.g., large ad campaigns or partnerships with urban chambers of commerce) appear central to more comprehensive risk prevention. Creating funding channels that support interagency, interjurisdictional, and sub-regional campaigns (e.g., Phoenix, AZ and the Tonto National Forest, or Albuquerque, NM and the Santa Fe National Forest) positions prevention staff to act at more appropriate scales when messaging about salient topics like forest closures or campfire use (Devenport and Edgeley 2026, p. 12; Edgeley et al. 2026, p. 10). Prevention could also benefit more broadly from mirroring other coordination structures and efforts beyond regional quarterly meetings (e.g., weekly interagency meetings during fire season, development of unified messaging) to begin prompting collective action and producing opportunities for meaning creation via social interaction beyond the unit level. 

Emphasis on improved data quality as a mechanism for parameterizing prevention “success” presented another context for engaging sensemaking to build a cohesive identity by demonstrating prevention value. People and organizations often extract a manageable subset of cues that embody their understandings and anchor meaning during sensemaking; ignition data is therefore an exemplary “sensemaking mechanism” grounded in a shared issue that was universally relied upon by interviewees (Sackmann 1991, p. 5). Reliance on ignition data, despite its vulnerabilities and inconsistencies, demonstrates how interviewees seek to develop plausible (rather than accurate) understandings of the human-caused ignition issue based on the information available to them to incite progress. Increased use of the undetermined cause category in ignition reporting reflects “uncorrected observations and experience” (Weick 1995a, p. 3) that intensify ambiguity in the short run until others

Prevention sensemaking is an ongoing process that will continue to evolve through iterative adjustments triggered by new events, conditions, or cues, underscoring the benefits of shared understandings and meanings of prevention across multiple levels within agency structures. The ambiguous, fragmented nature of prevention and related difficulty demonstrating prevention effectiveness in the absence of consistent metrics or evaluations leaves it vulnerable to termination or reorganization in periods of federal hardship when those in positions of power are unable to the collective value of unit-level efforts. This is particularly pertinent during current shifts in federal fire management towards more unified approaches, including but not limited to the recent creation of the U.S. Wildland Fire Service (which may introduce enough ambiguity or uncertainty to initiate a sensemaking event within federal fire management more broadly), consolidation of employees via office relocation or closure, and workforce reductions tied to changes in federal funding. Whether these changes strengthen or further fragment prevention sensemaking will depend less on the nature of the disruption and more on the ability of the relevant agency to develop coherent narratives and offer opportunities for social interaction that support meaning creation to help prevention staff (re)interpret their identity in an organizational context. More broadly, similar sensemaking challenges may emerge in relation to other natural resource management challenges, including water management, grazing, and air quality. These changes renew conversations about cooperative federalism as a model for wildfire risk reduction and hazards management more generally moving forward, underscoring a need to look beyond federal land to comprehensively address human-caused ignition risks and potentially reenvision the role and organizational location of prevention staff moving forward. 

## **6 Conclusion** 

The current federal prevention workforce is fragmented by inconsistent interpretations and actions that limit organizational ability to leverage and innovate shared capacity to motivate human-caused ignition risk reduction. Organizational sensemaking and sensegiving about prevention was in high demand among interviewees who sought a shared frame of reference about what prevention entailed to parameterize their work, articulate their organizational place and value, and to initiate the deconstruction of plausibility-related inertia. Sensemaking can go beyond shared meanings to invoke collective action that operationalizes understandings into coordinated outcomes; actions such as establishment of prevention staff positions with greater coordination capacity and strategic pooling of resources to reach urban audiences offer opportunities to turn shared meanings into progress (Weick 1995a, p. 188). Interviews revealed increasingly localized sensemaking efforts among prevention staff who were geographically and organizationally from social interaction and meaning construction with prevention peers. Needs identified here, including increased funding, increased staffing, and greater use of local knowledge, are consistently sought among fire staff within federal agencies, incorporation of prevention staff into these broader management discussions (Franz and Edgeley 2025; Thompson et al. 2023, p. 123). Despite these challenges, interviewees were willing to explore and trial new approaches and structures. Improving prevention morale and enhancing the legitimacy of prevention staff and their work remain paramount to more holistic fire management. 

Together, our findings support theoretical advancement of sensemaking and sensegiving in the wildfire literature by demonstrating that fragmented organizational structures weaken sensemaking. The absence of proactive sensegiving produces inconsistent interpretations of roles and responsibilities across unit, state, and regional levels, producing localized organizational identities that reflect employee backgrounds and understandings of place. While the flexibility to support adaptive, place-based management is central to generating prevention impact, provision of a scaffolding that supports comparison and collective action is necessary for prevention staff to unify. This study invites broader examination of the relevance and value of prevention; difficulty fostering sensemaking and sensegiving indicates that it is not consistently viewed as central to agency missions, and that consideration of how this work might be framed, informed, and situated within organizations may offer new avenues for identity construction among prevention staff.

**Catrin M. Edgeley** is an Assistant Professor of Human Dimensions of Natural Resources at Utah State University specializing in wildfire social science. She works with communities to support wildfire adaptation across varied social and ecological contexts, exploring how partnerships with land management agencies, non-government organizations, and local leaders to co-produce pathways forward. Cat earned her Ph.D. in Natural Resources from the University of Idaho and an 



MSc in Environmental Hazards and Risk and a BSc in Geography from Durham University, UK. 

**Gabriel Kohler** has more than 10 years of experience in forest and fire management, specializing in collaborative, cross-jurisdictional landscape restoration across the U.S. Southwest. He previously managed the Fire Adapted New Mexico Learning Network at the Forest Stewards Guild, led social and economic monitoring for large-scale forestry projects, and researched wildfire risk and human-caused ignitions. Gabe is an experienced firefighter, trained facilitator, and certified ESRI spa- 



tial data analyst, and holds an M.S. in Forest Ecosystems and Society from Oregon State University. 

**Sarah E. Devenport** is a Research Associate at the Ecological Restoration Institute in Flagstaff, Arizona. Her research focuses on wildfire social science, collaborative governance, and public perspectives of forest management. Sarah earned a B.S. and M.S. in Forestry from Northern Arizona University.
