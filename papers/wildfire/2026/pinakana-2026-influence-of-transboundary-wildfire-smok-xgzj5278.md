# Influence of transboundary wildfire smoke on fine particulate matter concentrations at South Texas border schools

## Abstract

Exposure to air pollutants in indoor and outdoor school environments poses significant health risks to children due to their heightened vulnerability. Increasing wildfire frequency has intensified transboundary smoke transport, with border communities among the first to experience associated air quality impacts. While the impacts of wildfires on air quality are increasingly recognized through satellite observations and modeling, ground-based observations remain scarce in under-monitored regions. Low-cost air sensors were deployed across schools in the Roma and Rio Grande City areas of Starr County, along the U.S.–Mexico border, for 146-days from November 2023 to April 2024. The raw sensor measurements were corrected using a collocation-based calibration with a research-grade instrument and analyzed alongside meteorological data, back trajectories, and satellite-derived smoke and fire products. Daily average fine particulate matter concentrations were significantly higher during smoke-impacted days compared with non-smoke periods ( p < 0.001), with site-level increases ranging from 8.61% to 54.9%. Trajectory analysis integrated with these measurements indicate that the wildfire emissions from Mexico significantly influence fine particulate pollution in South Texas, during the wildfire seasons. These findings highlight the need for targeted mitigation strategies to decrease the exposure amongst border communities, especially that of children.

Air pollution remains one of the leading environmental threats to human health worldwide. Exposure to elevated levels during early life is of particular concern, as it is associated with increased risk of both acute and chronic health effects. Children are more susceptible to the harmful effects of air pollution than adults due to several factors. These include a faster breathing rate, leading to greater air intake per body weight<sup>1</sup> , less efficient nasal filtering<sup>2</sup> , and an immature and developing immune system<sup>3</sup> . Asthma is the most common chronic disease in children, significantly impacting respiratory health. Exposure to air pollutants, particularly fine particulate matter (PM2.5), has been shown to increase the risk of asthmatic symptoms such as wheezing, airway inflammation, and reduced lung function growth<sup>4,5</sup> . Short-term exposure to ambient air pollutants was also associated with hospitalizations of children due to pneumonia<sup>6</sup> and lower respiratory tract infections<sup>7</sup> . Several largescale studies also indicated that exposure to air pollutants is associated with decreased academic performance in children<sup>8–10</sup> and with school absenteeism<sup>11</sup> . 

The school environment accounts for a major portion of children’s daily time-use activities. On average, children aged 5 to 18 years spend approximately 30.96 h per week at school, equivalent to~6.2 h per weekday, making it the second largest daily time-use activity after sleep, which accounts for 67.20 h a week, equivalent to ~9.6 h per day<sup>12</sup> . Hence, it is paramount to minimize the exposure levels at schools to reduce the detrimental impact on children’s short- and long-term health and their corresponding academic performances. Air pollution levels at schools are influenced by both indoor and outdoor sources, with outdoor emissions often playing the dominantrole in determiningthe indoor levels.A studyby Nwokorie et al.<sup>13</sup> revealed that outdoor sources accounted for more than 95%ofindoorPM2.5 levelsinschoolsinPortHarcourt,Nigeria.Theschool’s location also plays a key role in determining overall exposure burden, due to various factors such as proximity to roads or highways as well as other point or area sources of air pollution<sup>14</sup> . In the United States (U.S.), schools in low socio-economic status (SES) areas were associated with higher concentrations of PM2.5 and NO2 than schools with lower levels of poverty<sup>15</sup> . collected in this county. The results indicate that ambient PM2.5 concentrations were elevated between late February and early April 2024. Directional analysis showed elevated concentrations when air masses arrived from the S and SE, particularly at higher wind speeds, suggesting long-range transport as the likely source rather than local emissions. Backtrajectory analyzes using PSCF and CWT further supported this interpretation, with the source probability values being low in November through January and higher PSCF probabilities and elevated CWT valuesin February through April extending into northern and central Mexico. TrendsinwildfireactivityinMexico,peakingtowardmid-March,coincided with the period of PM2.5 exceedances observed. The convergence of windsector dependence trajectory modeling and satellite products of smoke and fire indicates that the transboundary wildfire smoke was indeed a dominant driver of episodic air pollution events in the studied region. 

Although this study’s results offer new insights into ambient PM2.5 concentrations in Starr County and their sources, there are some caveats thatshouldbetakenintoconsiderationwhileinterpretingthe findings.The low-cost air sensors used in this study were calibrated against a researchgrade instrument. The optical particle counters are sensitive to aerosol composition, and in the absence of an Federal Equivalent Method (FEM) or Federal Reference Method (FRM) in the county, the ability to validate sensor measurements against regulatory-grade instrumentation was not possible. The NOAA’s HMS smoke product used in this study doesn’t necessarilyconfirmground-levelsmokepresence.However, inthe absence of other smoke or fire data, this product was instrumental in our understandingofwildfireimpactsonPM2.5.Thedatawerealsonotcollectedover the year; a year-long measurement in future studies would provide additional insights into temporal trends in concentrations, the role of seasons, and sources. The study is also limited to PM2.5 mass concentrations only. Chemical speciation, tracer-based source apportionment, or vertically resolved aerosol profile measurements could have provided more quantitative attribution to wildfire smoke. Future studies can incorporate these measurements to help further investigate the relative contributions of wildfire smoke and other regional emission sources in this region. Indoor PM2.5 measurements were not conducted as part of this study; they would have quantifiedthe impactof these wildfire-related smoke exposures inthe schools’ indoor environments. 

Despite these limitations, we believe that the current work contributes to the ever-growing field of air quality literature, especially in an understudied region such as Starr County. This study was conducted in the outdoor microenvironment of school campuses. Based on our findings, the school authorities can implement wildfire response plans by modifying outdoor activities during affected periods to reduce children’s exposure. Pollutant concentrations at the intra-urban level are impacted by the local topography, natural barriers, vegetation, tree cover, built environment, and point and area sources of air pollution<sup>37</sup> . School authorities have incorporated greening of areas surrounding the schools to minimize the pollution exposure burden as reported in many research studies<sup>38–40</sup> . However, plant species for greening initiatives must be chosen carefully, as those that emit high levels of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and release large amounts of pollen may inadvertentlydeteriorate air quality and aggravate symptoms of wheezing and asthma in schoolgoing children<sup>39,41</sup> . 

## Methods 

### Instrumentation and sampling design 

A low-cost air sensor TSI BlueSky<sup>TM</sup> (Shoreview, MN, USA), model 8143, was used in this study to measure ambient PM2.5 concentrations. These sensors are primarily operated with Sensirion SPS30 PM to provide PM data and have been used in multiple studies to understand PM2.5 concentrations in various areas<sup>42–45</sup> . The instruments were deployed at all schools on November 15th 2023, and collected data till April 08th 2024, for a total of 146 days. The sensors were installed simultaneously at all the study locations during the study period. Figure S7 shows pictures of the installations at each school. 

### Data preprocessing for quality assurance 

The data collected from the TSI 8143 sensors was preprocessed to remove values outside the operational range. As per the TSI BlueSky sensor specifications, all the values with temperatures less than 40 °C, relative humidity less than zero and PM2.5 concentration greater than 1000 μg/m<sup>3</sup> were removed.MultiplestudiesreportedthatPMmeasurementsbyoptical-based air sensors are affected by factors such as temperature, relative humidity (RH) and PM size fractions<sup>46–49</sup> . To ensure the precision and accuracy of the data collected by these sensors, collocation amongst the sensors and with a reference method instrument is a widely adopted practice<sup>50</sup> . A relationship between the meteorological variables and the PM measurements is then established, aiding in the development of a correction equation. In the absence of a FEM or FRM monitor for collocations, research-grade instruments such as TSI DustTrak II 8533<sup>51</sup> and TSI DustTrak<sup>TM</sup> DRX Aerosol Monitor 8534<sup>27</sup> are used. ### Trajectory-based analysis and statistical analysis 

Conditional Probability Function, Nonparametric Wind Regression, and PSCF are being increasingly used to understand the source attributions 27,57–59. Trajectory-based analysis was conducted in this study to assess the impact of long- and near-range sources on pollutant concentrations. The Hybrid Single-Particle Lagrangian Integrated Trajectory (HYSPLIT) model was used to generate backward air-mass trajectories for the sites, daily from November 15, 2023, to April 08 2024, at 6-h intervals. The trajectories were generated using the R package “splitr”<sup>60</sup> , with the function hysplit_trajectory(·), at an arrival height of 500 m above ground level. The trajectories were then combined with ambient PM2.5 measurements to identify the contribution of high-concentration back trajectories and to conduct PSCF and CWT analyzes. PSCF is a trajectory transport pathwaybased analysis and was used to identify sources in multiple studies<sup>61–63</sup> . It calculates the probability of a source located at latitude i and longitude j, using the following Eq. 5:

### Smoke and fire products 

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA)’s widely used Hazard Mapping System (HMS) was used in this study. These data are producedfrommultiple polarand geostationarysatellite observations<sup>67</sup> and 

are available at a daily resolution at https://www.ospo.noaa.gov/products/ land/hms.html#data. There are several limitations to these products, including their inability to detect small agricultural fires, the lack of informationonplumesources,anddensecloudcoverthatimpactstheproduct<sup>68</sup> .
