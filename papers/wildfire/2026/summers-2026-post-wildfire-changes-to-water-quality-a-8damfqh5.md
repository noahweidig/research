# Post-wildfire changes to water quality: a review with implications for harmful algal blooms and reservoir resiliency

## Abstract

Abstract Wildfires wreak havoc on both urban and natural ecosystems and are expected to worsen under future climate conditions. Wildfires have the potential for long-term impacts on the health of downstream reservoirs. This study seeks to compile current literature worldwide regarding impacts of wildfires on reservoir water quality, with a special focus on the implications for harmful algal blooms. The overarching purpose was to fully understand wildfire impact on aquatic health and identify key gaps in research that are needed for accurate management of reservoirs. Due to changes in watershed conditions post-wildfire (mainly temperature, light, and nutrient dynamics), burned waterbodies are more susceptible to blooms in the months to years following a wildfire, with forested areas having recorded algae in burned waterways as high as 5–71× than that of unburned and simulated forested reservoirs showing up to 3× the peak chlorophyll-a values as their simulated unburned counterparts. Changes in algae are mainly attributed to changes to soil properties, increased nutrient loads, and increased light availability and temperatures caused by burning of surrounding vegetation. Factors that influence algae can be short-term, such as light limitation from smoke and large influxes of nutrients and metals, or longer-term, such as changes to canopy cover, legacy nutrients in sediments, surrounding runoff, and sediment chemistry. These external changes can influence harmful algae blooms (HABs) through alteration of bloom timing, extent, and community composition. There is still a need for longer-term studies of nutrient and algal dynamics, as most studies are limited to monitoring algae immediately following a wildfire. It is essential that reservoir managers anticipate how aquatic ecosystems are likely to respond to higher frequency and severity of wildfires and how their management strategies will need to be adapted.

In addition to immediate effects, fire has substantial long-term impacts on forest ecology and functionality, affecting nutrient turnover, hydrophobicity of soils, ecological biodiversity, and can even result in conversion of forests to shrublands (Agbeshie et al. 2022). These changes have profound and prolonged impacts on water quality in downstream reservoirs. Wildfires dramatically change the structure of surrounding watersheds, vegetation, and soil conditions, altering processes that control streamflow, soil erosion, nutrient export, and downstream water chemistry (Hohner et al. 2019; Ice et al. 2004). Changes to fish habitats often result in reductions in fish populations, changes to biodiversity, and even shifts in community composition (Burton 2005). 

Post-wildfire reservoir conditions (via alterations to temperature, light, and nutrients) are often (but not always) more conducive for harmful algal growth (Summers et al. 2026). Harmful algal blooms are typically defined as algae in excess (eutrophic to hypereutrophic), with the ability to alter habitat, nutrient cycling, and productivity of a water body. The phenomenon of fire induced or enhanced algae growth has recently been termed pyroeutrophication (Waters et al. 2023) and the primary pathways in which drivers of reservoir algae productivity are potentially altered following a wildfire are described in Fig. 1 and discussed in the sections 

below. HABs can additionally be defined as phytoplankton communities (diatoms, green and golden algae, and cyanobacteria) that express toxins. Nutrient-rich ash can also fuel rapid phytoplankton growth and shifts toward cyanobacterial dominance, potentially producing toxins such as microcystin (liver toxin), anatoxins and saxitoxins (neurotoxins), and cylindrospermopsins (genotoxins) which have the potential to persist into downstream drinking water sources (Hohner et al. 2019; Klose et al. 2015). 

Wildfire hydrologic impacts are typically estimated on a more local, site-specific basis (Tshering et al. 2023; Wine et al. 2018). Fewer works exist on understanding the overall impacts of wildfires on aquatic systems as a whole (McCullough et al. 2019; Smith et al. 2011), and even fewer works address the potential short-term and long-term impacts on harmful algal blooms (Paul et al. 2022). Models suggest that climate will only become more conducive to increasingly frequent and severe wildfires (Di Virgilio 2019; Davis et al. 2017; West et al. 2016), leading to even more necessity to understand the longterm trajectory of the hydrologic, physical, and biogeochemical impacts on aquatic systems and their resiliency. 

As the occurrence of wildfires and subsequent effects on water resources become more prevalent, it is essential that we understand the full extent of their impact.

Changes induced by wildfires in the surrounding watershed have the potential to persist for decades, which could have catastrophic effects on aquatic ecosystems, harmful algae dynamics, fish populations, and even human health. As reservoirs have a longer water residence time than streams and rivers, fire effects on their ecosystems could be further prolonged than for other freshwater systems. This makes it vital that reservoir managers anticipate how aquatic ecosystems are likely to respond and how their management strategies will need to be adapted (both in the short-term and long-term). In this literature review, we seek to combine the current knowledge that exists regarding the impact of wildfiredriven changes on watershed and reservoir processes in order to link these changes to alterations in harmful algal blooms and reservoir resilience, as well as identify key gaps that may be crucial to preserving water quality. The focal point of this study is to look at post-wildfire behavior through a management lens, with special emphasis on potential changes to harmful algal blooms, as well as concern for aquatic biota and human health. 

#### **Methods** 

Literature was collected from Zotero (via previously compiled wildfire papers relevant to USACE work) and Google Scholar (until June 23, 2026). The following are the major terms used in our initial search (January 2026–April 2026): (wildfire) AND (water quality OR algal blooms OR reservoir recovery). Deeper dives were done into individual factors influencing algae using the terms: (wildfire) AND (lakes OR reservoirs OR water quality) AND (algae OR HABs) AND (Nutrients OR organic matter OR dissolved oxygen OR metals OR temperature OR light OR vegetation cover OR sediment OR suspended solids). The selection process was carried out primarily by one investigator, with additional key papers supplied by two other investigators. Disparities between investigators were settled by consensus. We also checked the reference lists of all key articles for any additional eligible papers. When it was determined that many papers were from more recent years, additional literature review was carried out, restricting the years to pre-2000. 

### **Wildfire alterations to water quality in rivers** 

### **and streams** 

Behavior of tributary rivers and streams can have substantial impact on water chemistry within reservoir systems (Zhang et al. 2022a, b; Wang et al. 2020). Tributaries that flow through burnt soil can introduce additional nutrient loads, particulate organic matter, and dissolved organic matter to reservoirs, which can alter reservoir chemistry and community composition. Fluctuations of oxygen and metals in burned tributaries could impair 

the health of both riverine and reservoir fish. Changes in riverine dynamics have been shown to prompt cyanobacterial blooms in downstream reservoirs (Bowling et al. 2013; Vidal et al. 2012). As such, exploration of likely changes in riverine water quality following post-wildfire is essential to determine the full impact on reservoir water quality and the potential to induce a harmful algal bloom. 

#### **Nutrients** 

As many reservoirs are often classified as phosphoruslimited or nitrogen-limited, with algae responding to nutrient pulses, it is important to understand how wildfires affect the nutrient dynamics in tributaries and the surrounding watershed. Nutrient inputs are typically altered by wildfires in two main ways: aerial ash deposition (short-term) and changes in surrounding sediment properties (longer-term). 

Wildfire ash composition varies based on temperature of the burn, duration of the burn, and ecosystem specifics (Harper et al. 2019; Santín et al. 2015; Bodí et al. 2014; Pereira et al. 2012). Most forms of ash are composed of a variation of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium, potassium, aluminum, magnesium, and iron. The main nutrients of concern for harmful algal blooms are typically phosphate, nitrate, or ammonium. Each of these nutrients have been well documented as increasing in riverine load following a wildfire (Nitzsche et al. 2024; Paul et al. 2022; Sherson et al. 2015; Smith et al. 2011). Ash can be transported aerially (via downwind smoke deposition) or hydrologically (via rains washing ash debris into nearby waterways). 

The immediate increase in nutrients in rivers following a fire is primarily attributed to aerial input from smoke and ash (Earl and Blinn 2003). Concentrations of phosphorus and nitrogen were reported at an increase of 5- to 60-fold in the North Fork of the Flathead River in Glacier National Park, attributed to aerial deposition from smoke and ash (Spencer et al. 2003). These effects are typically short-term, with nutrients sourced from aerial ash returning to background concentrations in a span of weeks to months (Earl and Blinn 2003; Spencer et al. 2003). It is also important to note that smoke has the potential to travel hundreds to thousands of kilometers, impacting the water quality of unburned watersheds and introducing additional nutrient loads and toxic compounds to further waterbodies (Farruggia et al. 2024). 

In addition to nutrients deposited aerially, nutrients (particularly phosphate) also enter rivers and streams due to changes in surrounding soil properties, increased erosion (An et al. 2025; McGuire et al. 2024), and via rainwashed fine ash debris (Earl and Blinn 2003). Fire has been found to increase the available phosphorus (Cheng

Excess nutrient inputs are one of the biggest contributors for prompting an initial or worsening of a preexisting harmful algal bloom (Summers and Ryder 2023). This makes it essential to understand the implications of nutrients produced by wildfires entering rivers and streams. Systems with shallower depth showed the highest sensitivity to harmful blooms following a wildfire (Olson et al. 2023). 

Nutrient dynamics in the surrounding burned watershed could further alter nutrient loads entering reservoirs from their tributaries for months to years. Consistent, long-term load of phosphate from rivers into reservoirs has shown strong influence on algae growth, as well as potential to be stored in reservoir sediments, further amplifying nutrient accumulation in reservoirs (Summers et al. 2026). Changes in the dominant nutrient entering a system also could impact the type of algae that grows. Influxes of nitrate and nitrite following a wildfires and subsequent floods in Australia caused a species shift from the typical _Nodularia_ bloom to _Synechococcus_ sp. (Cook and Holland 2012). A spatiotemporal analysis examining algal response as a function of lake proximity and lag since fire showed that California lakes within 6 km of fires were initially inhibited by turbidity but later experienced algal blooms while lakes 6–10 km away from fires experienced more immediate blooms (Coker et al. 2026). fire induced changes in bloom drivers (temperature, light, and nutrients) and water quality outcomes. A simulated forested reservoir experienced up to a 3× increase in peak chlorophyll-a as well as an extended period of elevated chlorophyl-a levels following fire. Resolving the trends observed from these studies into guidance that can be applied to individual lakes and reservoirs is essential to understanding and managing local short-term and long-term water quality response. 

#### **Particulate and dissolved organic matter** 

Particulate and dissolved organic matter (POM/DOM) play key roles in light availability and nutrient cycling within reservoirs; thus, it is important to understand how changes to watershed and riverine POM/DOM may change following a wildfire. Particulate organic matter is typically sourced from vegetative surface wash-off, soil weathering, and plant residues (Li et al. 2025). Rivers that travel through burned areas have shown elevation of POM, typically ranging from 3 to 4× that of unburnt levels (Peter-Contesse et al. 2024; Brown et al. 2015, 2013; Ramchunder et al. 2011). Burn severity may have less impact on changes in POM in comparison to other variables, with POM export correlated with hydrologic factors (mainly discharge rate) (Barton et al. 2024) and climate/ ecosystem type showing a correlation with changes in POM composition in soil (Peter-Contesse et al. 2024). 

Dissolved organic matter is typically sourced from vegetative carbon sinks, rainfall wash-off, and organic matter decomposition (Li et al. 2025). Changes in DOM concentration in rivers following a wildfire are inconsistent (Tshering et al. 2023), with some studies indicating an increase (Uzun et al. 2020; Cawley et al. 2018; Revchuk et al. 2014), while other studies show a decrease (Wei et al. 2021; Rodriguez-Cardona et al. 2020). Wildfires also have been known to alter the chemical composition of terrestrially derived DOM exported from burned watersheds to rivers and streams (Uzun et al. 2020; Hohner et al. 2016; Revchuk and Suffet 2014). Chemical composition changes to note include increases in aromatic DOM (Wampler et al. 2024), increases in proportions of fulvic-like, tryptophan-like, and tyrosine-like compounds (Uzun et al. 2020), decreases in bioavailability (Matula et al. 2025) and increases in abundance of black carbon (Matula et al. 2025). Wampler et al. (2024) indicated that the extent of alteration of chemical composition of DOM correlates with burn severity, with more severe fires leading to more chemical composition change. Changes in DOM can vary widely between sites. The length of time that changes in DOM persist in waterbodies has been reported up to 16 years post-fire (Tshering et al. 2023), while some have seen a return to background levels in under a year (Uzun et al. 2020).

Excess influx of DOM load from rivers directly impacts light availability, reducing primary production in lakes (Thrane et al. 2014). Loss of light can lead to large dieoffs of algae and plants, which can introduce additional nutrients via decomposition (Ayala‐Borda et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2023; Saini et al. 2022). These excess nutrients have the potential to create a larger bloom once light conditions become more favorable. Additionally, degradation of organic matter can release harmful algal toxins and consume dissolved oxygen which is detrimental to fish and other aquatic organisms’ health (Oh et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2023; Rolton et al. 2022). 

Changes to the chemical composition of DOM/POM in the burned watershed are likely to influence algal dynamics after a wildfire. Compositions of sedimentderived and soil-derived DOM have been known to influence community composition, allowing certain species of algae to thrive while inhibiting growth of others (Xiang et al. 2023). This means that any alterations of surrounding DOM caused by a wildfire have the potential to result in a shift of algal species dominance. Changes in POM/ DOM composition also have shown the ability to prompt atypical, larger blooms. A study in the years following a wildfire in Australia found that anomalies in Chl-a followed peaks in black carbon detected entering the water (Tang et al. 2021). 

#### **Dissolved oxygen** 

Understanding wildfire effects on dissolved oxygen in rivers is important to grasp the overall picture of reservoir health, as dissolved oxygen has a strong impact on community composition and fish health. Dissolved oxygen concentrations decrease rather quickly during a wildfire, with reports of depleted dissolved oxygen as fast as 10 min following ash input (Curtis et al. 2025; Earl and Blinn 2003). However, these effects are typically shorterlived, returning to background levels within a few days (Curtis et al. 2025; Reale et al. 2021; Gresswell 1999). The short-term decreases in DO are attributed to decreases in primary production due to light limitation, direct POM/ DOM input, and changes in microbial response (Sanders et al. 2022; Reale et al. 2021; Marañón-Jiménez et al. 2013). The extent of dissolved oxygen depletion has been shown to correspond to the extent of burn severity in streams (Sanders et al. 2022). Mild to moderate wildfires typically result in slightly decreased dissolved oxygen levels in the range of a 10–20% reduction of background levels (Sanders et al. 2022; Earl and Blinn 2003), while more catastrophic fires have shown short-term sags of 0 mg/L (Curtis et al. 2025; Dahm et al. 2015). 

There is a strong relationship between algal growth and dissolved oxygen in lakes, which has led to DO often 

being used as an indicator of algal bloom occurrence or trophic state (Shi et al. 2025; Paulsson and Widerlund 2023; Coffin et al. 2018). Algae influence aquatic dissolved oxygen (DO) levels, with DO increasing during bloom initiation and decreasing following die-off (Hasan et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2021). However, changes in dissolved oxygen (such as those that occur during a wildfire) can also influence algal behavior. For instance, depletion of dissolved oxygen in aquatic systems can cause large die-offs of algae (often healthy species), which can lead to large-scale nutrient release that can be consumed by faster-growing harmful algae that can thrive in anoxic conditions (Zhu et al. 2013; Trimbee and Prepas 1988). Oxygen depletion can shift community composition of benthic species (Haas et al. 2014), as well as cause stress, abnormal growth, increase susceptibility to illness among fish species (Bulbul Ali and Mishra 2022), and lead to large fish kills (Hobbs and McDonald 2010; Thangaraja, et al. 2007; Townsend and Edwars 2003). In the year following an oxygen-induced fish kill, cyanobacteria density has been reported to increase, presumably due to the integration of additional nutrients released from fish decomposition (Schoenebeck et al. 2012). 

#### **Trace metals** 

It is important to consider how wildfires may impact the addition of metals into nearby waterways through runoff, erosion of sediments, atmospheric deposition, or release from existing sediments due to changing pH, oxygen, and temperature dynamics (Abraham et al. 2017). Eroded sediment and ash that enter rivers following a wildfire can contain abnormal levels of metals, widely varying based on land use, vegetation, underlying geology, and burn severity (Murphy et al. 2025; Shephard et al. 2025). A comprehensive literature review by Paul et al. 2022 demonstrated that 57% of streams sampled showed an increase in metal concentrations post-fire, 10% a decrease, and 33% had no changes. Higher severity fires have shown higher concentrations of trace metals in streams post-fire in comparison to smaller burns (Beyene et al. 2023; Rust et al. 2019b). Metals commonly found in wildfire ash are Mn, Fe, Zn, Pb, Cu, Al, Mg, Ca, and As (Harper et al. 2019; Cerrato et al. 2016; Pereira and Úbeda 2010). Pereira et al. 2012 showed that ash color is correlated with different metal types. Composition of metals in ash has been shown to vary with ecoregion (Harper et al. 2019) and geology (Lopez et al. 2025). Mn has shown significant correlation with fine-grained sediment and multiple elevations in the year post-fire, highlighting a relationship between sediment geochemistry and water quality (Tomaszewski et al. 2025).

transport (Tomaszewski et al. 2025; Romero-Matos et al. 2023; Rust et al. 2019b). Additional risk to downstream water quality is introduced if mining operations occur within the same watershed, where the dispersion of mining waste combined with metal—rich ash could transport extremely elevated loads of metals to downstream areas (Murphy et al. 2025). Study of burned soil has shown that concentrations of metals decrease over time (Hrelja et al. 2022), indicating effects on the surrounding watershed may be shorter-term. There is still much research to be done to understand post-wildfire impacts on metals, which are often neglected in favor of nutrients or other variables of interest (Shephard et al. 2025). 

Certain metal concentrations (particularly iron, cobalt, copper, manganese, molybdenum, and zinc) have been known to affect nutrient dynamics as well as algal growth and cyanotoxin production (Facey et al. 2019). While smaller amounts of heavy metals (lead, copper, cadmium etc.) can promote algal growth, higher concentrations can damage cells (Xiao et al. 2023; Cavalletti et al. 2022; Nowicka 2022). Certain species of algae are sensitive to excess amounts of copper, nickel, and zinc, with most sensitivity shown to copper (Filová et al. 2021). Iron (as well as Fe and Al) has been known to impact phosphorus retention, changing more reactive forms of phosphorus to more insoluble forms (such as FePO₄) and reducing overall phosphorus availability (Martinengo et al. 2024; Sundman et al. 2016; Liptzin and Silver 2009), to an extent where addition of iron has been used as a treatment method for eutrophic lakes (Münch et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2020; Orihel et al. 2016). Some cyanobacteria (e.g., _Microcystis aeruginosa_ ) have shown a greater capacity to endure heavy metals such as cadmium in water than other algae species, leading to a potential for reduced community diversity in post-fire waters similar to what has been observed when metals are present from other sources (Li et al. 2022). Several studies have shown that iron and zinc that make their way to the ocean (where many algae can be iron-limited) can actually prompt blooms (Bunnell et al. 2025; Weis et al. 2022) as well as in large freshwater bodies such as the Great Lakes (Larson et al. 2023). Some metals act as trace nutrients because they are vital for fundamental biological functions of algae such as iron utilization in chlorophyll-a synthesis and nitrogen fixation (Manic et al. 2024; Larson et al. 2023). 

While inhibitory for algal growth, excess metals can also impair the health of other aquatic organisms (Chen et al. 2024; Brix et al. 2022; Bakker et al. 2016). Tissue samples taken of fish in burned tributaries have shown that aluminum, iron, nickel, and lead make their way into the tissues of benthic species (Rust et al. 2019a). Any metals that accumulate in fish may eventually make their 

way up the food chain, posing a threat to human health (Muneer et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2017). Additionally, metals are some of the primary regulated contaminants in drinking water (Shephard et al. 2025). 

### **Changes to temperature and light dynamics** 

Wildfires have large potential to change temperature and light dynamics in both the short-term and long-term. As algae are heavily dependent on water temperature and light, with different species having different optimal thresholds (Bialevich et al. 2022; Levin et al. 2021; Bucci et al. 2020; Etheridge and Roesler 2005), it is essential to fully understand how these contribute to altered algae growth. This will lead to better assessment of reservoir response both during the fire, and in the aftermath following. 

#### **Changes during a fire** 

While temperature and light changes during a fire are short-lived, there are often extreme fluctuations that can affect water quality and algal growth long after the wildfire has gone. Data collection during a fire itself is challenging and often dangerous, resulting in less data availability than post-fire (Sanders et al. 2022). Perhaps the most immediate effect seen in waterbodies during a wildfire is the increase in stream temperature (compared to unburnt), which has been recorded at + 4 °C (Sanders et al. 2022; Rhoades et al. 2011) and even as high as + 8 °C (Hitt 2003) in forested streams. Factors affecting immediate temperature change in waterbodies from wildfire include fire intensity and volume of water in the burned region (Beakes et al. 2014; Rieman and Clayton 1997). The largest increases in temperature are seen in the most severely burned areas (Sanders et al. 2022). 

It also has been recorded that wildfire smoke reduces available solar radiation, resulting in smaller (~0.5–1 °C) decreases in stream temperatures in certain areas, such as those further from the fire (Sanders et al. 2022; David et al. 2018). During wildfires, smoke plumes lead to reductions of UV and visible solar radiation (Scordo et al. 2021; Williamson et al. 2016; Tzanis et al. 2009). Particles within these plumes (and subsequently deposited into the waterbodies) absorb and/or scatter light, decreasing the amount of radiation reaching the waterbodies (Bolan et al. 2025; Scordo et al. 2021). Clear lakes are likely the most sensitive to light attenuation caused by smoke plumes (Caroni et al. 2024). Studies have shown that the quantity of visible light available during a wildfire is typically reduced to 30–55% of normal levels (Masoom et al. 2023; Scordo et al. 2022, 2021). It has also been recorded that solar photovoltaic production decreases under wildfire smoke conditions (Corwin et al. 2025; Kim and Gillingham 2025; Cai et al. 2023; Gilletly et al. 2023) and

Toxic algae often thrive at higher temperature levels (Meng et al. 2024; Gobler 2020; Davis et al. 2009; Castro et al. 2004), meaning any increases in temperature of tributaries could pose harm to aquatic life and downstream drinking water. However, decreases in light availability during wildfires could prohibit, or at least delay, larger algal blooms, despite rising temperatures and excess nutrient inputs (Summers et al. 2026; Caroni et al. 2024; Ranalli 2004). Co-limitation between nutrients and light heavily influences both algal growth and overall water quality with light limitation (via artificial mixing, riparian shading, etc.) often used as a more feasible method of controlling algae (Summers and Ryder 2023). 

#### **Changes following a fire** 

In addition to the short-term effects discussed above, wildfire causes longer-term changes to temperature levels and light availability that can impact reservoir dynamics on a larger, even more permanent scale. Longer-term changes are heavily influenced by ecosystem type, burn severity, stream channel reorganization, volume of burned riparian vegetation and the subsequent increase in solar radiation (Beakes et al. 2014). Fires are one of the biggest drivers of vegetation composition (Barhoumi et al. 2021). Lower intensity fires reduce the understory, promote growth, and lead to a quicker recovery (Nolan et al. 2021; Midgley et al. 2011). Higher severity fires reduce dense vegetation, thin canopies, and scorch soil, leading to a much slower recovery, especially in boreal forests (Lv et al. 2025; Busby et al. 2020). 

It is important to note the influence of ecoregion when discussing wildfire impact on vegetation. While most areas typically recover to pre-fire vegetation conditions within 2 years (Xu et al. 2024; Maxwald et al. 2022), forested areas can take closer to 10 years to recover vegetative cover (Hao et al. 2022) and regrowth to a mature forest can take decades. In extreme cases, such as in Michigan, formerly forested sites have remained shrubdominated for more than a century after stand-replacing fires (Dickmann 2009). Removal of slow-growing vegetation in forested areas may result in accelerated thawing of permafrost and subsequent activation of its stored organic matter (further increasing nutrients available to enter aquatic ecosystems), long-term changes to lake temperature and light availability, reduction of nesting grounds for waterfowl, and favor of aquatic generalist ecology (Lewis et al. 2014). Additionally, hotter fire temperatures, such as those induced by large-scale forest fires, have the potential to sterilize soil, preventing 

seedling growth and further delaying recovery (Ibáñez et al. 2022). While other ecoregions (especially those with fast-growing vegetation) may recover quickly, it is likely lakes within forested areas will see much longerterm habitat changes, especially pertaining to light and temperature. These are especially important to consider as fire suppression over the past century has altered fire regimes in forested areas, and the likelihood of large stand-replacing fires has increased as a result (Gresswell 1999). 

Burning of surrounding vegetation (particularly loss of canopy cover) leads to more light availability following a wildfire (Cooper et al. 2015; Bladon et al. 2014; Silins et al. 2014). This loss of shading additionally elevates water temperature in burned areas, leading to increases (typically + 0.6 °C in comparison to unburnt) in the years after a wildfire (Beyene et al. 2023; Beakes et al. 2014; Dunham et al. 2007). Changes in temperature are highly site specific and dependent on topography (Chen and Chang 2023). 

In the year following a wildfire, algae have been reported as highest in water bodies where vegetation burned and lowest in water bodies where the riparian canopy remained intact (Cooper et al. 2015; Klose et al. 2015). Greater light availability via burned riparian canopy can additionally accelerate growth of aquatic plants and benthic communities, which alters aquatic ecosystem structure and composition (Bladon et al. 2014). 

### **Changes in sediment properties** 

While sediment properties in the surrounding watershed have a less direct impact on algae than other factors, it is important to understand how burned soil will alter nutrient and flow dynamics to tributaries that eventually reach the reservoir. Changes in sediment properties induced by wildfires can be divided into two categories: (1) changes in chemical and biological composition, such as nutrients, metals, decayed organic matter, and carbon content, and (2) changes in physical properties, such as water retention, size, and erodibility. The extent of these changes is dependent on fire intensity, temperature duration, fuel type, and original soil characteristics (type, moisture) (Agbeshie et al. 2022). 

#### **Changes to sediment chemistry in the watershed** 

Changes to chemical composition of soil in the surrounding watershed influence sediments that enter rivers and eventually reservoirs. As algae are often limited by a certain nutrient, any changes of nutrient composition in surrounding soil are important to take note of. Fires typically result in increases in pH, basic cations, and nutrient availability (Agbeshie et al. 2022; Fachin et al. 2021). Most chemical changes are observed in the

Page 8 of 22 

topsoil (Thomaz et al. 2014). Fire severity can have a big impact on changes in chemical composition of soils (Jhariya and Singh 2021; Ibáñez et al. 2021). Slower, more intense fires often have detrimental impact on soil quality (Certini 2005), with recovery times of longer than 2 years (Raiesi and Pejman 2021). 

Loss of soil organic carbon typically starts around 200 °C, with complete combustion around 500 °C, and lower intensity fires showing little change (Badía et al. 2014; Caon et al. 2014). Concentrations of  Ca<sup>2+</sup> , Mg<sup>2+</sup> ,  K<sup>+</sup> , and  Na<sup>+</sup> increase with fire intensity (Chungu et al. 2020; Verma et al. 2019). These increases are often short-lived, due to high vaporization thresholds, erosion, leaching, and plant uptake (James et al. 2018; Caon et al. 2014; Maynard et al. 2014). More intense fires result in increases in pH (Hinojosa et al. 2021; Francos et al. 2019). This is primarily attributed to the addition of nutrient-rich, basic ash (Alcañiz et al. 2018). pH in soil is important to note, as at a higher pH, phosphorus is more readily available (Neary et al. 2005). 

Under fire conditions, organic phosphorus mineralizes to form available orthophosphate (Zhang and Biswas 2017). Burnt areas result in higher availability of phosphorus in topsoil in comparison to unburnt zones, both for low and high-intensity fires (Fernández-García et al. 2019; Moya et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2018; Alcañiz et al. 2016). Increases in phosphorus following a fire have been attributed to transformation of organic P to available P via mineralization, addition of P heavy ash onto the soil surface, lower P losses of topsoil during burning due to a high vaporization temperature point of 1400 °C (which is difficult to reach by fire, as opposed to nitrogen at 350 °C), and the formation of insoluble P in the presence of calcareous substances (Agbeshie et al. 2022; Olson and Jones 2022). As many aquatic systems can be phosphorus-limited (Dijkstra et al. 2020; Søndergaard et al. 2017; Schindler et al. 2016), this is important to monitor in the surrounding watershed. 

It has been reported that following fires, inorganic nitrogen  (NH4+ and  NO3−) is often mineralized  (NH4+–N) or nitrified  (NO3−–N), resulting in an increase in available nitrogen in the topsoil (Agbeshie et al. 2020; Verma et al. 2019; Goberna et al. 2012). Fires that exceed 200 °C (temperature for N volatilization) can deplete organic nitrogen in the soil (Caon et al. 2014). The effects of forest fires on nitrogen are contradictory, with much yet to be understood (Agbeshie et al. 2022). As many aquatic systems are often nitrogen-limited (Chaffin et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2022; Camarero and Catalan 2012), it is important to fully understand effects of fire on nitrogen dynamics in the surrounding watershed. 

#### **Changes to physical properties of soils** 

Changes in physical properties of soil in the surrounding watershed caused by wildfires have the potential to impact the flow rate and the amount of nutrients entering reservoirs, which could have substantial effect on water quality and algal dynamics. Soil texture, or particle size distribution, can be affected by higher severity fires. As the temperature threshold for decline in clay content (400 to 800 °C) is lower than that of silt and sand (1400 °C), clay particles are often more affected by fires (Agbeshie et al. 2022; Alcañiz et al. 2018; Neary et al. 2005). Many studies have reported a loss of clay and/or a gain in sand within their reservoirs following a wildfire (Moya et al. 2019; Heydari et al. 2017; Granged et al. 2011). Clay provides cation exchange capacity, working as a buffer, and any reduction in clay can further amplify changes in pH caused by wildfires. Additionally, clay is often more mobile within reservoirs, allowing sediment to be transported further down reservoir. Sediment composed of more silt and sand will be more likely to be deposited sooner in reservoirs and remain (along with potential pollutants) for a longer residence time. 

Wildfires also tend to increase soil water repellency, which can reduce water infiltration, and increase runoff and soil erosion (Paul et al. 2022; Weninger et al. 2019; Robichaud et al. 2016). These changes are influenced by soil texture, percent organic matter, depth of soil, and temperature of fire (Agbeshie et al. 2022). Changes in soil physical properties can influence the flow rate and chemistry of waterbodies. Riverine discharge rates have been reported to be 3–180% higher in the year following a wildfire, attributed to temporary water repellency of topsoil/reduced soil infiltration capacity, and reductions in transpiration and interception (due to vegetation loss) (Lopez et al. 2025; Grillakis and Voulgarakis 2025; Lane et al. 2006; Moody and Martin 2001; Scott 1993). Higher erosion and deposition in nearby watersheds can last for up to 3–10 years following a wildfire (Brogan et al. 2019; Shakesby and Doerr 2006). Changes in discharge rate are highly influenced by ecoregion, with those closer to the equator seeing the greatest increases (Grillakis and Voulgarakis 2025). 

Increased runoff can amplify the concentrations and loads of nutrients, pollutants, and sediments entering waterways following a wildfire (Follmi et al. 2022; Pelletier et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2022). Runoff and erosion can be further amplified should a precipitation event follow a wildfire (Brogan et al. 2019; Beeson et al. 2001). While increased sedimentation may initially cause light limitation for phytoplankton, excess nutrient loads could deteriorate water quality, as well as lead to a larger bloom once sediment settles out and light conditions are more favorable for algal growth (Summers et al. 2026).

#### **Influx of suspended solids** 

Suspended solids influx heavily influences turbidity in reservoirs, which in turn can impact type and number of algae present in a system. Following wildfires, rivers and reservoirs that flow through burned areas show elevated levels of suspended solids (Jumps et al. 2022; Paul et al. 2022; Smith et al. 2011). These solids are a combination of wildfire ash and increased sediment yield, due to changes in soil properties in the surrounding watershed (Paul et al. 2022). The amount is highly variable, ranging from 2–80× the base conditions (Murphy et al. 2012; Lane et al. 2006; Townsend and Douglas 2000), and has even been recorded as greater than 1000× (Smith et al. 2011) with lower increases in tropical savannahs and higher amounts in forested watersheds. Increases in suspended solids concentration are dependent on wildfire severity, land use properties, and surface hydrology (Raoelison et al. 2023). If a precipitation and runoff event follows a wildfire, these values will be higher (Uzun et al. 2020; Son et al. 2015; Murphy et al. 2012). The median recovery time for elevated sediments in burned areas has been reported as 5 years, with severely burned catchments taking as long as 14 years to return to normal levels (Paul et al. 2022). 

Increases in total suspended solids following a wildfire lead to increased turbidity and light limitation (Chen and Chang 2023; Lopez et al. 2025). In comparison to other environmental variables, especially in shallower systems, turbidity has shown high correlation to chlorophyll-a levels (Summers and Ryder 2024). In general, decreasing turbidity leads to a lower standing crop of algae (Knight et al. 1984). However, studies have also indicated that a decrease in lake transparency, especially when temperatures are elevated, can lead to dominance of harmful cyanobacteria over beneficial diatoms (Zhang et al. 2022a, b; Brasil et al. 2016). Some cyanobacteria taxa migrate vertically in the water column in response to suspended solid levels and light availability (Gu et al. 2022). Influxes of suspended solids can clog fish gills and lead to large-scale fish kills (Karmakar and Roy 2022; Rust et al. 2019a) as well as smother marine invertebrates (Knight et al. 1984). 

#### **Changes in aquatic sediments** 

Pollutants leached from sediments after a wildfire eventually make their way to rivers and lakes and can substantially impact water quality downstream (Cawley et al. 2018). Changes to aquatic sediments are typically due to a combination of increased sediment delivery, changes to vegetation, and direct ash deposition (Farruggia et al. 2024; Hebda et al. 2024; Pelletier et al. 2022; Pompeani et al. 2020). Downstream sites are likely to be affected more heavily, with greater influxes of nitrogen and 

carbon via sediments (Cawley et al. 2018). Smaller tributaries are more vulnerable to influxes of burned material than larger rivers (Kieta et al. 2023). 

Lakes and reservoirs often have longer residence times for their sediments, making them ideal to preserve environmental history (Hebda et al. 2024). Lakes have shown changes to sediments for two to three decades following a wildfire (Pelletier et al. 2022; Pompeani et al. 2020; Dunnette et al. 2014), indicating shorter-term changes to watershed sediment properties could be further amplified for longer periods in lakes. Impacts on sediment records have also shown correlation with fire severity, with high severity fires having a recovery time of a century or more (Hebda et al. 2024). Fire derived sediment nutrient enrichment has been shown to be harder to detect in eutrophic lakes than mesotrophic or oligotrophic lakes (De Palma-Dow 2022). 

Fires are followed by an increase in sulfur, nitrogen, and carbon concentrations in lake sediments (Pompeani et al. 2020). Metals commonly associated with ash deposition into lake sediments post-wildfire include Al, Fe, Mg, and Mn, and metals associated with increased sediment flux include Sb, As, Ni, Ba, Mn, Mo, and Sr (Pelletier et al. 2020). Elevated levels of Pb, Hg, and Cd have been reported in lakes as long as 20 years after a wildfire (Pelletier et al. 2022). Phosphorus levels in lake sediments range from minimal increases to doubling following a wildfire (Farruggia et al. 2024) and may be complicated by interactions with new influxes of metals forming insoluble complexes (e.g.,  FePO4). In addition to changes in the accumulation and storage of nutrients and metals in sediments, post-fire disturbance of water column temperature, pH, alkalinity, salinity, or redox condition can induce release of previously accumulated substrates such as phosphorus or heavy metals. This internal loading can alter reservoir water quality even if tributary loading of nutrients, metals, ash, or organic materials from the surrounding watershed is not significantly changed (Zhao et al. 2024; Frémion et al. 2017; Håkansson et al. 1989). 

Data regarding post wildfire impacts on water quality and lake sediments is rather limited and there is a need for longer-term monitoring of impacts on aquatic systems (Summers et al. 2026; De Palma-Dow 2022). Nutrients that accumulate in sediments during a wildfire period have the potential to impact water quality and algae for years (Summers et al. 2026; Emelko et al. 2015). Nutrients and metals are continuously transported to lake bottoms via sedimentation and are often recycled to the water column at a steady rate (Forsberg 1989). However, nutrient release occurs at a much more rapid rate if bottom oxygen conditions reach hypoxic or anoxic levels, resulting in high levels of stored nutrient release to the water column above (Defeo et al. 2024; Nowlin 

Summers _et al. Fire Ecology          (2026) 22:162_ 

Page 10 of 22 

et al. 2005; Chiaro and Burke 1980). These rapid nutrient releases have shown correlation with increases in Chl-a and phycocyanin levels in reservoirs (Yin et al. 2023; Zou et al. 2020). 

### **Overall implications for reservoir water quality** 

As climate change, urban and rural development, and other processes transform landscapes and make the occurrence of wildfires and subsequent effects on water resources more prevalent, it is essential that we understand the full extent of their impacts. As discussed in the sections above, changes induced by wildfires in the surrounding watershed can persist for decades. These changes can have catastrophic effects on aquatic ecosystems, harmful algae dynamics, fish populations, and even human health. As reservoirs have a longer water residence time than streams and rivers, fire effects on their ecosystems have the potential to be further prolonged than for other freshwater systems. 

#### **Changes to harmful algal blooms** 

Harmful algal blooms can degrade aquatic ecosystems (via oxygen depletion, light limitation, and food web disruption), interfere with the local economy and recreation, and often pose a threat to human health. Harmful algal blooms in reservoirs are sensitive to external changes in the surrounding watershed, which are often outside the 

control of reservoir operators. Accurate understanding of these external changes caused by wildfires is essential for planning both preventative and adaptive management strategies within the reservoir itself. A summary of individual variables regarding the soil, inflow, and reservoir that could influence algal dynamics post-wildfire is laid out in Fig. 2. The seasonally dynamic competition between diatoms, green and golden algae, and cyanobacteria for light, nutrients, carbon, minerals such as silica, and trace essential nutrients such as metals can be disrupted by any of the individual variables. 

It is important to think of how wildfires affect algae in the context of two different timeframes: short-term and long-term. First off, short-term changes, associated with the wildfire itself and the period immediately following include large nutrient and metal influxes (through both TSS influx and direct ash deposition), as well as shortterm light limitation. Remote sensing has shown occurrence of harmful cyanobacteria has increased in the week following wildfire smoke occurrence above a lake (Olson et al. 2023). Increases in surface chlorophyll-a concentrations have been shown to correlate with increases in smoke density (Olson et al. 2026). If a storm event follows a wildfire, algal biomass can be further elevated (Klose et al. 2015). Aquatic systems that are nutrient-limited are likely to show the most sensitivity to external pulses of nutrients and/or metals. For instance, phytoplankton

Shifts to different types of algae have been observed during wildfires (Capone et al. 2026; Scordo et al. 2021), which is primarily attributed to light availability. Light limitation has also shown the potential to hinder (Scordo et al. 2021) or delay an expected bloom until light conditions become more favorable to algal growth (Summers et al. 2026). It is important to note that if a fire occurs during conditions that are not favorable to algal growth, effects from the fire may not be seen until the following spring (Summers et al. 2026; Spencer et al. 2003). 

This leads us to consider how overall changes to the watershed will impact algal dynamics in the longer-term. This includes changes to canopy cover, legacy nutrients stored in aquatic sediments, and changes in surrounding runoff and sediment chemistry. Less is known about these effects, as many studies focus on shorter-term changes. Streams where riparian vegetation has been severely burned show greater algal biomass than systems where vegetation is unaffected (Klose et al. 2015). This has been shown to still persist a year after the wildfire (Cooper et al. 2015). As discussed above, inflow and sediment nutrients are often altered for decades following a wildfire, which will likely alter algal response for decades as well. Increases in carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus have been recorded in aquatic sediments after fire occurrence (Waters et al. 2023). Phosphorus concentrations can become elevated during stratification, hypoxic conditions, and subsequent legacy nutrient release (De PalmaDow et al. 2022). If phosphorus initially released during a wildfire is stored in aquatic sediments, it has the potential to be released and affect algae years down the line. Annual algal productivity was found to be 5–71 times higher in rivers in burned watersheds than in unburned watersheds, persisting for 5 years after a fire in a forested area (Silins et al. 2014). This was primarily attributed to slow recovery of the watershed to excess phosphorus loading. Changes to surrounding soil properties and erosion rate can lead to further sedimentation and amplification of pollutant loads in the years following a fire. It is also important to note that fire can change the N:P ratio (typically lower) in sediments following a fire (Waters et al. 2023), which has been shown to alter phytoplankton dynamics, as well as the effectiveness of certain management strategies (Summers and Ryder 2023). 

A comprehensive review of post-wildfire water quality data in the Western United States by Brucker et al. 

(2025) provides strong evidence of multi-year water quality degradation following wildfires, with extent of degradation influenced by specific basin and wildfire features. When looking at a longer-term perspective regarding the equilibrium state of lakes, a stronger relationship emerges between nutrient concentrations and chlorophyll-a (Davidson et al. 2023). If wildfires are influencing watershed nutrient properties for years after a wildfire, they could have the potential to fully alter the permanent trophic state of a system. Consider, for example, a simple competition between various phytoplankton taxa (diatoms, green algae, and cyanobacteria) amid changing temperature, light, pH, and nutrient (N, P, C, trace element) conditions. If post-fire flood flows and ash loading increase turbidity without raising pH then diatoms may have the competitive advantage because of their adaptations for low light conditions. However, if pH is also elevated diatoms will be suppressed due to the high solubility of silica at high pH. Once pyroeutrophication begins, green algae and cyanobacteria begin to rapidly multiply and consume nutrients and carbon, which can perpetuate the higher pH condition by depleting  CO2 from the water column. Depletion of dissolved oxygen can induce anoxic conditions and release of heavy metals from sediments which may act as trace micronutrients, supporting growth of both green algae and cyanobacteria. However, while green algae may persist through lower light, higher pH, and lower  CO2 conditions, several factors could push the phytoplankton community to cyanobacterial dominance including development of a low N/P ratio through external or internal phosphorus loading and depletion of N from the water column, which would favor N-fixing cyanobacterial species over green algae. 

Since the complex interplay of preexisting water quality and water temperature, light, and nutrient changes in the water column can lead to a multitude of possible water quality outcomes for lakes and reservoirs impacted by fires, it may be difficult to anticipate those outcomes a priori. Comprehensive assessment of processes in the watershed, tributaries, reservoir water column, and sediment that includes monitoring of hydrologic patterns, dissolved and sediment constituent concentrations, and phytoplankton communities may be necessary to predict long-term water quality outcomes. Water quality models that enable competition between multiple phytoplankton groups may be necessary to capture short- and long-term dynamics.

#### **Further implications for aquatic and human health** 

One of the most important things to consider is whether post-wildfire influenced changes to lake dynamics will result in conditions that exceed the standards required for aquatic life and/or human safety in either the shortterm or long-term. Many rivers and reservoirs are home to endangered fish populations, used for recreational purposes, or used as a drinking water source for nearby areas. Changes to aquatic and human health are influenced directly by wildfires and can be further amplified by alterations to harmful algal bloom patterns. 

Post-wildfire periods often see a shift in community composition in lake ecosystems. Food webs in burned watersheds often shift to shorter life-cycle generalists (Paul et al. 2022). In systems where excessive algal growth occurs, there is often a shift to algal-based food resources. Increased algal production can lead to subsequent increases in benthic macroinvertebrate abundance and changes in size of some aquatic predators (Emelko et al. 2015). Certain algal species release harmful toxins into the water, which can be toxic to local fauna, as well as pets. Lakes often see an overall decline in both biomass and diversity of fish species. Fish populations decrease due to increased temperatures, influx of suspended solids, and hypoxia induced during algal die-off. 

As discussed in the “Dissolved oxygen” section, wildfires (as well as post-wildfire algal growth and subsequent die-off) can drastically reduce oxygen levels and lead to large fish kills. Decomposition of these fish kills can lead to excess nutrients with a reservoir, prompting further algal growth. Changes to fish populations can happen immediately following a fire, or in the first post-storm event (Rinne 1996). Several threatened and endangered fish, such as the Gila trout, have shown greater sensitivity to wildfire conditions (Dunham et al. 2003; Brown et al. 2001). Typical recovery time post-fire is 5 years for invertebrates and 5 to 10 years for fish species (Paul et al. 2022). Additionally, studies have shown an increase in mercury in several species of fish post-fire, including rainbow trout, brown trout, and European perch (Moreno et al. 2016; Kelly et al. 2006). Human consumption of fish high in mercury can lead to several neurological issues, especially among vulnerable individuals (children, pregnant women) thus investigation of suspected risks to recreational fishing or hatcheries may be an operational need following wildfires impacting reservoirs. 

Wildfires can substantially alter the hydrological response of watersheds, reducing rainfall infiltration into soils and increasing overland flow (Cannon and Gartner 2005). Debris flow that results from storms in burned areas can pose significant harm to infrastructure and natural resources, as well as severe damage to ecological habitats (De Graff 2014). Post-wildfire debris flow events have caused human injury, as well as fatalities (Santi and Macaulay 2021; Cannon and Gartner 2005). Debris flow and a large volume of eroded sediments can result in mass failure of sediment dams (McGuire, et al. 2017). Debris from these large flow events eventually makes its way to lakes and reservoirs, where it is often trapped (Wasklewicz et al. 2023). Debris flow can clog constructed debris basins, leading to millions of dollars spent on removal and disposal (De Graff 2014). 

As a large portion of communities source their water from forest catchments (Smith et al. 2011), where wildfires often occur, wildfires and subsequent algal blooms can have profound effects on drinking water supply. Water quality degradation following wildfires significantly challenges water treatment plant process performance and can even force shutdown (Hohner et al. 2019). A study of wildfires across the USA showed elevations of several contaminants regulated in the public drinking water systems downstream, including nitrate and arsenic (Pennino et al. 2022). As discussed in the “Trace metals” section, trace metals are often elevated following wildfire. Detection and treatment of these metals is essential to ensuring safe drinking water. #### **Current gaps and next steps** 

This literature review summarizes current research that exists regarding post-wildfire water quality and algal blooms. Wildfires have the potential to prompt an initial, short-term algal bloom, mainly due to rapid nutrient influx and changes to temperature and light dynamics. They also have the potential to change algal community dynamics, as harmful, fast-growing algae are more likely to respond to sudden changes in habitat. While the large initial bloom may be the most apparent, it is important to understand how changes to the surrounding watershed could make conditions more favorable to growth in the future. A simplified summary of potential post-fire changes is laid out in Table 1. Key post-fire changes to consider for longer-term management of harmful algal blooms include reduction in riparian cover (increasing temperature and light availability), excess sediment and nutrient loading, alterations to flow dynamics, and changes to in-reservoir sediment properties. These changes could result in alterations to water quality and algal dynamics for years after a fire has passed. 

Prevention, detection, and management (with the ultimate purpose of reducing the severity and frequency) of harmful algal blooms is a core goal of the (USACE) water resources development projects, as authorized in the 2024 Water Resources Development Act (https:// www. usace. army. mil/ Missi ons/ Civil- Works/ Proje ct- Plann ing/ Legis lative- Links/ wrda_ 2024/). As the factors above change, there’s a need to understand how reservoir water quality can respond, and how management strategies can be adapted. A lot is still unknown about what thresholds should be met (extent of burn intensity, duration of fire, etc.) to effect downstream water quality after a wildfire. Summers et al. (2026) demonstrated that a water quality model (CE-QUAL-W2) can mechanistically account for fire impacts on HABs if the factors described in the sections above are accurately assessed for the local watershed. This model and others (Delft3D, EFDC, WASP, etc.) have the ability to parameterize hydrologic and atmospheric conditions as well as tributary sediment and 

nutrient loads. However, most post-wildfire watershed modeling has centered only on the hydrologic impacts, and the challenges of new water quality data collection in the post-fire period are not trivial. Thus, although there are excellent watershed modeling tools available (HEC-HMS, SWAT, GSSHA, HSPF) they cannot yet be sufficiently parameterized to assess short-term and longterm water quality impacts to reservoirs from fires. Much is still inconclusive about impacts on reservoirs and their surrounding watersheds, especially on a longerterm basis. To predict the complex interactions of these processes in the future, further use of modeling may be necessary. 

It is essential to fully expand research to develop the mechanistic understanding of how reservoirs may respond to wildfires, especially given the probable increase in severity and prevalence of fires under future climate. Thus, it is important to identify what areas of research are less covered in the literature, and which understudied topics are most pertinent to predict water quality changes. We have identified several gaps where further study would greatly enhance our ability to understand and model reservoir water quality following fires because of their impacts on temperature, light, and nutrients as well as the phytoplankton community: 

- Long-term monitoring of phosphorus: Studies have shown that effects of phosphorus may be delayed for several months to years after a fire, indicating a need for monitoring not just immediately after a fire. Phosphorus is often stored in sediments, with further potential to be released under hypoxic conditions. Additionally, the dominant phosphorus form loaded to reservoirs may transition following fire, depending on the severity of burning and extent of post-fire watershed erosion; therefore, monitoring of multiple species beyond total phosphorus is desirable to fully capture the dynamics of post-fire nutrient availability. 

- Further understanding of post-fire dissolved organic matter trends: Changes to DOM are inconsistent in the literature, with some studies showing a decrease following fires and some showing an increase. Further study is needed to show how and why DOM is altered following a wildfire, especially given its role in the formation of DBPs. 

- Expansion into study of metals: Monitoring of metals often takes a backseat to nutrients in water quality modeling. Study of post-fire trace metals, especially those that pose a concern to drinking water or act as trace essential nutrients for phytoplankton, should be expanded. - Research on emerging pollutants: Less research exists regarding many emerging pollutants of concern. Microplastics, for example, have emerged as a significant research topic in the past few years and have the potential to leach under higher temperatures. There is a pressing need to develop understanding of recovery trajectories and guidance for reservoir management in the months to years following wildfire.
