# Flood-wildfire co-occurrence risks disrupt global staple food trade and erode SDG progress

## Abstract

Co-occurrence of floods and wildfires further complicates the assessment of their full impacts on crop production and trade. This study develops a framework linking co-occurrence risks, staple-crop production, trade, and Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) based on multi-source data. More than 242000 global co-occurrence risk events across nine levels were identified during 2002-2018, with the highest concentrations occurring in Central Africa and Mainland Southeast Asia. Over the 17-year period, approximately 53% of co-occurrence risk events occurred in staple-crop production areas. A 1% increase in co-occurrence risk was associated with production reductions of 3% in maize, 3% in rice, 2% in soybean, and 2% in wheat. These production losses further associated with projected global exports, with average reductions of 24314 kt for maize, 167969 kt for rice, 35653 kt for soybean, and 13936 kt for wheat, particularly exports from the United States and Thailand. At the global level, the overall SDG score decreased by an average of 3%, with 76% of countries located in the Global South. These SDG changes were reflected in deteriorating international relationships (SDGs 16 and 17, with a combined change of -0.62) and adverse impacts on marine ecosystems and gender equality (SDGs 14 and 5, with a combined change of -0.54). Our findings offer insights into the potential of natural disasters for global sustainability through a comprehensive framework.

Staple crops account for the lion’s share of global agricultural trade, underpinning food stability in both importing and exporting countries across the world<sup>1</sup> . These crops, including wheat, maize, soybean, and rice, are irreplaceable pillars of daily diets, meeting the nutrient needs of billions of people<sup>2</sup> . Bolstering trade resilience and supply stability for these staple crops is therefore critical to safeguarding global sustainability<sup>3</sup> . However, it is noteworthy that the primary cultivation zones for these staple crops (e.g., the North China Plain and the U.S. Midwest) are often concentrated in areas that are highly vulnerable to natural disasters, particularly frequent floods and destructive wildfires<sup>4,5</sup> . These climate-related hazards frequently disrupt planting and harvesting schedules, damage crop yields, and further impair the quality of produce available for trade<sup>6</sup> . Many of the above areas lack of sufficient infrastructure and adaptive measures to mitigate such impacts, and vulnerability varies by several orders of magnitude both between and within countries<sup>7</sup> . As climate change amplifies the frequency and severityof floodsand wildfires,thestabilityofstaplecroptradevolumes could face unprecedented strain due to the risks faced by these disasterprone areas<sup>8,9</sup> . 

Amid growing recognition of climate hazards to staple crop systems, two typical climate-driven risks floods and wildfires have received increasing attention. Their frequent expansion can significantly reduce the production of staple crops, especially in the case of low-frequency but highintensity floods<sup>10</sup> . Furthermore, exporters’ strategies may change in response to production shifts caused by these climate-driven risks<sup>11</sup> . However, although their isolated negative effects have been documented, the more complex effects of co-occurrence risks defined as the concurrent or sequential occurrence of floods and wildfires on staple crop production and global trade remain poorly understood<sup>12</sup> . Such co-occurrence risks are recognized as among the most harmful hazards to local biodiversity and human beings, and they may further reduce staple-crop production by shortening suitable cultivation periods and disrupting the water and temperature conditions required for crop growth and optimal yield<sup>13</sup> . However, theseco-occurrenceriskshavenotyetbeensystematicallyinvestigatedatthe global scale, limiting accurate assessments of production changes under such conditions and hindering analysis of their implications for future global trade.

More importantly, a growing number of countries increasingly leverage global agricultural trade as an efficient mechanism to alleviate domestic agricultural constraints, often prioritizing partnerships with geopolitically aligned trading partners<sup>11,14</sup> . Yet a large proportion of these global trade pathways are characterized by an unbalanced and unsustainable nexus, especially in terms of potential pressure transformation<sup>15</sup> , with importing countries exhibiting excessive reliance on the natural resource endowments and production capacity of a limited set of exporting areas<sup>11</sup> . Consequently, such import-dependent economies face heightened vulnerability to cascading disruptions when their key staple crop suppliers are affected by cooccurrence risks<sup>16</sup> . Given the foundational role of staple crops in advancing multiple targets across the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), these trade-mediated shocks can severely undermine progress toward national and global SDG agendas<sup>17,18</sup> . Despite the significance of these linkages, the precise magnitude of the impacts of co-occurrence risks in exporting areas, andhowtheyreverberatetoimpedeSDGattainmentinimportingcountries remains inadequately quantified and poorly characterized in the existing literature.

## Discussion 

Global South countries face increasing challenges from climate-related hazards and associated socioeconomic pressures<sup>21</sup> . Consistent with these concerns, our results show that more than 50% of identified flood-wildfire co-occurrence risks are associated with staple crop production. However, the impacts of these risks are highly heterogeneous across regions. Cooccurrence risks are particularly concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and MSEA, where many events occur in cropland areas and smallholderdominated agricultural systems<sup>22–24</sup> . These risks are associated with negative impacts on staple crop production, with reductions reaching up to 16% for maize and 14% for rice. Although these losses may represent a limited proportion of global agricultural markets, their local consequences could be substantial because rural households in vulnerable regions often depend heavily on staple crop production for their livelihoods. These findings indicate that compound climate hazards may amplify existing vulnerabilities in agricultural communities and challenge their recovery capacity after climate shocks<sup>25–27</sup> . 

Beyond local production impacts, our results demonstrate that climate-induced agricultural disruptions can propagate through international staple crop trade networks. Reductions in production caused by cooccurrence risks may affect the export capacities of major producing countries and alter trade flows among countries. For example, several sensitive trade pathways, including Zambia-Mozambique for maize and Pakistan-China for rice, show substantial export reductions under cooccurrence risk scenarios, with some pathways approaching complete loss of export volumes. These results highlight that climate risks can introduce additional uncertainties into global food trade systems, particularly where countries rely heavily on specific suppliers or trade relationships<sup>28,29</sup> . Furthermore, our SDGs assessment indicates that trade-mediated impacts are more evident for SDG 16 and SDG 17 than for global food availability indicators, suggesting that climate-related trade disruptions may influence international coordination and cooperation mechanisms. 

The combined effects on domestic production and international trade suggest that Global South countries may face compounded exposure to climate-related food system risks. Countries with limited adaptive capacity may experience simultaneous pressures from reduced local production and increased uncertainty in external food supplies. Therefore, adaptation strategies should consider both agricultural resilience and trade-system resilience. Strengthening climate adaptation measures in vulnerable cropproducing regions could reduce production losses associated with compound hazards. At the same time, diversifying import sources and strengthening regional food security cooperation may reduce dependence on specific trade pathways. However, these measures should complement rather than replace efforts to improve domestic agricultural capacity. In addition, because our results highlight the role of international cooperation 

in mitigating trade-mediated impacts, enhancing climate risk information sharing and collaborative management mechanisms could support more resilient food systems. The effectiveness of these strategies will depend on national economic conditions, trade structures, and adaptation capacities, which warrant further investigation<sup>30–35</sup> . 

In summary, our framework linking co-occurrence risks, staple crop production,trade,andSDGsprovidesanintegrated approachforevaluating the cascading impacts of compound climate hazards across spatial scales. Our results demonstrate that flood-wildfire co-occurrence risks are associating with global staple crop production and propagate through trade networks, with particularly pronounced implications for Global South regions.Addressingthesechallengesrequirescoordinatedeffortstoenhance agricultural resilience, diversify trade systems, strengthen regional cooperation, and improve climate risk monitoring and information sharing<sup>36–38</sup> . 

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the analysis period is restricted to 2002-2018, which may not fully capture recent changes in the frequency and intensity of compound flood and wildfire risks under ongoing climate change. In addition, due to data availability, major global events after 2018, such as COVID-19 and associated global supply chain adjustments, are not considered. Second, this study focuses on the direct relationship of compound risks with staple crop production, trade, and SDGs, while the potential mediating and moderating effects of local adaptation measures, policy interventions, and socioeconomic conditions remain insufficiently explored. Third, the trade assessment is based on existing production changes and observed trade relationships rather than on a dynamic trade equilibrium framework. Although this approach enables the identification of potentially vulnerable trade pathways, it does not explicitly incorporate adaptive responses from governments and markets, including trade agreements, export restrictions, strategic food reserves, market substitution, and international assistance mechanisms. Therefore, the estimated trade reductions should be interpreted as potential exposure under climate risk scenarios rather than as precise predictions of future trade flows. 

Future research could address these limitations by integrating more recent datasets and collaborating with multiple institutions to capture evolving compound disaster dynamics and post-2018 global changes. Incorporating detailed socioeconomic, policy, and adaptation-related information would help disentangle the complex interactions among climate risks, human responses, and sustainability outcomes, and support the development of region-specific adaptation strategies for improving staple crop and trade resilience. Furthermore, future studies could employ dynamic trade models, such as equilibrium or agent-based approaches, to better represent market adjustments and policy interventions in response to climate-induced disruptions. Expanding the analytical framework to include additional hazard factors (e.g., droughts, heat extremes, pests, and conflicts), agricultural drivers (e.g., irrigation and fertilizer inputs), and more comprehensive comparisons across countries, crops, climate zones, and years could further improve understanding of the mechanisms through which compound risks affect global food systems. 

In conclusion, this study develops an integrated framework linking flood-wildfire co-occurrence risks, staple crop production, international trade, and SDGs changes to evaluate the cascading impacts of compound climate hazards across spatial scales. Our results reveal that co-occurrence risks of floods and wildfires are widely distributed globally, with particularly high concentrations in Sub-Saharan Africa and MSEA. Importantly, more than half of the identified co-occurrence risks occur in staple crop production areas, indicating substantial exposure of global food systems to compound climate hazards. These risks are associated staple crop production, with reductions of up to 16% for maize and 14% for rice, although the magnitude varies substantially among crops and regions. 

Beyond local production losses, our results demonstrate that climateinduced agricultural disruptions can propagate through international trade networks. Several trade pathways show considerable reductions in export volumes under co-occurrence risk scenarios, particularly those involving countries with high dependence on specific trade partners. ## Methods 

The overall methods are made up of co-occurrence risks of floods and wildfires identification, evaluation on production, trade, and SDGs. The major workflow is shown in Fig. 4, What we did is as follows<sup>1</sup> . Using the UN Comtrade database (2003-2019, domestic exports and foreign imports), we focused on exporting countries and, for each, applied the same bootstrap resampling as in the production step to estimate the trade response to cooccurrence risk<sup>2</sup> . From that bootstrap we collected the export elasticity, which we now define explicitly as the percentagechange in a national export volume for a 1% change<sup>3</sup> . We then partitioned countries into three behavioral scenarios using the 33rd and 66th percentiles of the observed exportshare distribution (the share of additional/remaining output sent abroad). Theyaretherisk-appetite(RA),risk-prevention(RP),andrisk-neutral(RN) scenario<sup>4</sup> . Trade changes under each scenario are then computed from the UN Comtrade volumes using the scenario-specific export shares and the bootstrapped elasticity. 

### Co-occurrence risks of floods and wildfires identification 

Given the complexity of co-occurrence risks from floods and wildfires, we design a comprehensive framework for their identification. First, data collection and preparation were conducted. The flood event raster data (30 m resolution, global 2001–2018) and wildfire points (global 2001–2018) were collected from datasets generated based on observed flood and wildfire records. Flood duration and wildfire size were selected as the primary indicators for characterizing the respective hazards. For the assessment of co-occurrence risks (this is only used for drawing co-occurrence risks index 

figures like Fig. 1 instead of analysis), the sum of these two indicators is considered. Co-occurrence represents hazards occurring in the same year. 

Second, co-occurrence risks were identified. Given the data limitations associated with historical natural-disaster records, annual spatial overlaps between flood events and wildfires were identified. During the process of extracting relevant information, wildfire points were used as the primary spatial targets for associating flood events. Considering differences in accuracy between rasters and points, we collect flood events that overlap wildfire points to reduce potential information loss in the database between pixels and points. Furthermore, to perform a detailed analysis, cooccurrence risks were classified into three levels; low (L), medium (M), and high (H) for both flood duration and wildfire size, resulting in nine possible co-occurrence-risk types (e.g., L-H, representing low flood risk and high wildfire risk) according to the 33% and 66% quantiles of flood duration and wildfire size, respectively. For each country, the dominant cooccurrence-risk type was identified based on the category with the highest frequency of occurrence. 

Forthe33rdand66thpercentiles, first,thisquantile-basedsplitensures that each category contains an identical sample size, avoiding biased statistical comparisons caused by uneven observation counts across low, medium, and high hazard intensity groups. Second, equal-frequency thresholds eliminate subjective bias from arbitrarily fixed numerical cutoffs, offering a data-driven classification standard. Third, consistent three-tier percentile stratification unifies the grouping logic for both flood duration and wildfire magnitude, enabling fair pairwise analysis of all nine combined compound hazard intensity scenarios without inconsistent classification rules across different hazard metrics. The shared letters (L/M/H) are only shorthand for own distribution and do not imply that a flood and a wildfire carrying the same letter have equivalent absolute magnitude. 

The national-level production losses are calculated based on their respective elasticity. In detail, bootstrap method is conducted for different national elasticities, and these elasticities are used to calculate national-level production losses based on the national co-occurrence risks levels. It should be noted that this study adopts the calendar year as the temporal matching window to identify flood-fire overlapping pixels, without introducing intraannual temporal constraints to distinguish concurrent and sequential disaster occurrences because of limited temporal precision in the data. Therefore, future research can incorporate daily temporal information to constrain the time interval between floods and fires, which would help further distinguish truly interacting compound events from independent co-occurring risks. 

### Relationship evaluation on production and trade 

Therelationshipofsuchco-occurrencerisksof floodsandwildfiresonstaple crops (maize, rice, soybean, and wheat) production (total output) has been calculated as follow. We used pixels as the basic spatial units of analysis and estimated the mean changes in staple-crop production associated with cooccurrence risks. Specifically, production anomalies in risk-affected pixels were compared with a baseline using a bootstrap approach to reduce the potential influence of sampling variability and other unexplained sources of variation. National-level production losses were then estimated by aggregating the production changes associated with individual co-occurrencerisk events across pixels for each year from 2002 to 2018, thereby assessing the cumulative relationship of co-occurrence risks over the study period. First, following the nine categories mentioned above, we selected the overlapping pixels that contain both co-occurrence risk points and staple crop production<sup>39,40</sup> . 

Bootstrapping is adopted in this work to reliably estimate statistical relationships among multi-dimensional indicators, as this resampling techniquerelaxesstrictassumptionsondatadistributionanddeliversrobust confidence intervals for the quantifiedrisk-production-trade linkages. It has been commonly used in large number of studies including economic<sup>41</sup> , environment<sup>42</sup> , and society<sup>43</sup> . Compared with alternative analytical approaches including conventional parametric regression and structural equation modelling, bootstrapping offers greater flexibility for non-normal

Second, the further relationship of such co-occurrence risks are evaluatedusingstatisticalbootstrapping.Specifically,theseninecategoriesofcooccurrence risks are iterated 10,000 times with random samples (60% of all samples each time) to verify their significance at the 10% level<sup>16,41</sup> . when the significance test is passed (the 90% confidence interval excludes zero for real effects), the estimated effects of co-occurrence risks on production can be used.

When examining trade changes linking with production, we design different scenarios forfurther analysis usingvolume-scale data from the UN Comtrade database for 2003–2019. The trade data are restricted to domestic exports and foreign imports to eliminate potential effects of re-export and re-import. 

We take exporting countries as the focus and define three scenarios, which are risk-prevention (RP), risk-appetite (RA), and risk-neutral (RN))<sup>43</sup> . First, similar experiments bootstrap methods have been conducted, and the corresponding elasticities (% change) have been collected. Second, based on quantile methods for dividing scenarios<sup>44–46</sup> , each country’s 33% and 66% quantiles are calculated. These are then associated with the export share intervals for RA (95–100%, meaning that 95–100% of additional remaining output is allocated to exports), RP (25–85%), and RN (86–94%). Furthermore, to make the outcomes clear and accessible for readers without professional geographical or economic backgrounds, national food reserve policies and international assistance rules are not included, and these will be addressed in additional studies. 

These export share intervals are setto clearly differentiate conservative, speculative and balanced export behaviorsguided byestablished risktheory. The RA scenario (95–100% of additional production exported) describes a strategy in which nearly all incremental production is directed to international exports to pursue maximum trade-related economic returns. This choice prioritizes export revenue over domestic stockpiling, accepting greater domestic food-supply vulnerability to global market shocks, and corresponds to a risk-taking orientation. By contrast, the RP scenario (25–85%of additionalproductionexported)keepsa considerableportionof newly produced commodities within the domestic system rather than exporting them. Retaining these domestic surplus builds local food buffers, reduces dependence on volatile cross-border supply channels, and serves as a proactive measure to guard against food-security risks. The intermediate RN scenario (86–94% exported) balances export benefits and domestic risk mitigation, falling between the two strategies described above. This study does not rely on econometric regression to empirically estimate risk preference thresholds. Instead, the three classification scenarios are based on the classical theoretical trichotomy of risk attitudes widely adopted in agricultural trade economics. 

### Relationship evaluation on Sustainable Development Goals 

After examining the relationship on production and trade, the relationship on the SDGs is also considered. The basic framework is that the exporter helps importers SDGs through diversified agricultural trade, especially in four staple crops. However, although food especially staple crops are the 

foundationofallSDGs,itisdifficulttoknow theprecisefactorslinkingthese four staple crops to the 17 SDGs. 

Therefore, we run statistical experiments to directly test the potential relationships with the help of bootstrap methods. Specifically, through random 60% sampling and significance testing check at the 10% level, we obtain intervals for the estimated effects. To show the relationship directly, export volumes and SDG scores for importing countries are used. Based on the experimental results, we derive elasticities between SDGs and trade in staple crops (wheat, rice, soybeans, and maize), along with detailed confidence intervals. 

The SDG data is from a previous study by Huan et al.<sup>18</sup> , which provided detailed assessment of global and regional SDG progress. It offers higher precision than previous efforts by incorporating target-level evaluations and synergistic performance analyses for every goal and every country from 2000 to 2023. The scale range from 0 to 1 across years, with no units. 

Furthermore, due to computational and numerical-precision limitations, some calculated values may be indistinguishable from zero despite representingsmallnon-zeroeffects.Toovercomethisissue,theleastvalueof 0.0001 is used to replace the observed valued that lack accurate records due to computational limits. In practical analysis, two aspects are considered. First, national and regional group-level results were evaluated according to the classification standards adopted in previous studies, while SDG scores werecalculateddirectlyforeachgoalbecausetheindicatorsarestandardized at the goal level<sup>18</sup> . Second, the potential contributions of individual SDGs to overall national-level reductions were examined. The proportional contribution of each SDG to the total reduction was calculated to provide a clearer representation of the relative importance of different goals.
