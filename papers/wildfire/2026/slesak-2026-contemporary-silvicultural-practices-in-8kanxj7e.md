# Contemporary Silvicultural Practices in the Western Portion of the Pacific Northwest, USA

## Abstract

Abstract The western portion of the Pacific Northwest (PNW) has some of the most productive and iconic forests of the United States. Management of these forests has changed dramatically over the past three decades, driven by the primary objectives and constraints associated with major forest landowner groups. Key changes include 1) increased focus on the establishment phase and a reduction in intermediate treatments for ownerships practicing intensive management (e.g., forest industry), 2) an increase in the use of variable retention harvesting on state, Bureau of Land Management, and tribal managed lands, and 3) and a shift to almost exclusive use of thinning on National Forest System lands. Most silvicultural activity is focused on Douglas-fir on privately owned timberlands despite federal ownerships comprising more than half of all timberland in the region. Regeneration is primarily accomplished via planting using both genetically improved and wild-collected seed depending on species and ownership. Increased fire occurrence and severity have given rise to a broad class of treatments related to fuels reduction and thinning to increase resiliency, which is likely to continue to expand. Future changes in practice will be driven by evolving objectives of major forest landowner groups, but we anticipate increased focus on the establishment phase across all groups. Reduced investment in silviculture research and a trend towards standardized silvicultural prescriptions are key challenges to future innovation in the region at a time when the need for innovation to maintain forests and the benefits they provide has never been greater.

## **Introduction** 

The Pacific Northwest (PNW) has some of the most productive forests in the United States that provide significant quantities of forest products, ecosystem services, and societal benefits. The region extends from northern California and northward to British Columbia, Canada (Fig. 1) encompassing a diverse array of forest types driven by a wide range in climate associated with the mountainous topography that is iconic to the region. Coniferous forests of coast Douglas-fir ( _Pseudotsuga menziesii var. menziesii_ ), western hemlock ( _Tsuga heterophylla_ ), true firs ( _Abies_ spp.), and their mixtures dominate the landscape, along with many other minor but commercially important species such as red alder ( _Alnus rubra_ ) and bigleaf maple ( _Acer macrophyllum_ ). Forests and forestry have always been a major driver for the cultural and economic prosperity that the region enjoys, underlying a critical need for continued stewardship to maintain ecosystem services and ecological functions that forests provide. 

Our knowledge of forest development in response to silvicultural practice in the PNW is continuously expanding, informed by a body of research and experience initiated in the early nineteenth century that continues today (Curtis et al. 2007; Peterson and Anderson 2009). At the same time, management objectives evolve in response to changing social norms, emerging issues and stressors that modify ecosystem functions, and changing needs for forest products and ecosystem services. The combination of these factors requires continuous evaluation and refinement of silvicultural practices to meet the needs of all who depend on forests and the benefits they provide. 

In this paper, we provide contemporary trends for silviculture practices in the PNW with focus on the area in Oregon and Washington west of the Cascade mountain crest (i.e., westside forests); areas east of the Cascade crest are covered in other papers within this special issue. Our emphasis is on the current state of regional conditions (e.g., climate, disturbance, ownership objectives, etc.) and their influence on broad changes in silvicultural practices over the past 30 years. As such, we do not provide a detailed overview of the region’s characteristics, cover types, silvicultural techniques and prescriptions, and history which are covered in detail elsewhere (e.g., Tesch 1995; Maguire et al. 2015; Van Pelt 2025). We also evaluate new avenues and emerging challenges that are likely to influence how silviculture will continue to change in the future.

Ocean also moderates climate along coastal areas of the region, providing favorable conditions for forest growth and influencing range distribution of coastal species such as Sitka spruce ( _Picea sitchensis_ ). 

As with most regions, soils in the PNW have large influence on a host of ecosystem functions that control forest composition and productiviity. Variations in soil parent material (both source and depositional mode) combined with variability in climate results a wide array of soil orders in the western region. The Olympic Peninsula and Puget Sound region of the PNW are heavily influenced by glacial deposits including dense till and coarse-textured outwash. Soils in Oregon’s Coast Range are developed from marine sedimentary deposits, while those along the Cascades are derived from ancient basalts and other marine sedimentary rocks. Soils across the region also have varying influence of ash and pumice from volcanic eruptions, giving rise to wide array of Andisols and soils with andic properties (NRCS 2026). Together, these variations result in a tremendous range of forest soil fertility across the region (Littke et al. 2011). 

The interaction of climate and soil plays an outsized role in forest establishment, development, and productivity. For example, summer soil water availability, which is controlled by soil water storage capacity and growing season precipitation, is a major factor influencing successful establishment of planted seedlings (Harrington and Tappeiner 1991; Dinger and Rose 2009) and has large control on longer-term stand productivity (Latta et al. 2009). Given this, climate change is a major factor influencing reforestation success and maladaptive responses to extreme climate events at later points in stand development (St. Clair and Howe 2007). Mean annual temperature in the region has already increased by ~ 1 ℃ on average and could increase an additional 1–2 ℃ by midcentury; snowpack and summer precipitation

### **Disturbance Regimes and Vectors** 

Fire has always been a major form of disturbance in the PNW (Reilly et al. 2022), with considerable variability in historic fire regimes across the region’s forest types associated with variation in climate, topography, soils, and cultural burning activities (Spies et al. 2019). There is increasing evidence that prior to widespread EuroAmerican settlement, frequent fires of small extent and limited severity occurred in both dry and moist forests of the western Cascades (Tepley et al. 2014; Johnston et al. 2023, 2026) and the Klamath Mountains (Tepley et al. 2017), contributing to the development of complex, multi-cohort stands with variable structure and successional trajectories (Tepley et al. 2014; Johnston et al. 2026). Following settlement, fire frequencies in the western Cascades dropped dramatically through the 1900s due to fire suppression (Johnston et al. 2023, 2026). In recent decades, the PNW has seen a significant increase in large fires with increasing severity (Cova et al. 2025). Some of the recent increase was driven by synoptic, east wind events that occurred historically (Reilly et al. 2022), but most of the increase appears to be driven by a combination of past fire exclusion coupled with more fire-prone weather events associated with climate change (Hagmann et al. 2021; Cova et al. 2025). Given this, extensive high-severity wildfire is likely to persist for the foreseeable future, especially given interactions with climate change (Halofsky et al. 2020; Abatzoglou et al. 2021), which is projected to increase burn probabilities and the frequency of large fires across much of the region, particularly in the western Cascades of Oregon and the North Cascades of Washington (Dye et al. 2024). These changes will influence silvicultural decisions and practices as fire severity has large control on the post-fire regeneration environment via degree of canopy loss and impacts to soils including reductions in nutrient and water availability (Certini 2005; Homann et al. 2011). 

Biological disturbance agents (BDA) include plants, pests and pathogens that can be either native or introduced that cause tree decline and mortality (Shaw et al. 2022). There is an extensive number of known BDAs in the PNW (see supplemental Table S1) which are considered in forest management and silviculture. Some notable impactful BDAs include Swiss needle cast ( _Nothophaeocryptopus gaeumannii_ ), laminated ( _Coniferiporia sulphurascens_ ) and Armillaria ( _Armillaria_ spp.) root rots, and various wood-boring beetles such as Douglas-fir beetle ( _Dendroctonus pseudotsugae_ ), flatheaded fir borer ( _Phaenops drummondii_ ), Mountain Pine beetle ( _Dendroctonus ponderosae_ ), and Fir engraver ( _Scolytus ventralis_ ) (WA DNR 2025). Recent occurrence of western red cedar decline is also of major concern but it appears to be driven by changing abiotic conditions rather than a BDA (Andrus et al. 2024). 

With few exceptions, use of specific silvicultural systems is generally not broadly employed to preemptively address BDAs. Thinning is one silvicultural practice used 

Journal of Forestry 

to increase resource availability and decrease susceptibility to some BDAs (Fettig et al. 2007), but the greatest and most recent influence of BDAs on silviculture has been on decisions regarding what tree species or genotype to plant during reforestation. Silviculturists may modify what is planted in areas where a BDA is endemic (e.g., in areas known to contain various root diseases), or in response to increasing maladaptation of local trees to climate which causes them to become more stressed and susceptible to BDAs (Agne et al. 2018). For example, the native fungus Swiss Needle Cast is prolific along the coastlines of Oregon and Washington and can reduce volume by up to 45% in the worst performing stands (Mainwaring 2024), causing managers to plant alternative species such as western hemlock and Sitka spruce. Similarly, the presence of white pine blister rust ( _Cronartium ribicola_ ) in the region has broadly reduced planting and management of western white pine ( _Pinus monticola_ ); genetic breeding efforts for rust-resistant white pine may change this dynamic in the future (Sniezko and Liu 2022). 

### **Wood Products** 

There are several contemporary trends in utilization that have large influences on silvicultural decisions worth highlighting. Primary among these is an increase in the use of small diameter (< 20 cm small end) logs for dimensional lumber and a decreased capacity for processing of large diameter logs (Simmons et al. 2025). This likely resulted from shortened rotation lengths on ownerships with objectives to maximize net financial return (see below), incentivizing mills to maximize wood volume recovery from small-diameter logs. Advances in cross laminated lumber and other engineered wood products may have also contributed to this trend, as these products can serve as a substitute for large logs. The change has been so pronounced that there are only a handful of mills in the region that will purchase large logs (> 60 cm on small end), and harvesting of large old growth trees now rarely occurs. Regardless of the reason, the shift in utilization standards has a large influence on silvicultural decisions across all ownerships ranging from thinning occurrence and type to rotation length (generally decreasing both). 

By far the dominant species harvested is Douglas-fir (~ 60–75% of annual cut), followed by western hemlock (~ 10–25% of annual cut) (Simmons et al. 2025; Scott et al. 2025), as reflected in the dominance of these species across the landscape (Table 1). Hardwoods and other conifer species, such as red alder and western red cedar, are harvested to some extent but mills specializing in these species have declined due to limited supply over the past decades. Despite comparable land bases (Table 2), the majority of forest harvest occurs on private ownerships (~ 75%) with federal ownerships (either U.S. Department of Agriculture, Forest Service (Forest Service) or Bureau of Land Management (BLM) lands) providing less than 10% of annual volume removals (Simmons et al. 2025; Scott et al. 2025). There have been recent policy changes that may increase the annual cut from federal ownerships (USDA Office of the Secretary 2025), but the general pattern of most harvest occurring from Douglas-fir forests on private ownerships is unlikely to change soon.

### **Commercial Timberland Area and Growing Stock of Major Species** 

Changes in commercial timberland area and growing stock over the last 30 years are difficult to assess because of different estimation methodologies (e.g., changes in inventory protocols). For this paper, estimates were generated with the EVALIDator tool (Forest Service 2025a, b) using FIA data from the western portion of the region that was collected between 2012–2022. This most recent assessment estimates a total of 41.4 million acres (Table 1) of commercial timberland compared to 38.9 million acres reported in Tesch (1995, citing Waddell et al. 1989), a ~ 6% increase equally split between private and public ownerships. This difference likely arises from the arbitrary boundary between eastern and western portions of WA and OR used previously (Waddell et al. 1989) compared to a county-based delineation used here (Supplemental Fig. 1). With that in mind, total growing stock has increased by 17% across all species (Table 2), which aligns with reports of increased forest carbon over recent decades in the region, particularly on National Forest lands (Gray et al. 2016). The relative contribution of a given species to total growing stock in the region has remained stable with a few notable exceptions. Among softwoods, the relative proportion has increased for Douglas-fir (to almost 70% of the total, Table 2) and decreased for western hemlock. Among hardwoods the relative proportion has increased for “other hardwoods” and decreased for red alder. National Forest System lands contain the most Douglas-fir growing stock (> 40% of total), followed by private ownerships (~ 30%), the BLM (~ 20%), and state ownerships (~ 10%) (data not shown).

### **Ownerships and Management Objectives** 

Land ownership strongly influences management objectives and by extension the silvicultural regimes and practices that are employed, resulting in a mosaic of conditions across the landscape (Fig. 3). There are a host of land ownership types in the PNW, but here we focus on the broad categories of federal, state, tribal, and forest industry. Non-industrial private owners are also an important ownership category in the region, but the high variability in their objectives precludes generalization. Of course, specific objectives are quite variable within the broad ownership categories we have focused on as well, but there are some important generalities worth noting. 

In the PNW, the primary federal agencies that actively manage forestlands include the Forest Service and the BLM. Both agencies have multiple use mandates that

In addition, the BLM has a fiduciary responsibility to generate revenue for local counties under the Oregon and California Revested Lands Sustained Yield Management Act of 1937, which also influences its management (BLM 2026). In general, silvicultural activities on federal lands are extensive relative to the other major ownership groups. Relative to each other, silvicultural activities on BLM lands are more varied and intensive than those on Forest Service lands, with the BLM incorporating genetically improved planting stock, more frequent use of mechanical vegetation management, and use of both regeneration harvests and intermediate thinning treatments. Silvicultural activities on Forest Service lands are dominated by intermediate thinning treatments (> 90% of acres harvested), with some very limited use of regeneration harvests (USDA 2025b). Activities of both agencies have been heavily influenced by wildland fire in recent years; post-fire salvage harvesting occurs on BLM land but is uncommon on Forest Service land, but postfire planting is common on both BLM and Forest Service lands. 

Industrial forest ownership is a catchall that includes public corporations and private companies that manage a range of timberland acreages across the region. In general, the primary ownership objective has shifted from what was once a focus on maximum wood production to one centered on maximizing financial return (Murphy et al. 2005). Silvicultural activities that maximize rapid stand growth over short rotations (35–45 

Journal of Forestry 

years) are increasingly common including the use of genetically improved planting stock, sustained (multiple year) competing vegetation control, and precommercial thinning. This change in objective is associated with the rise of Timber Investment Management Organizations (TIMOs) and Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs), which have become a major component of large corporate forest ownership starting at the turn of the century (Bliss et al. 2010; Sass et al. 2021). Note that some industry ownerships, particularly those privately held, may deviate substantially from these generalities to optimize multiple forest benefits beyond financial return. 

State ownerships, which include the Oregon Department of Forestry (ODF) and the Washington Department of Natural Resources (WADNR), are intermediate in their level of management intensity but lean toward more intensive. In Washington, more than 80% of state-owned timberlands are designated as State Trust Lands with a legal obligation to generate revenue for trust beneficiaries. Because of this, a primary objective is wood production, with genetically improved planting stock, vegetation control, and intermediate treatments commonly used. However, they differ from industry ownerships with longer average rotation lengths (~ 50–60 years), management of a broader range of tree species, greater retention of live trees following regeneration harvests, and implementing wider riparian buffers and more habitat-focused management (Halofsky and Donato 2024). Oregon manages their state-owned lands in a similar manner but has a much smaller land base compared to WADNR and other major ownership categories (Table 2). 

Tribal forest management objectives and silvicultural practices are quite variable; most tribes in the western PNW have relatively small forest land bases (~ 10,000–16,000 ac or less) compared to eastern PNW tribes, but some manage much larger areas of forestland with dedicated forest management programs (e.g., Quinault Indian Nation in WA, QIA 2017). Generalizing management objectives among tribes has limited utility, but some commonalities include: 1) a focus on environmental stewardship to maintain culturally and ecologically important aspects of forests that support tribal well-being, and 2) management for sustained provision of both timber and non-timber forest products with more emphasis on the latter (Gordon et al. 2023). Silvicultural activities on tribal lands in the PNW span a continuum from extensive to somewhat intensive with practices that often incorporate development of structural heterogeneity, utilize moderately intensive vegetation management, and favor mixed species stand composition or culturally important species such as western red cedar. Recently, there has been increased coordination between tribes and adjacent federal lands to promote cross-boundary, landscape-scale management across tribes’ ancestral territories, as exemplified by a recent shared stewardship agreement between the Cow Creek Band of Umpqua Tribe of Indians and the Forest Service (USDA 2026). 

## **Silvicultural Systems and Practices** 

### **General** 

Silvicultural activities in the region are largely focused on conifer cover types (e.g., Douglas-fir, western hemlock, and mixed conifer) with some limited activity in 

Journal of Forestry 

hardwood cover types (e.g., red alder); the assessment below is primarily relevant to conifers. There have been many changes in the use of silviculture in the PNW over the past three decades, but the most notable is a shift towards more simplified approaches that vary with landowner group: (1) shorter rotations on intensively managed lands, with greater emphasis and resources on the establishment phase with decreasing use of intermediate treatments; (2) increased use of variable retention harvesting on state managed, tribal, and BLM lands; and (3) on National Forest System lands, a wholesale decrease in regeneration harvesting and a shift to intermediate thinning treatments. The evolution of these simplified approaches reflects the primary objectives and policies of each of the major landowner groups outlined above. A majority of silvicultural activity occurs in low- to mid-elevation areas because of geographic patterns in landownership; many high elevation forests (e.g., mountain hemlock, subalpine fir, lodgepole pine, etc.) occur on National Forest System lands that fall under reserve categories of the NWFP. We detail associated changes in practice for each phase of the generalized silvicultural system below. 

### **Establishment Phase** 

On private lands, the decrease in rotation lengths and use of intermediate treatments has shifted focus of silvicultural activities to the establishment phase to maximize investment return. Establishment practices are generally intensive across ownerships who conduct regeneration harvests to ensure successful establishment and rapid growth of the succeeding stand. Common site preparation practices include slash piling and burning, and competing vegetation control with herbicides on non-federal ownerships, or mechanical treatments on federal lands (primarily BLM). Vegetation control in the Mediterranean climate of the Pacific Northwest is critical in many areas, especially on drier, and hotter sites in southern Oregon, low elevation areas, valley fringes and areas with less than 50″ of annual rainfall (Harrington and Tappeiner 1991; Oester and Fitzgerald 2020; Ford et al. 2017). Release treatments are typically conducted during the spring of the first and often the second growing season after planting with a focus on controlling invasive forbs and grasses that heavily compete for soil water with planted tree seedlings. Given projections of increased drought (Hicke et al. 2022), increased intensity and duration of vegetation control may become more common for successful establishment in the future (Flamenco et al. 2019; Maguire et al. 2009) especially on low soil quality sites with coarsetextured soils where water availability is inherently low (Slesak et al. 2026). With few exceptions, artificial regeneration via planting is used to rapidly establish stands and control the species and genetics of planted seedlings. Douglas-fir is by far the most common species planted, but in coastal areas the use of western hemlock has increased over the past 20 years due to the negative impacts of Swiss Needle Cast on Douglas-fir, a more favorable hemlock market, and its increased volume production over Douglas-fir (the shade-tolerant western hemlock can be managed at substantially higher densities, Steinbrenner 1976). Many other species are also planted, but in smaller quantities (Table 3). Reforestation with hardwoods, in particular red alder, has decreased for several reasons, including greater susceptibility to drought 

Journal of Forestry 

and adverse weather events, as well as limited experience in successfully managing the species across a range of situations (Harrington 2006). 

Cooperative breeding programs have focused on the development of genetically improved seed for Douglas-fir and western hemlock, which is used exclusively to reforest most of the intensively managed land in the region. Breeding programs for other species have also been developed (Table 3) to select for specific traits such as growth and foliage color (e.g., noble fir) and disease resistance (5-needle pines, Sniezko and Liu 2022). The Forest Service primarily uses woods-run (wild collected) seed, but all landowners use woods-run to some extent, especially for minor species without an associated breeding program. Establishment of seed orchards is now standard practice (including for woods-run seed) to provide a reliable seed supply at a lower cost. Lastly, although planting is the dominant regeneration method used in the region, natural regeneration via seed is used in some instances, in particular on Forest Service lands following fire when nearby seed sources exist (Laughlin et al. 2023; Forest Service 2024). 

Monocultures, especially Douglas-fir on forest industry ownerships, are typically planted to simplify management and optimize volume production over common rotation lengths compared to multi-species mixtures (Omari et al. 2021; Slesak et al. 2025b). Other ownerships such as the Forest Service or state agencies are more likely to plant a mixture of species, often pairing shade tolerant and intolerant species. For example, many WA DNR stands are planted with a majority of shade intolerant Douglas-fir coupled with a minor (< 20% of seedlings) component of western hemlock or western red cedar to increase diversity, forest resilience or address root rot issues or cultural concerns. Mixed species stands also develop naturally when shade tolerant species seed in to planted monocultures; this is common in near coastal areas where western hemlock becomes a large component of planted Douglas-fir stands (Burns and Honkala 1990; Fiske and DeBell 1989). 

Choice of species and populations within species is starting to shift in response to climate change. Reforestation with climate adapted species and populations is the most cost-effective approach for climate adaptation in the region (Agne et al. 2025). Use of assisted population migration, or the movement of populations within a species to sites within the species habitat range and with a predicted future climate similar to the one they evolved under (Aitken and Whitlock 2013; Williams and Dumroese 2013), is of increasing interest and use (Agne and Slesak 2025). The PNW region is particularly well-suited for this practice because: 1) Douglas-fir is the dominant planted species and its populations are closely adapted to their local environments that cover a wide geographic range (St Clair et al. 2005); 2) the PNW encompasses a tremendous range of climates because of its topography (Fig. 1); and 3) well-defined seed zones developed decades ago can now be used to guide seed transfer among climates. General seed transfer guidelines that balance near term reductions in productivity with longer-term adaptation to future climate have been developed from provenance trials across the region (St. Clair et al. 2019; Bansal et al. 2015), with transfers of ~ 2 ℃ in climate space commonly recommended. These guidelines are beginning to be used by public agencies for deployment of wild collected seed (St. Clair et al. 2022), and there are efforts to support expansion of the practice more broadly (Agne et al. 2025). Bareroot seedlings, especially for Douglas-fir, still dominate the seedling market due to their lower cost, superior size and greater browse resilience on mesic, productive sites. However, container produced plug seedlings have seen a steady increase over the past 20 years for a variety of reasons (Grossnickle and El-Kassaby 2016). In areas with prolonged drought, higher temperatures and shorter spring planting windows, container grown plug seedlings are becoming the standard stock type because of higher survival compared to bareroot. Containerized plugs can also be grown in one year versus the two years needed for bareroot, providing landowners flexibility to respond to changing markets, post wildfire planting, and opportunities for fall planting (see below). New research may help to increase container seedling field survival with climate change, including nursery drought conditioning of seedlings (Moler and Nelson 2021), the inclusion of biochar or other amendments in plugs (Dumroese et al. 2011), and the improvement of seedling root systems through novel container systems with a focus on individually wrapped seedlings (Khadduri and Wightman 2024). 

Planting densities have typically been around 400 trees per acre (TPA) as this density provided a spacing that maximized individual tree growth and total stand volume until density dependent mortality occurs (Harrington et al. 2009). This planting density is still used in combination with precommercial thinning, but lower and more site and species-specific planting densities are becoming common depending on management objectives (Agne and Slesak 2025). For more intensive management, lower rotation ages combined with a reduced premium on large logs have resulted in less need for precommercial and commercial thinning during the rotation. Further, improved genetics and increased vegetation control have also resulted in greater seedling survival and faster stand development. Together, these factors allow for lower planting densities that still result in high wood production for Douglas-fir (Slesak et al. 2025a, b). However, some managers still plant relatively high densities to provide flexibility on future activities or reduce risk of understocking following unforeseen mortality. On ownerships with less intensive management, notably National Forest System lands, limitations associated with seedling availability (Fargione et al. 2021), coupled with a large and increasing backlog of reforestation needs (Forest Service 2022) have resulted in use of planting densities as low as 150–200 TPA (Agne and Slesak 2025). Planting at these low densities allows managers to reforest larger area while still meeting minimum stocking requirements, among other factors (e.g., limited funds for precommercial thinning). Planting is still typically done in rows at variable spacing between seedlings depending on target densities, but there is emerging interest in planting at different spatial configurations to promote non-timber benefits (North et al. 2019). 

Planting typically occurs as early as possible in the year so that seedlings can establish with adequate soil water before summer drought; delays in planting often lead to reforestation failures (Steinfeld et al. 2007). Lower-elevation sites are generally planted in early spring (Jan-Mar) and higher elevation sites in later spring (AprMay) when sites become accessible after snowmelt. Fall planting is of increasing 

Journal of Forestry 

interest because it allows seedlings to develop roots before winter and provides them with maximum time for optimal growing conditions the following year (Grossnickle and McDonald 2021). However, fall planting is logistically more challenging than the more common winter or spring planting, requiring close coordination among the nursery, site manager, and planting crews to ensure that seedlings are hardened off and planted when soils are sufficiently moist to support survival (Grossnickle and McDonald 2021). Overall, post-planting survival can vary widely from < 40% to > 95% depending on site conditions (soils, aspect, climate), but typically ranges between 80–90% on intensively managed lands. 

### **Intermediate Treatments** 

Use of pre-commercial thinning (PCT) is generally decreasing but still remains an important tool to control stocking levels, especially in coastal and high-elevation forests (above 2,500 ft), where natural regeneration of western hemlock, spruce and true fir species is more abundant. As planting densities in lower-elevation Douglasfir plantations have decreased, and vegetation management programs continue to improve, use of PCT is likely to continue declining. The Forest Service still conducts PCT in the region, but declining use over the last two decades (USDA 2025b) mirrors trends from other ownerships. In the past, PCT was typically conducted at stand age of 10–20 years with a target residual density of ~ 250–300 TPA depending on species (Reukema 1975), but it is now conducted at earlier ages (~ 8–12 years) because of more rapid stand development. 

With rotation ages in the 30–40-year range on many large industrial ownerships, no substantial price premiums for larger logs, and the use of lower planting densities (see above), the application of commercial thinning has also decreased significantly on non-federal lands. However, commercial thinning remains a commonly applied treatment on some small and mid-sized industrial ownerships that manage on somewhat longer rotations (i.e., 50–65 year), as well as on nonindustrial private lands (Puettemann et al. 2024). On private lands, thinning is typically conducted 15–20 years before final harvest, removing half the stems and one third of the basal area and volume primarily from the intermediate and co-dominant crown classes (thinning from below). 

In contrast with large private lands, both uniform commercial thinning and variable density thinning (VDT) remain important silvicultural tools on public, tribal, and conservation focused non-profit organization lands. Most thinning activities occur on federal lands, but state lands also utilize VDT in areas dedicated to providing older forest habitat, as well as commercial thinning in a variety of circumstances that either preclude a regeneration harvest or stipulate longer rotations such as in sub-basins managed for rain-on-snow (WA DNR 1997). Thinning represents the primary harvest method utilized on National Forest System lands (> 90% of all harvest activity from 2005–2025, USDA 2025b) and non-profit organization lands in the region. The goal of conducting VDT treatments by these landowners is to achieve older forest structural characteristics that are important as habitat for threatened species (Case et al. 2023). In addition to thinning, mid-rotation fertilization was initiated in the  1960 s based on early research showing pronounced growth responses to nitrogen additions on soils developed from glacial deposits (Gessel et al. 1965). Instead, there has been a tremendous drop in fertilizer use in the last decade with only a small fraction (< 1%) of industry lands fertilized. This is likely a result of the shift in objectives from maximum wood production to maximized financial return, and new research which showed that not all stands were nitrogen-limited and generally marginal responses to fertilization with other nutrients (Mainwaring et al. 2014). 

### **Regeneration Phase** 

Most regeneration harvests are required to retain some number of live trees as mandated by Forest Practices rules in OR and WA. Live tree retention on private lands must meet the minimum state-specific Forest Practices Rules that apply when clearcutting. These rules typically require retention of a small number of live trees or snags per acre, along with riparian buffers, with specific requirements varying by state (WA DNR 2023; ODF 2023). In contrast, state agencies typically use variable retention harvesting (VRH, Franklin and Donato 2020) that retain greater amounts of live trees as outlined in their respective Habitat Conservation Plans (WA DNR 1997, ODF 2024). Variable retention harvesting is a type of regeneration harvest that retains significant quantities of live trees and structural legacies in a deliberate manner for additional protection of stream and sensitive areas, wildlife habitat, and protection of threatened and endangered species. In addition to wider buffers around streams, wetlands, and other sensitive areas, VRH typically involves retention of ~ 5% of live trees in variable spatial patterns, ranging from individual trees with desirable characteristics (size, form, or species) to clumps of trees that span a range of sizes.. Regeneration harvest methods on BLM lands include use of VRH with residual densities ranging from 5–30% of pre-harvest levels, and some use of selection harvest methods, often incorporating a mix of individual tree and group selection methods (i.e., free selection). 

There have been many studies in the region that have examined the effects of VRH on a variety of ecosystem responses including wildlife and habitat (Aubry et al. 2009; Gitzen et al. 2007), aesthetics (Ribe 2009), early seedling growth (Urgenson et al. 2013), and stand development (Maguire et al. 2007). At the landscape scale, VRH results in a significant amount of tree retention, notably as linear features along riparian management zones, large tree clumps interior to harvest units, and dispersed live trees and snags within the harvested matrix (Fig. 5). 

### **Fire and Silviculture** 

Increasing frequencies of large fires over the last two to three decades (Parks and Abatzoglou 2020) have contributed to some shifts in silvicultural trends. Postfire salvage harvests remain common in the region, but trends in salvage harvest practices have diverged markedly across ownerships since the early to mid-1990s (Zuspan et al. 2024). Mean rates of postfire salvage on private lands have remained relatively stable, although salvage following low to moderate severity fires has increased along with increases in mean salvage harvest patch sizes (Zuspan et al. 2024). In contrast salvage rates on federal lands decreased sharply in the mid-1990s and remain low, with the largest decline occurring in areas that burned at low to moderate severities. Declines in salvage harvesting are related to complex socio-ecological factors that shape post-disturbance decision-making (Ryan and Hammin 2008). Postfire reforestation on private industrial lands occurs rapidly and practices largely parallel those used following regeneration harvests. In contrast, postfire reforestation on Forest Service lands is quite variable and depend on fire severity (e.g., presence of live trees to allow natural regeneration; influence of dead standing trees on the light environment), resource constraints (e.g., availability of seedlings and staff, Fargione et al. 2021), and policy requirements that can delay planting and related activities (e.g., allowing development of competing vegetation). The combination of these factors can create challenges in achieving successful establishment (North et al. 2019). 

In recent years, postfire planting mixes on federal lands in the Cascades and Klamath Mountains have shifted toward combinations of species with increased adaptation to wildfire and drought, including mixtures of Douglas-fir, western white pine, and sometimes small proportions of incense-cedar or sugar pine in moist Douglas-fir/western hemlock series sites. On drier sites in southwest Oregon, mixes of Douglas-fir, ponderosa pine, and sugar pine are increasingly common. Like other dry regions in the west, postfire reforestation challenges are greater on these drier and warmer site types (Davis et al. 2023). Natural regeneration establishes most readily following moderate severity fires for most species in the region, while Douglas-fir, western hemlock, and to some extent western redcedar also regenerate readily after high severity fire on moist sites (Dunn et al. 2020). 

Slash disposal via pile burning remains a common element of site preparation following regeneration harvests across most ownerships in the region. On federal lands, thinning, often coupled with pile burning or broadcast burning treatments, has become increasingly common as a fuel treatment method; in contrast, broadcast burning on other ownerships has decreased steadily and is not commonly practiced. The increased emphasis on thinning and prescribed burning on federal lands is notable in the warmer and drier mixed conifer forests characteristic of the Klamath mountains and southern Cascades in southwest Oregon, where collaborative partnerships have developed to address cross-boundary management issues related to wildfire mitigation, ecological resilience, and biodiversity conservation at landscape scales (e.g., Metlen et al. 2017, 2021, USDA 2026). 

There is ample evidence that thinning and prescribed burning treatments reduce wildfire severity in the warmer and drier forests of the interior Northwest (Davis et al. 2024; Brodie et al. 2024), but recent work in the PNW has shown limited effects of management history and forest structural variables on wildfire severity during extreme fire weather events (Reilly et al. 2022). Some authors have suggested that traditional fuel treatments that focus on reducing surface fuel loading and both horizontal and vertical fuel continuity such as thinning, broadcast burning, and pile burning may offer limited or short-lived effectiveness under the conditions that typically drive large fires in the moist, highly productive forests of the PNW (Halofsky et al. 2018a, b, 2020). At the same time, many studies have shown that forest structural variables such as canopy cover and height (Evers et al. 2022; Cova et al. 2025), tree diameter and species (Dunn and Bailey 2016; Johnston et al. 2019), and stand age or time since disturbance (Zald and Dunn 2018; Busby et al. 2023; Cova et al.

Since silvicultural decisions directly influence these variables, and given the projected increases in wildfire risk across the region (Dye et al. 2024), use of fuel treatments and experimentation with silvicultural approaches to fuel management is likely to increase in the coming decades. 

### **Role of Technological and Computational Advances** 

The biggest influence of technological advances on silviculture in the PNW is the development of statistical and process-based models and spatially-explicit decision support tools to manage the forest landscape on a site-specific basis. For example, the maximum stand density index (Heiderman and Kimsey 2021) predicts site and species-specific maximums for the PNW (and other parts of the country), supporting stand-specific density management prescriptions. GIS-based tools have fundamentally changed how silvicultural practices are implemented and assessed from the stand to landscape. For example, the Private Forest Accord, an expansive agreement between timber industry and conservation groups to update forest practice rules in Oregon, was largely possible because of advances in GIS tools to map, monitor, and assess many of the agreement’s provisions (e.g., Lu et al. 2026). The explosion of data products has challenged forest management organizations to adapt their systems and processes to effectively use the information and convert it into more efficient, data-driven operational decision-making. 

Development of remotely sensed data products has driven significant changes in mapping and inventory, forest health monitoring, carbon accounting and operational planning. Most notable are significant changes in forest inventory and assessment, where remote sensing technologies such as Light Detection and Ranging (LIDAR), multispectral light technology and high-resolution photography are becoming increasingly common tools with the potential to replace many plot-based, survey approaches. Although certain challenges remain, such as species identification and capturing trees living below the dominant canopy, the technological advances have seen exponential growth with some companies building their forest inventory system primarily on remotely sensed data (Fassnacht et al. 2024). 

In forest operations across the region, drone-based imagery analysis via machine learning is increasingly used for stocking and post-PCT surveys, providing silviculturists with rapid assessments of treatment effectiveness (Buchelt et al. 2024). Drone-based technology is used in other aspects of forest operations that influence silviculture, including nursery seedling inventories, cone surveys, and application of herbicides (Clark et al. 2023, Richardson 2024). Harvesting operations have also benefited from technological advances, with new equipment and techniques improving both productivity and safety. For example, tethered logging systems are now widely used on steep slopes making operations considerably safer, more productive and more cost efficient (Chung et al. 2022).

### **Future Directions in Practice and Challenges to Innovation** 

A central driver of future silvicultural practice will be the evolution and continued refinement of management objectives and their influence on silvicultural decisions needed to achieve them. Management objectives have always had a fundamental influence on silviculture, but the linkage plays an outsized role in the PNW region because of the major ownership groups that manage large tracts of land with distinctly different objectives. For example, we expect landowners focused on maximizing financial return will continue with that focus, but the manner in which maximum financial return is achieved may change as more forest products and services (e.g., carbon storage, recreation) become monetized, causing concurrent changes in practice. Alternatively, the focus on maximum financial return may cause financial investment ownerships to divest from their land base, with unknown consequences for PNW forests. In contrast, for public ownerships, any change in policy or law could have dramatic influence on the use and extent of silvicultural practices given the large proportion of commercial timberland they manage (Table 1). The abrupt change in silviculture practice on federal lands following adoption of the Northwest Forest Plan is a powerful example of such change (Spies et al. 2019), and now practices are beginning to change again in response to recent policy directives. 

Management objectives are also heavily influenced by socio-ecological regional conditions, and we expect that changes in climate and fire behavior and efforts to mitigate their impacts, as well as ongoing shifts in societal values will have an outsized role in shaping the future of PNW silviculture. The introduction or expansion of BDAs, such as the recent discovery of emerald ash borer in Oregon (Zhu et al. 2025), and ongoing interactions between hot, dry conditions and multiple BDAs in the warmer and drier parts of the region (Bennett et al. 2023) will also influence practice. Forecasting specific changes in management objectives is not possible, but the inevitability of changing conditions, shifting societal norms, and increased focus on financial return for some landowner groups makes some change inevitable. 

### **Continued Innovation During the Establishment Phase** 

Given recent trends, we think it is likely that efforts will continue to increase during the establishment phase to ensure optimal conditions for rapid stand development. Advances in establishment phase innovation (and investment) will be concentrated in ownerships that conduct intensive management, but all ownerships are likely to pursue opportunities to increase overall reforestation success. Focus on site- and situation-specific practices that advance stand development under current and predicted future conditions will dominate these efforts. We expect greater emphasis on the identification or development (via breeding or genetic improvement) of seed sources that match a given set of site conditions (i.e., edaphic and environmental conditions) and objectives. For example, assisted species and population migration are broadly recognized as low-cost approaches for climate adaptation, and operational use of these practices in the region is likely to increase if barriers to implementation can be addressed for both wild collected and genetically improved seed 

Journal of Forestry 

(Agne and Slesak 2025). Other examples of seed-site matching work include efforts by tree improvement cooperatives in Oregon and Washington to identify Douglas-fir genotypes with increased drought resistance, and interest in hybridization of Douglas-fir coastal and interior varieties to increase drought and frost resistance of coast Douglas-fir (Compton et al. 2023). 

Other areas of innovation during the establishment phase are likely to include advances in seedling production based on the target plant concept which emphasizes the matching of seedling characteristics to site-specific conditions (Landis 2011; Davis and Pinto 2021). Efforts to improve drought resistance via drought conditioning in the nursery (e.g., Moler and Nelson 2021) and refinement of root:shoot biomass targets to match site resource availability are a few examples of potential innovations in plant production. The large influence of plant competition on seedling resource availability will drive innovations in competition control through improved ability to assess situation-specific conditions (edaphic factors + management constraints + dominant competitors) and determine the optimal magnitude and duration of methods for effective control that minimize economic costs and ecological impact. Lastly, given the unpredictable and changing conditions during the spring planting season, we anticipate increased interest and effort for fall planting that will help to address some of the barriers that currently exist to implementation (Grossnickle and McDonald 2021). 

### **Rapid Advances in Technology** 

The amount and rate of technological advance over the first two decades of this century has been tremendous, giving rapid rise to new tools that support silviculture (D’Amato et al. 2018, and as outlined above). The current explosion of development in artificial intelligence (AI) and robotics has great potential to eclipse those earlier advances, but uncertainty remains on the practical utility of AI and related technologies, and more importantly, how such advances will influence silviculture specifically in the PNW. We think the greatest potential innovations will be associated with computational advances that could fundamentally transform how we quantify and assess site and stand conditions, providing decision and implementation tools for precision silviculture across a range of spatial scales. These tools may provide the ability to move beyond broad objectives of “structurally complex” to detailed structural prescriptions that optimize benefits from a set of ecosystem functions. Other advances are likely in a variety of applications such as forest genetics (genomics and breeding), logistics planning, and nursery technology. 

Advances in use of unmanned vehicles (aerial and ground based) and robotics will continue to expand our abilities for data collection, contributing to the computational advances touched on above. Given the absolute amount and extent of timberland in the PNW region (Fig. 1, Table 1) these advances may be particularly useful for quantifying conditions across a large geographic footprint with varying degrees of accessibility. We also expect that these technologies will directly influence practices and processes that are critical to silvicultural activities. Given the vast amount of regeneration via planting in the PNW, it is likely that technological applications 

Journal of Forestry 

will be developed and utilized for all aspects of the reforestation pipeline, with particular emphasis on seed acquisition, nursery production, and out-planting logistics (seedling handling, storage, and transport). Of course each of these potential applications will come with additional financial cost which may limit early adoption; financial barriers will depend on the overall utility of the new technology and its relevance to addressing management constraints that vary across land ownership groups. 

### **Challenges to Silvicultural Innovation and Practice** 

A major factor influencing silviculture innovation is research, notably long-term research studies that inform specific silvicultural practices and guide development of policy and management objectives that in turn influence operational practice. The PNW has a long history of silviculture research (Curtis et al. 2007) including many long-term and large-scale silviculture experiments to assess stand development to various treatments (Poage and Anderson 2007). A major challenge for future innovation is maintaining and establishing silviculture studies given persistent declines in research funding as noted by others (D’Amato et al. 2018; McGinley et al. 2019). To put this in context for the PNW, the PNW Research Station in Forest Service Research & Development had five scientists in Oregon and Washington focused on silviculture research a decade ago; currently there is only one. Other entities conduct silviculture research including academic institutions and some large forest industry companies, but these entities have also faced marked declines in research funding (Wagner et al. 2025; Homyack et al. 2022), with related impacts to research cooperatives housed within Oregon State University and the University of Washington. The emergence of investment forestry has infused a considerable amount of innovation and technological efficiency, but short-term investment horizons and constantly changing ownerships also has consequences for research funding and silviculture investments necessary for long-term, science-informed forest management. 

Such disinvestment in forestry and silviculture research is a major challenge to continued innovation within the region. Some research will continue for high-value or high-impact topics (e.g., Douglas-fir breeding and establishment, climate adaptation and assisted migration, silviculture and wildfire, technological advances; Homyack et al. 2022), but other research areas are likely to suffer including work on species other than Douglas-fir, development of novel silvicultural systems resilient to ecosystem stressors (climate change, invasive and expanding BDA’s, etc.), and quantifying the relationship of stand conditions to a broad array of ecosystem services. We may end up with technological advances that enable us to precisely define stand structural attributes in the future but be limited in relating those attributes to ecosystem functions and benefits beyond wood production. 

More importantly, we believe that silviculture in the PNW region runs the risk of marginalization as federal lands have severely scaled back active forest management, and prescriptions and practices have become increasingly simplified and streamlined on privately managed forests. If these trends continue, it may reduce the need for silviculturists to utilize the blend of art and science inherent to implementing 

Journal of Forestry 

innovative and diverse silvicultural practices. Landowners with the objective for maximum financial return know how to achieve it at the expense of forest complexity, while public lands are administratively or economically constrained, limiting innovation and implementation across the region. If the trend of two extremes in silvicultural systems continues—overly simplified with high cultural inputs and investment (plantation silviculture) or limited inputs (e.g., planting) with natural, but slow stand development—the need for trained silviculturists to implement them is reduced. At the same time, the need for innovative silviculture to maintain or optimize forest ecosystem functions and services has never been greater. The impacts of climate change and the potential for mitigation via forest C sequestration, coupled with changing forest conditions, disturbance, ownership patterns, and land use, has created challenges that must be addressed in order to maintain the benefits that forests provide to the PNW region (Halofsky et al. 2018a, b; Dye et al. 2024; Spies et al. 2019). 

Given the challenges laying ahead, a more balanced and renewed collaborative management approach across public and private lands in the PNW could help to achieve a wide array of forest benefits for the region. The current combination of forest management approaches conducted in the region is somewhat analogous to the triad concept of forest management (Seymour and Hunter 1992) although not intentional or proportional as conceptually envisioned. Triad management is a landscape approach designed to balance forestry with biodiversity conservation that includes 1) intensive plantation management (i.e., forest industry), 2) forest reserves (akin to recent management on National Forest System lands), and 3) a matrix of multi- objective management between the above extremes (Himes et al. 2022). It is this latter approach that offers the most opportunity for the expansion of silvicultural innovation in the region using concepts of ecological silviculture to manage stands for multiple forest resources (Palik and D’Amato 2025). State-managed forest lands in Oregon and Washington, BLM-administered lands, and many tribal lands already serve as a model for this by using a combination of variable retention harvesting and variable density thinning with moderate rotation lengths to provide a wide array of forest benefits. There are similar examples from non-industrial private ownerships (Puettemann et al. 2024) and other landowners across the region also embrace this approach. The region would benefit if other major landowner groups followed suit, providing silviculturists opportunity to develop and tend resilient forests for the many benefits that they are able to provide. 

## **Conclusion** 

The iconic forests of the western Pacific Northwest are integral to the region’s prosperity, providing untold cultural, economic, and ecological benefits. Forest management and silviculture can help to maintain these benefits, especially when considering all of the roles that major ownership groups encompass. Differences in the primary objectives or policies among ownership groups have driven much of the change in silviculture practice over the past 30 years, resulting in distinctly different silvicultural approaches being used that are somewhat analogous to the concept of 

Journal of Forestry 

triad forestry. The outcome is increasingly standardized and streamlined silvicultural systems being used across a vast amount of the region’s timberland. Looking to the future and the known and potentially new threats to maintaining forest functions and benefits, there is a need to promote and invest in silvicultural innovation to expand on multi-resource management that exists between the triad extremes that currently dominate forest management in the region. Coordinated landscape level planning among the major forest ownership groups may be necessary to functionally achieve this need.
