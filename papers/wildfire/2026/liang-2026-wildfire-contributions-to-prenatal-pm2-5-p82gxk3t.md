# Wildfire contributions to prenatal PM2.5 exposure in the contiguous United States, 2003–2019

## Abstract

Introduction Anthropogenic air pollution has declined substantially across the United States, but wildfire smoke may be offsetting these gains. PM2.5 exposure increases the risk of adverse birth outcomes, and wildfire-related PM2.5 may be more toxic than PM2.5 from other sources. Yet limited data exist on spatiotemporal changes in population-level prenatal wildfire PM2.5 exposure. Existing studies have characterized geographic patterns of wildfire contributions to ambient PM2.5 but have not estimated population-based prenatal exposure at the national scale, a perspective essential for allocating public-health resources to the people most affected. Methods We linked daily county-level PM2.5 data from 2,842 counties (2003–2019) to national birth certificate records covering 64.5 million live births. Using chemical transport modeling, we separated wildfire-related from non-fire PM2.5 and computed period-averaged exposure for baseline (2003–2005) and endline (2017–2019). We assessed trends across nine NOAA climate regions, demographic subgroups, and counties stratified by wildland-urban interface (WUI) status and perinatal healthcare availability, and quantified prenatal exceedance days above 35 µg/m³. Results Prenatal PM2.5 declined from 12.82 to 8.45 µg/m³ (−34.1%), but non-fire PM2.5 declined more steeply (−37.2%), indicating that wildfire offset a portion of these gains. Wildfire's share more than doubled (3.71% to 8.19%), with 0.22 µg/m³ of improvement foregone nationally. Fire-attributable exceedance days were essentially unchanged (∼14%–15% of infants) while non-fire exceedance fell 85%; wildfire now accounts for 65% of remaining events. WUI, low-income, and American Indian/Alaska Native infants bore disproportionate burdens, and maternity hospital shortage counties had wildfire contributions nearly double those of adequate-access counties by 2017–2019. Discussion Increasing wildfire activity is systematically offsetting prenatal air quality gains from anthropogenic emission reductions, disproportionately burdening low-income, rural, and Indigenous communities, with the potential to further erode progress as wildfire activity intensifies.

## Introduction 

Over the past several decades, the United States achieved substantial improvements in ambient air quality, largely due to effective regulatory policies implemented under the Clean Air Act (1, 2). Between 1970 and 2022, combined emissions of six common pollutants declined by 78 percent, while concentrations of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) decreased by 39 percent between 1990 and 2010 (3). These improvements have yielded substantial public health benefits, particularly for vulnerable populations including infants, whose health at birth is particularly sensitive to air pollution exposures experienced in the prenatal period (4, 5). 

However, emerging evidence suggests that progress in air quality improvements has slowed across much of the country, coinciding with increased wildfire activity across North America (6–8). Wildfires can account for as much as 40% of ambient PM2.5 concentrations during peak fire years in the Western US (9). Since the mid-2010s, wildfire smoke has influenced annual PM2.5 patterns in nearly three-quarters of contiguous US (CONUS) states, offsetting up to 25% of air-quality gains achieved in earlier decades (8). This reversal corresponds to approximately four years of lost national air quality progress, consistent with documented increases in wildfire intensity and size attributed to warmer and drier conditions, historical land management practices, and expanding human development at the wildland-urban interface (WUI) (10, 11). 

Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that PM2.5 from wildfire may be more toxic than equivalent doses of ambient PM2.5 from other sources (12–14), particularly with respect to respiratory health outcomes (15). Toxicological studies have linked the higher toxicity of wildfire-related PM2.5 to distinct chemical characteristics, including higher carbonaceous content and a greater capacity to induce oxidative stress (16, 17). 

Health measured at birth (e.g., birthweight and gestational age) is particularly sensitive to air pollution exposures experienced in the prenatal period (5). Recent studies have linked wildfire smoke exposure during pregnancy to increased risks of preterm birth (18, 19), low birth weight (20–22), and a broader set of perinatal and early-childhood health outcomes (23–25). The underlying mechanisms may involve enhanced maternal-fetal inflammatory responses, oxidative stress pathways, and alterations in placental function (26). Wildfire smoke contains elevated concentrations of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) generated by incomplete biomass combustion. PAHs can cross the placental barrier, bind aryl hydrocarbon receptors in placental and fetal tissues, induce oxidative DNA damage, and disrupt placental vascular development — pathways linked to reduced fetal growth, preterm delivery, and altered neurodevelopment (27–30). Because wildfire smoke carries higher PAH and organic-carbon content than typical ambient PM2.5 from non-fire sources, these mechanisms may be amplified relative to background exposure (26). Adverse birth outcomes are associated with persistent health and cognitive impairment throughout the life course, which underlies the particular public health importance of prenatal wildfire smoke exposure (4). 

No studies to date have comprehensively evaluated how prenatal exposure to PM2.5 among live-born infants has changed 

over time and space at the national scale, or the relative importance of wildfire in shaping these trends. While previous work has documented spatiotemporal patterns of wildfire smoke and its contribution to PM2.5 levels, these studies have characterized wildfire pollutants across time and space rather than estimating population-based exposure, which is critical for developing efficient public health mitigation strategies. Our population-based approach to prenatal exposure is particularly useful to policymakers and practitioners that must allocate scarce resources that benefit people rather than land areas; a population-based perspective is especially important in the infant health context where efficient targeting of resources is needed given the high cost and regionalization of intensive neonatal services (31). 

Using state-of-the-art chemical transport modeling and national birth certificate data (2003–2019), we provide a national-scale assessment of trends in prenatal PM2.5 exposure over nearly two decades and document the contribution of increased wildfire activity. We examine these patterns across geographic regions and population subgroups defined by race/ ethnicity, county income, urbanicity, and WUI status. We further characterize how these trends differ by perinatal healthcare resource availability, an important dimension given the regionalization of neonatal services. We quantify the population burden of prenatal exceedance days and use shiftshare decomposition to distinguish whether trends reflect changes in air quality where people already live or shifts in where births occur. 

## Methods 

### Air pollution data 

We used the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) model (version 5.3.3) to simulate PM2.5 at 12 km spatial resolution over CONUS for 2003–2019. Simulations were driven by emissions and meteorological inputs from the EPA’s Air Quality Time Series Project (EQUATES) (32, 33). 

To estimate the contribution of fire-related emissions to ambient PM2.5, we conducted two CMAQ runs: (1) an allemissions baseline incorporating all emission sources, and (2) a “no-fire” sensitivity run in which all biomass burning emissions within the modeling domain were zeroed out. The no-fire run excluded U.S. wildfires as well as wildfires in Canada and Mexico, and also excluded domestic agricultural burning and prescribed fires. The difference between the two runs was used as our estimate of fire-attributable PM2.5 (34–36). Consistent with the literature, we refer to this quantity as “wildfireattributable” PM2.5, because wildfire smoke is the predominant component and the dominant driver of the rising trend in fireattributable PM2.5 over our study period, although the underlying CMAQ attribution also captures other, smaller sources of biomass burning such as agricultural and prescribed fire (8, 34). 

We regridded the 5 km NASA SEDAC Gridded Population of the World (GPW) v4 population data (37) onto the 12 km CMAQ CONUS grid using inverse distance weighting (IDW)

Because SEDAC releases GPW at five-year intervals (2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2020), annual population fields for intermediate years were estimated by linear interpolation between adjacent releases. County-level daily PM2.5 was then computed as the populationweighted mean across CMAQ 12-km grid cells assigned to the county. 

We estimated three exposure measures for each pregnancy: total PM2.5 (PM2.5-T); non-fire PM2.5 (PM2.5-NF), representing exposure that would have resulted without wildfire; and wildfirerelated PM2.5 (PM2.5-WF), defined as PM2.5-T minus PM2.5-NF. 

### Study population 

The study population included all live-born infants recorded in the national birth registry between 2003 and 2019, the full period covered by the EPA EQUATES emissions inventory. These records, compiled from birth certificates by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, include nearly all live births in the US, totaling 64.5 million births (3.79 million annually). Birth data were linked with air pollution data based on the mother’s county of residence at time of delivery. For each infant, daily PM2.5 exposure was calculated and averaged over the prenatal period, determined by subtracting gestational age from the delivery date. Gestational age was based on the obstetric/clinical estimate (OE), available on the NCHS natality file for both the 1989 and 2003 birth-certificate revisions and used throughout our 2003–2019 study period. States adopted the 2003 revised birth certificate on a rolling basis through 2016, but the OE measure is available across all states and years with one exception: California in 2004–2006, where the clinicalgestation field was missing. For these California-year cases, OE was imputed via regression on a quadratic in LMP-based gestational age, maternal race, maternal age, and state and year fixed effects, following the data-cleaning protocol described in (31). We did not switch between LMP and OE measures based on calendar year. Consistent with NCHS coding conventions adopted in 2014, gestational age was top- and bottom-coded to 17–47 weeks throughout the study period. Birth records report year-month of birth, and we assumed births occurred on the 15th day of the birth month, consistent with the convention used in prior population-level analyses of NCHS public-use natality data (38), which suppresses exact date of birth to protect confidentiality. Multiple births, births with implausible gestational ages (<20 or >45 weeks), and records with missing geographic information were excluded (approximately 4% of records). 

Maternal race/ethnicity was derived from birth certificate records. We harmonized race/ethnicity across the 1989 and 2003 birth certificate revisions to create consistent categories: nonHispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, Asian/Pacific Islander, and American Indian/Alaska Native (AIAN). The 1989 revision collected race and Hispanic origin as separate items, while the 2003 revision allowed multiple race reporting; we used bridged-race categories provided by the National Center for Health Statistics to ensure comparability over the study period. 

This study used de-identified birth certificate data obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) under a 

restricted-use data use agreement. The University of Maryland Institutional Review Board determined this study to be exempt from full review (45 CFR 46.104(d)(4)) as it involved only secondary analysis of existing de-identified administrative data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 

### Geographic coverage 

The analytical domain encompassed 2,842 counties across 48 states within CONUS, organized according to nine climatologically distinct regions established by NOAA: Northeast, Southeast, South, Ohio Valley, Upper Midwest, Northern Rockies and Plains (NRP), Southwest, Northwest, and West (39, 40). 

### Trend estimation and counterfactual analysis 

Because wildfire PM2.5 varies substantially year-to-year reflecting episodic fire activity, we avoided comparing singleyear endpoints and instead averaged exposure over three-year baseline (2003–2005) and endline (2017–2019) periods to smooth year-to-year volatility while preserving a meaningful interval over which to assess change. The 2006–2016 years were not omitted: they were used in the annual linear-trend regressions and in all year-resolved time-series figures (Figures 1, 4, 5). The baseline/endline contrast summarizes cumulative change between two stable periods; the annual trend regressions and time-series figures use the full 17-year series. Within each period, we averaged birth-weighted (i.e., weighted by birth counts so that counties with more births contribute proportionally more) PM2.5 values across years. We also estimated annual linear trends via regression of birth-weighted PM2.5 on year for each region and subgroup. 

To quantify the impact of increasing wildfire activity, we computed a counterfactual: the endline PM2.5 that would have prevailed had wildfire contributions remained at baseline levels. Specifically, counterfactual endline PM2.5 = endline PM2.5-NF + baseline PM2.5WF. The wildfire offset is defined as observed endline PM2.5-T minus this counterfactual—representing the additional PM2.5 exposure attributable to the increase in wildfire activity. 

### Subgroup analysis 

To characterize population-level exposure disparities, we computed trends and counterfactuals across demographic and geographic subgroups: maternal race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, Asian/Pacific Islander, AIAN), county income level (terciles of per capita income from the CDC Social Vulnerability Index, defined nationally across all 2,842 counties), urbanicity (NCHS 2013 Urban-Rural Classification: metropolitan vs. non-metropolitan), and WUI status. For each subgroup, births were assigned to all counties where the subgroup had births, and birth-weighted average PM2.5 exposure was computed using the same periodaveraging approach.

### Perinatal healthcare resource assessment 

We augmented our analysis with county-level data on perinatal healthcare resources to characterize wildfire PM2.5 exposure trends in areas with limited healthcare access. Drawing on methods described in Boudreaux et al. (31), we classified counties using two binary indicators of perinatal healthcare shortage: (1) whether the nearest maternity care hospital to the population-weighted county centroid was low volume (fewer than 500 annual births) or geographically isolated (more than 30 miles from the nearest alternative hospital), and (2) whether the nearest Level 3 or 4 neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) was more than 60 miles from the population-weighted county centroid. These measures capture aspects of healthcare access not fully reflected by standard urban/rural classifications. Because the healthcare resource data represent a single crosssection, we treat these designations as time-invariant over the study period.

### Statistical analysis 

Temporal trends were estimated using ordinary least squares regression of annual birth-weighted PM2.5 on calendar year. Statistical significance was assessed at _α_ = 0.05. For the subgroup analyses, we calculated the percent change between the baseline and endline periods to estimate the relative change in PM2.5 and wildfire-related PM2.5 concentrations over time. Given the descriptive intent of these stratified comparisons, we did not apply multiple comparison corrections; results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. We stratified all primary analyses by perinatal healthcare shortage indicators and tested for differences in PM2.5 trends and wildfire contributions using birth-weighted averages and linear trend regressions. 

## Results 

### Descriptive overview 

Over the full study period, 3.79 million infants were born annually across 2,842 counties (Table 1). The average prenatal PM2.5-T was 10.43 µg/m<sup>3</sup> , of which 0.44 µg/m<sup>3</sup> (4.22%) was attributable to wildfire. The Ohio Valley had the highest average PM2.5-T (12.12 µg/m<sup>3</sup> ) and the Southwest the lowest (6.72 µg/m<sup>3</sup> ). The relative share of PM2.5-WF was highest in the NRP (7.63%) and Southeast (6.31%). Annual births ranged from 66,361 in the NRP to 667,646 in the Southeast, underscoring the importance of birth-weighted analysis. 

We quantified the number of infants experiencing at least one prenatal day when ambient PM2.5 exceeded 35 µg/m<sup>3</sup> , the 24 hour National Ambient Air Quality Standard (NAAQS; this standard was unchanged by the January 2024 EPA revision, which lowered only the annual NAAQS from 12 to 9 µg/m<sup>3</sup> ). For each infant, we determined whether any exceedance day was fireattributable—defined as a day when PM2.5 exceeded the threshold with wildfire included but would not have exceeded it without wildfire contributions. 

### Shift-share (Kitagawa) decomposition 

To assess whether changes in prenatal PM2.5 reflect changes in air quality within the counties where births occur, as opposed to shifts in the geographic distribution of births across counties, we employed a Kitagawa decomposition (41). Nationally and within each climate region, we decomposed the change in birth-weighted average PM2.5 between baseline and endline into three additive components: (1) a place effect, capturing changes in county-level air quality while holding the distribution of births across counties fixed; (2) a migration effect, capturing changes in the distribution of births across counties while holding county-level air quality fixed; and (3) an interaction term (42, 43). Here “place” refers to the county of maternal residence; the decomposition is computed across counties and then summarized nationally and by climate region. 

### Trends in prenatal PM2.5 and the wildfire offset 

Average daily prenatal PM2.5-T declined from 12.82 µg/m<sup>3</sup> in the baseline period (2003–2005) to 8.45 µg/m<sup>3</sup> in the endline period (2017–2019), a reduction of 4.37 µg/m<sup>3</sup> or 34.1% (Table 2, Figure 1). However, non-fire PM2.5 declined more steeply—from 12.34 to 7.76 µg/m<sup>3</sup> (4.58 µg/m<sup>3</sup> ; 37.2%)— indicating that a portion of the gains from anthropogenic emission reductions were offset by increasing wildfire activity. 

Nationally, had wildfire contributions remained at baseline levels, endline PM2.5 would have been 8.23 µg/m<sup>3</sup> rather than 8.45 µg/m<sup>3</sup> —an additional 0.219 µg/m<sup>3</sup> (2.60%). Wildfire’s share of total prenatal PM2.5 more than doubled, from 3.71% at baseline to 8.19% at endline (+4.49 percentage points). While PM2.5-WF varied substantially year-to-year reflecting episodic wildfire, the upward trend was persistent (Figure 1). 

Regional variation was notable (Table 2). The Northwest experienced the largest wildfire offset: 0.674 µg/m<sup>3</sup> . Without increasing wildfire, prenatal PM2.5 would have declined by 14.7% (non-fire trend), but the observed decline was only 6.6% —wildfire erased 7.90 percentage points of improvement, more than half the non-fire gains. Wildfire’s share in the Northwest surged from 5.11% to 13.34%. The West showed a similar pattern: a wildfire offset of 0.478 µg/m<sup>3</sup> , with wildfire’s share rising from 3.68% to 9.68%. ### Prenatal exposure by perinatal healthcare resources 

Among the 2,842 counties in our study, 57% had a maternity hospital that was either low volume or geographically isolated, and 39% were located more than 60 miles from a Level 3/4 NICU. ## Discussion 

The US made important progress in reducing prenatal PM2.5 exposure during 2003–2019. The average daily prenatal PM2.5 

declined by 4.37 µg/m<sup>3</sup> (34.1%), benefiting the vast majority of the 3.79 million infants born annually. However, these gains would have been even larger without increasing wildfire contributions. We estimate that increasing wildfire activity offset 0.219 µg/m<sup>3</sup> (2.60%) of the prenatal PM2.5 reduction nationally, with offsets exceeding 5% in the Northwest and West (Table 2). Our counterfactual analysis estimates that had wildfire contributions remained at baseline levels, endline PM2.5 would have been 8.23 µg/m<sup>3</sup> rather than 8.45 µg/m<sup>3</sup> . This offset was largest in the Northwest (0.674 µg/m<sup>3</sup> , representing 7.90% of non-fire improvement foregone) and West (0.478 µg/m<sup>3</sup> , 5.20%). 

Perhaps the most striking finding is the approximate twothirds reduction in prenatal exposure to at least 1 exceedance day over the past 2 decades—a remarkable public health achievement driven largely by Clean Air Act regulations and related emission reduction policies. Yet this reduction in total exceedance was accompanied by a pronounced compositional shift: while total exceedance fell by roughly two-thirds, fireattributable exceedance was essentially unchanged, so wildfire accounts for a growing share of the exceedance days that remain. Fire-attributable exceedance held at approximately 14% of infants across the study period, and by the endline period roughly two-thirds of all remaining prenatal exceedance days were attributable to wildfire. 

The compositional shift toward wildfire-dominated exposure profiles may have health consequences beyond what PM2.5 mass concentrations alone would suggest. Wildfire-derived PM2.5 contains higher concentrations of organic carbon and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) associated with greater oxidative stress and inflammatory responses (12, 16, 17). Subgroup analyses were descriptive and unadjusted, reflecting our aim to characterize the population-level distribution of exposure rather than to estimate causal effects; characteristics such as income and rurality overlap in important ways and should not be interpreted as independent contributions.

Our stratification by perinatal healthcare resources reveals a compounding vulnerability: communities with the largest wildfire contribution increases are often the same ones with the least access to specialized perinatal care. Counties with maternity hospital shortages experienced wildfire contributions nearly double those of adequate-access counties by 2017–2019 (10.57% vs. 7.62%), and counties far from NICUs saw contributions reaching 11.52%. These communities—often small, rural, and in the western US—face rising air pollution burdens from wildfire smoke coupled with limited access to the specialized obstetric and neonatal care needed to manage pollution-related pregnancy complications. Prior work has documented that these healthcare-shortage areas tend to have fewer OB-GYN providers, greater distances to maternity hospitals, and greater distances to NICUs (31), suggesting limited capacity for clinical response to wildfirerelated health risks. This finding extends our population perspective beyond demographic subgroups to identify the healthcare infrastructure dimension of wildfire exposure disparities. 

Our shift-share decomposition clarifies that the dominant channel for changing prenatal exposure is within-county air quality change, not population redistribution. WUI expansion does not appear to be a primary driver of changing prenatal PM2.5 exposure at the regional scale. The wildfire emissions themselves must be addressed through fuel management, prescribed burns, and climate adaptation (48). 

Our study is the first to take a comprehensive populationbased perspective on the long-term evolution of prenatal air pollution exposure, documenting how wildfire contributions have changed across all regions of CONUS. Our birth-weighted approach emphasizes human impact rather than geographic coverage, providing estimates directly relevant to public health resource allocation. The counterfactual framework quantifies the specific cost of increasing wildfire activity in terms of foregone air quality improvement. Our stratification by perinatal healthcare resources adds policy-relevant context about which communities face the greatest combined burden of rising wildfire exposure and limited healthcare access. 

Several limitations warrant consideration. County-level residence at birth may not capture residential mobility during pregnancy (49, 50). Our chemical transport modeling approach for wildfire attribution involves uncertainties related to emissions inventories and model parameterizations (34–36). Alternative approaches have different limitations (51, 52), and no consensus exists on optimal wildfire attribution methods. We could not account for behavioral adaptations that may modify actual exposures, and these protective behaviors are likely unequally distributed across socioeconomic groups. Our perinatal healthcare resource measures are time-invariant and may not capture facility openings, closures, or volume changes during the study period; however, this approach describes 

current resource availability relevant to ongoing clinical and policy decisions. For gestational age, OE was missing for California in 2004–2006 and was imputed via regression on LMP-based gestational age and other covariates, following the data-cleaning protocol described in (31); affected births represent less than 3% of the study population, and sensitivity to this imputation is expected to be small given that exposure is averaged over a 250–300 day pregnancy. 

## Conclusions 

While the US achieved important progress in reducing prenatal PM2.5 exposure during 2003–2019, increasing wildfire activity systematically offset a portion of these gains, with wildfire’s share of prenatal PM2.5 more than doubling nationally. Approximately two-thirds of remaining prenatal PM2.5 exceedance days are now attributable to wildfire, and the burden falls disproportionately on low-income, rural, and Indigenous communities. Approximately 630,700 infants per year are born in counties with maternity hospital shortages where wildfire contributions exceeded 10% by the end of the study period. 

Traditional emission reduction policies, based on the regulation of anthropogenic sources, will not be sufficient to address wildfire-related exposures and it is unclear whether US policymakers will prioritize strategies that will reduce the occurrence of mega-fires that have widespread population impacts. Healthcare providers in fire-prone regions should incorporate wildfire risk assessment and education into prenatal care protocols, with particular attention to communities with limited perinatal healthcare infrastructure. Enhanced air quality monitoring, early warning systems, and the distribution of climate adaptation technologies (air filters, masks, and clean air shelters) may help mitigate wildfirerelated PM2.5 burdens.
