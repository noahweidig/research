# Is Wildfire Planning Evolving to Support Communities? Taking Stock of 20 years of Community Wildfire Protection Plans in the state of Colorado, United States

## Abstract

Over the past two decades, wildfire policies at the national level have sought to catalyze locally led wildfire mitigation and forest management, while promoting collaboration across sectors. In the United States, Community Wildfire Protection Plans (CWPPs) have become one of the primary tools for local wildfire mitigation planning. CWPPs continue to be developed and funded, yet little is known about how CWPPs have evolved in response to changing wildfire vulnerability and advancing knowledge about wildfire risk. We investigated trends in the scope, participation, and mitigation content included in these plans through a review of plans published between 2004 and 2023 in the state of Colorado. Our review finds a notable pattern of stability: scope, participation, and mitigation priorities remain largely consistent over two decades of CWPP development. We found that fewer CWPPs are being developed, and recent plans primarily consist ofupdates to previous plans, trends which seem mainly driven by updated standards and funding requirements. A consistent set of institutional actors have been involved in CWPPs over time, with few trends indicating an expansion in the diversity of plan participants. We also observed a strong focus on fuels reduction, but an increased percentage of plans devoted attention to community preparedness, data collection/monitoring, and policies/standards, although the proportion of actions dedicated to each area remained consistent over time. Drawing on existing adaptive governance and wildfire planning literatures, we speculate on the lack of changes observed over time and reasons why these plans may not have evolved but still provide value to communities under certain conditions. This research seeks to enhance our knowledge of facilitative and inhibiting factors associated with bottom-up collaborative planning.

#### **Abstract** 

Over the past two decades, wildfire policies at the national level have sought to catalyze locally led wildfire mitigation and forest management, while promoting collaboration across sectors. In the United States, Community Wildfire Protection Plans (CWPPs) have become one of the primary tools for local wildfire mitigation planning. CWPPs continue to be developed and funded, yet little is known about how CWPPs have evolved in response to changing wildfire vulnerability and advancing knowledge about wildfire risk. We investigated trends in the scope, participation, and mitigation content included in these plans through a review of plans published between 2004 and 2023 in the state of Colorado. Our review finds a notable pattern of stability: scope, participation, and mitigation priorities remain largely consistent over two decades of CWPP development. We found that fewer CWPPs are being developed, and recent plans primarily consist ofupdates to previous plans, trends which seem mainly driven by updated standards and funding requirements. We also observed a strong focus on fuels reduction, but an increased percentage of plans devoted attention to community preparedness, data collection/monitoring, and policies/standards, although the proportion of actions dedicated to each area remained consistent over time. Drawing on existing adaptive governance and wildfire planning literatures, we speculate on the lack of changes observed over time and reasons why these plans may not have evolved but still provide value to communities under certain conditions. This research seeks to enhance our knowledge of facilitative and inhibiting factors associated with bottom-up collaborative planning.

## **1 Introduction** 

Over the past few decades, wildfires around the world have increased in size, intensity, severity, and frequency (Burke et al. 2021, p. 1; Westerling 2016, p. 5). In the United States (U.S.), these fire events are largely due to a combination of Euro-American settlement-era logging and grazing; removal of Indigenous fire stewardship and aggressive suppression of wildland fire by federal and state governments; expanding human population and development into 

fire-prone areas; and changing climatic conditions towards warmer temperatures and increasing occurrences of drought (Coop et al. 2022, pp. 1953–1956; Parks et al. 2023, pp. 10–12; Pausas and Keeley 2021, p. 390; Calkin et al. 2014, p. 747; Radeloff et al. 2018, p. 3316; Abatzoglou and Williams 2016, p. 11773; Wasserman and Mueller 2023, pp. 10–12). As a result of more extreme wildfires, individuals, communities, and governments are increasingly experiencing additional hazards associated with wildfire, including wildfire smoke, post-fire flooding and debris flows, and

As wildfire activity has intensified and increased globally, many countries have enacted policies to spur changes in wildfire risk mitigation and management at both national government and community levels (Huidobro et al. 2025, p. 18; Pandey et al. 2023, p. 8). Recent policy changes in wildfire governance have shifted from centralized approaches dedicated to fire suppression to advocating for more decentralized efforts that emphasize a wider range of mitigation strategies (e.g., prescribed burning, capacity building) (Gillson et al. 2019, p. 9; McWethy et al. 2019, p. 799; Schoennagel et al. 2017, pp. 4586-4588; Stephens et al. 2014, pp. 117-118). Recognizing that wildfires often burn across jurisdictional boundaries, greater attention has also been devoted to addressing wildfire as a cross-boundary or transboundary issue that will require collaborative governance approaches across geographic and jurisdictional scales (Charnley et al. 2020; Miller et al. 2022, p. 2; Palaiologou et al. 2018, p. 511). 

As fire behavior, wildfire risk, and governance approaches have shifted over the last two decades, Community Wildfire Protection Plans (CWPPs) have emerged in the U.S. as a tool to support community-driven wildfire mitigation and coordination between local, state, and federal partners (Steelman and Burke 2007, p. 68; US Congress 2003, p. 3). Despite extensive previous scholarship on CWPPs (Abrams et al. 2016; Jakes et al. 2011; Williams et al. 2012), we have little understanding of how plans have evolved since their initial development in response to changing wildfire conditions and shifting governance perspectives. We might expect CWPPs to reflect updated scientific information, lessons learned, shifting community priorities, and evolving ideas of wildfire governance as communities adapt to changing conditions (Abrams et al. 2016, p. 568; Ulibarri et al. 2026, pp. 1–2; USGS 2025). In this paper, we evaluate how and whether the scope, participants engaged in plan development, and mitigation actions of CWPPs has changed over time, with a particular focus on the state of Colorado, U.S. 

### **1.1 Community Wildfire Protection Plans** 

CWPPs were conceived through the Healthy Forests Restoration Act (HFRA) in 2003, representing a planning process aimed at wildfire risk reduction in communities and on adjacent federal lands (US Congress 2003). Since the inception of CWPPs, over 1,000 CWPPs have been developed in 11 western states, with increasing adoption of plans over time and significant variation in scale and participation depending on the state (Hamilton et al. 2023, p. 3; Palsa et al. 2022, p. 2). Planning theory suggests that plans, like CWPPs, can be beneficial for several reasons, including raising awareness 

of policy issues, directly addressing policy problems, holding organizations accountable, and enhancing collaboration around these issues (Escobedo Garcia and Ulibarri 2022, p. 476). For example, CWPPs can unlock access to funding opportunities, help streamline and expedite fuels reduction projects under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), and facilitate landscape-level management (Bennett et al. 2025, p. 10; Jakes and Sturtevant 2013, pp. 1141– 1142; Toman et al. 2013, p. 34). 

### **1.2 Guidance for developing CWPPs** 

CWPPs are guided by three criteria within HFRA, which include being collaboratively developed between local entities and other relevant partners (federal, state, county, etc.), prioritizing areas for fuels reduction, and outlining recommendations to reduce structure ignitability (US Congress 2003). CWPPs were intended to be developed by and for communities, but the intended audience and what “community” means in these planning processes has remained unstated beyond HFRA’s requirement that representatives from the local government, local fire authority, and state forestry agency be involved (Daurio et al. in review, pp. 17–18; US Congress 2003). HFRA’s criteria are not overly prescriptive, and this flexibility may allow communities to identify relevant local risks and tailor mitigation priorities to the local context (Jakes et al. 2011, p. 361). However, this lack of clarity may also drive stasis and homogeneity in plans over time. 

In response to HFRA, some states developed additional recommendations for community wildfire planning. Colorado is one of few states in the U.S. to formally create their own minimum standards to provide additional guidance. Colorado’s minimum standards were originally created in 2009 and a result of Senate Bill 09 − 001, which required every county in Colorado to develop a CWPP for unincorporated areas (Gibbs et al. 2009). These standards were updated in 2022 and provided more guidance to address wildfire risk and mitigation more broadly (e.g., consider fire history, evacuation planning, social vulnerability), as well as include an implementation plan (Colorado State Forest Service 2022). According to the updated minimum standards, CWPPs in Colorado are also recommended to be updated every five years to be prioritized for state-level funding (Colorado State Forest Service 2022).

More recent research has focused on reviewing the content within these documents across a larger number of plans (Abrams et al. 2016; Colavito 2019; Palsa et al. 2022). ### **1.4 The importance of documenting CWPP trends over time** 

These prior studies contributed significantly to existing knowledge of CWPP processes and outputs, but after two decades of CWPPs, we lack a more holistic understanding of trends across CWPPs, including the scope and scale of mitigation actions and factors that may shape plan content (Palsa et al. 2022, p. 16). CWPPs are intended to be flexible, which may provide a forum for exchanging ideas and lessons learned from one CWPP to another (Hamilton et al. 2023, p. 9). As vulnerability to wildfire and knowledge about how community wildfire risk reduction has changed (Calkin et al. 2023, p. 2; Maranghides et al. 2022, pp. 1–2), we might expect CWPPs to evolve, innovate, and adapt to meet shifting community needs. 

CWPPs remain the dominant wildfire mitigation planning tool for wildland-urban interface (WUI) communities in the U.S. (Palsa et al. 2022, p. 2; Steel and Wolters 2025, p. 1824), and there has been renewed interest federally to invest in and support CWPP development in the wake of 

unprecedented recent fire seasons (e.g., Community Wildfire Defense Grant Program- CWDG). As renewed funding prompts another phase of CWPP development and implementation, additional research assessing the quality of these plans is warranted to enhance the utility of CWPPs. This study aims to take stock of two decades of CWPPs in Colorado and answer these research questions: (1) What is the scope of CWPPs in Colorado? (2) Who has been involved in developing these plans? (3) What types of mitigation strategies are proposed? and (4) How have patterns in the scope, participation, and content of these plans changed over time. To understand how CWPPs have evolved, we assessed trends in several indicators over time related to the scope (type of plans, geographic range), participation (consultant involvement, number and diversity of participants), and content (proportion of actions devoted to different aspects of mitigation, proportion of actions addressing implementation details) included in these plans over time. Although we expected to observe some changes over time in CWPPs, we found the plans and involvement to be remarkably stable over the past two decades. We speculate on why we observed this surprising lack of change over time in this paper, as well as the implications of this stasis/stability for Colorado and other wildfire impacted landscapes. 

## **2 Methods** 

### **2.1 Study area and rationale** 

We focus specifically on CWPPs that have been developed in Colorado for several reasons. Over the past 20 years, a large number of plans have been developed (about a fourth of CWPPs from western states are from Colorado), and the spatial level of plans varies considerably across the state (Palsa et al. 2022, p. 9). Colorado has also experienced multiple large and destructive wildfires in the past two decades and is characterized by a variety of landownership and socioeconomic contexts (Courtney et al. 2025, p. 825). The Colorado State Forest Service (CSFS) maintains a publicly accessible database of CWPPs developed over time (Colorado State Forest Service 2025). The research presented here is part of a larger study focused on wildfire mitigation planning and implementation in Colorado.

### **2.3 Data collection and analysis: identifying and categorizing CWPP Participants** 

To identify trends in consultant involvement and participation, we drew upon an existing dataset of CWPP participants (Palsa et al. 2022, p. 6). Palsa and colleagues (2022) collected data on each plan’s participants and their respective organizational affiliations from plans drafted between 2001 and 2021 across 11 western states, including Colorado (p. 6). To identify participant data, the authors reviewed each CWPP for any mention of participation, including participant lists, and categorized each participant by broader affiliation categories (e.g., county organization, state forest service), as well as higher level codes (e.g., individual, NGO) (see Palsa et al. 2022 for additional details). Entities were grouped into 17 affiliation categories (see Supplementary Material for full list). For our study, we also reviewed the additional plans developed in Colorado in 2022 and 2023 that were not included in the original study ( _n_ = 23) and collected participant data using the same approach described above. 

### **2.4 Data collection and analysis: identifying and categorizing CWPP wildfire mitigation strategies** 

A team of 10 researchers systematically reviewed a subset of CWPPs (205 of the 314 plans) published within Colorado from 2004 to 2022 to identify the types of mitigation actions proposed and degree of implementation considered. While basic plan information, including plan participants, was considered for all 314 plans, due to the time required to manually code each plan, we included a subset of CWPPs from the larger sample in this analysis (205 of the 314 plans). The team of researchers deductively coded the actions within these CWPPs into 15 overarching mitigation topics, or parent codes, that were adapted from the Fire Adapted Communities (FAC) Learning Network Framework (Fire Adapted Communities Learning Network 2025). This framework was chosen because of its basis in wildfire social science research (Paveglio et al. 2024, p. 197) and comprehensive itemization of components. The researchers then iteratively co-created a list of 59 types of strategies, or child codes, nested within these larger categories. Table 1 includes a list of all parent and child class codes employed (see Supplementary Material for child code descriptions). Mitigation actions were also categorized based on the implementation details and degree of intentionality described, which included: (1) _Unplanned_ if describing a belief or conviction about how fire mitigation should be carried out without specifying actions; (2) _Recommended_ if mentioning an action that is discrete but lacks details about how this action will be implemented; and (3) _Planned_ if describing an action with language that indicates there is a plan to undertake the action (e.g., sources of funding, timelines, etc.). 

To maintain consistency, two researchers coded each plan through a process of consensus coding. One researcher was assigned as the primary coder and reviewed each document, identifying all locations where specific actions were mentioned and coding the verbatim text into categories of mitigation topics and strategies. Once the primary coder finished their review, a second coder reviewed the verbatim text highlighted from the initial review and identified relevant mitigation topics and strategies independent of the primary coder’s review. Both coders then worked together to resolve any discrepancies in coding. 

### **2.5 Descriptive analysis** 

Upon completing this coding, we imported the data into R and conducted descriptive analyses to characterize trends in the scope, participation, and content of CWPPs within our sample (R Core Team 2025). We grouped the plans and associated data into three time periods: 2004–2009, 2010–2016, and 2017–2023. The first period roughly corresponds to the initial pulse of CWPP development following the passage of HFRA, the second period covers years of declining numbers of plans produced, and the third period marks increased planning activity, though not at the scale of plans developed during the first period. To assess the scope of CWPPs over time, we calculated the number of plans by year and the percentage of each type of plan (county, fire protection district, community/homeowners association), updated plans, counties with CWPPs, and plans by 

CSFS region. To assess trends in participation over time, we recorded the number of participants by plan and the percentage of plans involving consultants and representatives from different organizations/affiliations. To assess trends over time in CWPP content, we calculated the number of types of mitigation topics and strategies by plan, as well as the percentage of plans that addressed each mitigation topic and strategy, the percentage of actions devoted to each mitigation topic and strategy, and the percentage of unplanned, recommended, and planned strategies. Trends are reported as the percentage of plans addressing each topic, with the number of plans provided for transparency. Percentages are calculated based on the full sample of 314 CWPPs for results related to the scope and participation within CWPPs and out of 205 CWPPs for mitigation topics and strategies, unless otherwise noted. 

## **3 Results** 

### **3.1 Trends in the scope of Colorado CWPPs** 

We found that a large number of CWPPs (50% of the total pool) were published between 2007 and 2011 (see Fig. 1), with 2007 ( _n_ = 41) and 2011 ( _n_ = 43) as the top years for CWPP creation. Moreover, all but one of these plans were new plans (one was an update to a previously developed plan (see Supplementary Material for more detailed information). The development of new plans decreased following this initial period of plan development. A visual inspection revealed no clear relationship between the timing of CWPP development and major fire events at the state level (see Supplementary Material for a list of the fires included in Fig. 1). Updated CWPPs only accounted for 14% of our sample, with an average of 11 years between the original CWPP and the updated plan. We observed an increase in the development of CWPP updates over time compared to first generation CWPPs, with updates constituting about 50% of plans published between 2017 and 2023 and most published between 2021 and 2023 (Table 2). Of the 314 plans, 56% of plans were at the community-level, 25% were at the fire protection district-level, and 19% were at the county-level. We observed a gradual increase in the proportion of community-level plans developed over time (Table 2), but all three types of plans continued to be adopted. 

Of the 64 counties in Colorado, 91% have developed at least one CWPP. 75% have developed county-level CWPPs, but only 44% of counties have homeowners associations that have developed CWPPs, and 28% of counties have fire protection districts that have developed CWPPs. ### **3.3 Trends in mitigation actions in Colorado CWPPs** 

Of the subset of CWPPs included in in-depth coding ( _n_ = 205), we identified 16,577 unique risk mitigation actions, ranging from six actions in one plan to 766 in a single CWPP, with an average of 81 mitigation actions per plan. The average number of actions in plans varied by the type of plan, with community plans having about half the number of actions (59 actions) as county (117 actions) and fire protection district CWPPs (116 actions). Across plans, an average of 10 mitigation topics/areas (e.g., fuels reduction, education, etc.) were covered by plans in our sample. The average number of wildfire mitigation strategies within these overarching topics (e.g., developing evacuation plans, creating defensible space, etc.) was 24 types of strategies, which is similar across types of plans. We observed no consistent trends over time in the number of unique actions, mitigation topics, and wildfire mitigation strategies within CWPPs (see Supplementary Material).

## **4 Discussion** 

### **4.1 Summary of observed trends** 

We expected to see changes over time in CWPPs, but our results revealed few trends in the scope, participation, and mitigation content within these plans. Over time, we might expect CWPP adoption to spread across the state, as these policy tools become normalized and diffuse to neighboring regions (Bassett and Shandas 2010, p. 436; Berry and Berry 2018; US Congress 2003). However, the few trends that we observed related to the scope of CWPPs seem to be driven by policy standards and funding requirements. We 

```

```

368 

found a decrease in the rate of development of new CWPPs over time, with peaks in 2007–2011, which may have been driven by enthusiasm to adopt these tools following the passage of HFRA and changes to minimum state standards that mandated counties develop a CWPP (Gibbs et al. 2009). CWPPs developed in recent years have mainly been updates to earlier plans, which may be partly driven by updated state minimum standards requiring that CWPPs be updated every five years to be eligible for funding (Colorado State Forest Service 2022). Similar updates to minimum standards and additional federal funding for the development and implementation of CWPPs (e.g., CWDG Program) may spur future pulses of CWPP development. 

As wildfires increase in severity and impact critical infrastructure (e.g., water supply) (Rhoades et al. 2025, p. 16; Tunby et al. 2025, p. 10), we might expect increasingly diverse stakeholder participation in CWPPs to reflect broadening risk perception. While we found that an increasing percentage of plans over time involved representatives from nonprofit organizations and local/regional agencies (e.g., water conservation districts), these groups and most types of NGOs were still involved in developing less than a third of CWPPs over time. Our findings suggest that values-atrisk are not driving more diverse participation, which aligns with prior scholarship on participant diversity in CWPPs (Palsa et al. 2022, p. 12). We also found that institutional actors (federal and state agencies, fire departments, and county organizations) were consistently involved in most plans across the study period, while the share of community members involved in CWPPs decreased over time. This aligns with earlier research on CWPPs in Colorado (Absher et al. 2018a, p. 28). This finding raises questions about the perceived value of CWPPs by different groups and how these factors may influence participation or disengagement. In a recent study of CWPPs in Colorado, people involved in CWPP development felt that most CWPPs were not useful for community members because they were overly technical/academic (Bennett et al. 2025, p. 10). This evidence suggests that declining participation by residents over time may be due to perceptions about the target audience for these plans (i.e., institutional actors) and lack of utility for community members. 

While CWPPs were originally proposed to address hazardous fuels, we might expect that plans over time might align with calls to address a wider range of vulnerabilities (Moritz et al. 2022, p. 2). Our review revealed a strong, consistent focus on fuels reduction over time, which aligns closely with historic western WUI wildfire management in the U.S. (Abrams et al. 2016, p. 567; Absher et al. 2018b, p. 16; Jakes et al. 2011, p. 361). However, we also found an increasing percentage of plans proposed actions related to policies/standards, community preparedness, and data 

collection/use related to wildfires over time, with some plans strongly focused on homeowner preparedness. These trends contrast with an earlier review of CWPPs that found little emphasis on community preparedness for fires and structural requirements, restrictions, or other strategies aimed at altering homeowner behavior (Abrams et al. 2016, p. 567). However, the authors acknowledged that future plans might move beyond fuel treatments and contain a more diverse range of mitigation actions. Our findings may indicate that CWPPs are starting to adapt and reflect growing recognition that wildfire mitigation must move beyond suppression (Calkin et al. 2023, p. 2; Maranghides et al. 2022, pp. 1–2), but fuels reduction strategies are still the focus. 

Another notable trend is the decreasing attention to funding and personnel over time, even as extensive evidence demonstrates how critical both are to mitigate wildfire risk (Courtney et al. 2025, p. 833; Cheng and Dale 2020, p. 679; Huber-Stearns et al. 2022, p. 946). While funding is often necessary to overcome financial barriers, having personnel with existing grant application and management experience, as well as dedicated time, is also key to administering funded projects (Courtney et al. in review; Shybalkina 2024, pp. 295–296). Personnel are also crucial to building trust and relationships across key stakeholders to enact landscape level mitigation projects. Less attention towards securing funding and hiring personnel may result in a mismatch between the scope of work outlined in CWPPs and necessary economic and human capital to facilitate planned projects, which may be especially challenging in lower capacity communities (Courtney et al. in review). 

### **4.2 Potential explanations for the absence of observed trends** 

Contrary to our expectations, we observed no clear trends over time across CWPPs for several elements of scope, participation, and mitigation content. The lack of changes observed over time is surprising given the substantial evolution and innovation employed in environmental governance within the state of Colorado, like collaboratives focused on strategically planning for wildfire and forest restoration activities (Huayhuaca and Reid 2019, p. 7). “Next generation CWPPs” are another approach recently introduced in Colorado that involve more sophisticated modeling of fire risk, deeper community engagement, considerations for evacuation planning and post-fire impacts, and features to make the plans more useable (e.g., linking with existing planning documents) (Ember Alliance (2026). 

We have several potential explanations for why more trends were not apparent and how outputs developed within comparable institutional environments may become increasingly similar over time. Structural factors, such as

top-down government requirements, state-level approval, and funding requirements, may influence the scope and content of these documents (DiMaggio and Powell 1983, p. 150). Though plans in Colorado must also adhere to the state’s minimum standards, these standards are still general enough, like HFRA, that we might expect communities to tailor their plans to their existing socio-ecological conditions (Jakes et al. 2011, p. 361). While we observed some changes in the percentage of plans that addressed mitigation topics over time, the proportion of strategies devoted to different areas remained largely consistent, with the vast majority of actions devoted to fuels reduction and alignment with HFRA requirements. Our findings indicate that flexibility may not provide a sufficient mechanism for change, which aligns closely with prior research about how ambiguity around national wildfire policy requirements can result in disconnects locally (Franz et al. 2024, pp. 10–12; Mosso et al. 2026, p. 13). Within other hazard mitigation and land use planning contexts, evidence suggests that top-down federal and state mandates may result in plans that meet only minimum standards or compliance (Butler et al. 2021, p. 378; Escobedo Garcia and Ulibarri 2022, p. 486; Horney et al. 2017, p. 62), which may also be reflected in our findings. 

Limited requirements around participation in CWPP development may also limit who is engaged in these plans and the scope of mitigation content addressed in these documents. While CWPPs were intended to benefit communities, HFRA guidelines only reference institutional actors necessary for inclusion in CWPP development (U.S. Congress (2003). These requirements notably exclude community members and provide little clarity on facilitating collaborative planning processes (Daurio et al. in review; Mosso et al. 2026, p. 10). When top-down regulations require collaboration as an outcome without specifying how to achieve this, evidence suggests planning processes across contexts insufficiently integrate stakeholders (Perrone et al. 2023, p. 8; Escobedo Garcia and Ulibarri 2022, p. 486). These existing requirements may shape CWPP composition in standardized ways, potentially unintentionally excluding feedback from a wider range of relevant stakeholders (e.g., nonprofits, tribes, community members). A recent study also found low levels of local stakeholder participation in wildfire planning processes and that different planning approaches (CWPPs, general plans, and hazard mitigation plans) were not well aligned (Zhang et al. 2025a, pp. 10–11). If participation in CWPPs has been limited to the same institutional actors and siloed from other relevant planning, few new ideas may be introduced and priority will likely remain on fuels reduction, which is a primary objective for institutional entities and signatory authorities. Our findings may be indicative of broader wildfire governance challenges related to institutional silos, uneven participation across stakeholder groups, 

and disconnects between differing scales of wildfire policies beyond Colorado (McLennan and Handmer 2012, pp. 1–5; March 2016, pp. 170–171). 

While not the only incentive for developing CWPPs, grant eligibility is often cited as a motivating factor behind plan development (Bennett et al. 2025, p. 10; Palsa et al. 2022, p. 3). Fuels reduction projects continue to be prioritized for funding through federal initiatives like the Infrastructure and Investment in Jobs Act, which funded the CWDG program and other initiatives aimed at implementing the Wildfire Cohesive Strategy (Charnley et al. 2023, p. 283; Paveglio and Edgeley 2023, p. 370; USDA (USDA 2025). If wildfire plans are mainly driven by grants and designed to target funding requirements, we would expect little changes in CWPP content if funding requirements continue to prioritize fuels reduction (Dale and Barrett 2023; USDA 2025; Steelman and Burke 2007, p. 70), which may promote stasis rather than adaptation. 

Though no “one-size-fits-all” approach to dealing with wildfire risk or developing CWPPs exists (Bennett et al. 2025, p. 15; Jakes et al. 2012, p. 2; Toman et al. 2013, p. 7; Williams et al. 2012, p. 418), our understanding of what constitutes an “effective” CWPP across different contexts remains limited. Resources exist that highlight best management practices or lessons learned for CWPP development (Jakes et al. 2012; Williams et al. 2012), but there has been little research into effective elements of CWPPs and conditions associated with more useful plans (Bennett et al. 2025, p. 6; Mosso et al. 2026, pp. 13–14). Uncertainty related to socio-ecological conditions and effective strategies may cause organizations to model their approaches off what others have already done to reduce risks associated with trying something new (DiMaggio and Powell 1983, p. 151). This modeling of others’ approaches may promote stasis in wildfire plan development and content, limiting cross-plan learning, diffusion of innovative approaches, and adaptation in the face of change. The lack of guidance may be especially challenging for some communities in developing wildfire plans, especially for communities where leaders may have less technical expertise (Absher et al. 2018b, p. 13; Bennett et al. 2025, p.10) and may help explain the lack of trends. 

We also acknowledge that our data and analyses may not have captured certain trends in CWPP development and content. Most of the plans we reviewed were developed prior to Colorado’s revised minimum standards in 2022, so future research may capture the evolution of these plans as influenced by additional state guidance. Planning documents also may not always capture nuances associated with development, such as the degree of involvement of different actors (Palsa et al. 2022, p. 16). In some cases, there might also be a strategic imperative to include certain kinds of

### **4.3 Implications of stasis/stability in plans** 

Our findings about the lack of evolution of CWPPs over time raise important questions about these policy tools and other natural resource management plans: should environmental plans evolve alongside changing hazards, conditions, and governance approaches? Or if these plans are not “broken,” is stability desirable? The lack of change in CWPPs over time is not inherently a problem, as evidence suggests that persistent, stable policy tools can reduce uncertainty and potentially enhance coordination around similar documents (DiMaggio and Powell 1983, pp. 150–151). Regularly updating existing plans can also be more burdensome than beneficial if entities are updating to meet requirements, rather than incorporating lessons learned (Escobedo Garcia and Ulibarri 2022, p. 481). However, in environmental and natural resource management contexts characterized by changing conditions, plans may be most effective when they align with the existing social-ecological context and local values (Epstein et al. 2015; Folke et al. 2007). Existing research suggests that environmental plans are implemented and used more when they are updated regularly over time to reflect environmental changes, shifting community priorities, and iterative learning (Absher et al. 2018b, p. 16; Steel and Wolters 2025, pp. 1823–1842). Static plans risk becoming shelf documents, losing buy-in/credibility and inadequately preparing communities to address changing conditions. 

Wildfire planning exists within systems that have changed drastically since the early 2000’s, as many places around the world grapple with larger, more intense fire events coupled with additional hazards (e.g., wildfire smoke, post-fire flooding) (Burke et al. 2021, p. 1; Westerling 2016, p. 5). In response to wildfire events and evolving understandings of effective governance, many countries have shifted their policies to address wildfire mitigation more broadly and integrate planning across scales (Gonzalez-Mathieson et al. 2020, pp. 8–9; Huidobro et al. 2025, p. 18; Pandey et al. 2023, p. 8). While concerns have been raised about better aligning existing wildfire policies in the U.S. around existing social and ecological conditions (Steelman 2016), our results suggest that CWPPs in Colorado are not adapting to evolving conditions. CWPPs appear to be driven by structural factors and addressing baseline compliance, rather than substantive plans fitting the local context (Ulibarri et al. 2026, p. 6). 

### **4.4 Recommendations for fostering innovation in future wildfire planning** 

To encourage bottom-up planning that evolves with changing conditions and fits the local context, we provide some suggestions to improve CWPPs based on our findings. Future CWPPs should continue to move beyond a focus on fuels reduction goals and propose a wider array of mitigation approaches (e.g., evacuation planning, infrastructure improvements), which our findings indicate is becoming more common in recent plans. These types of strategies, especially related to the built environment, have been found to be most effective for reducing community wildfire risk, yet receive the least funding and support (Dale and Barrett 2023, p. 4). Planning teams should also continue to consider the importance of addressing funding and personnel-related strategies within these documents, as both are crucial to implementing proposed projects. Additional flexibility and diversification in federal and state wildfire mitigation priorities and funding streams could incentivize a wider range of proposed wildfire mitigation projects, especially for smaller communities with lower capacity (Courtney et al. in review). Some examples could include an increased focus on funding activities related to structure protection, creation of WUI codes, and post-fire recovery. 

While HFRA was intentionally vague to provide flexibility for CWPPs to fit the local context, our findings indicate that the flexibility afforded by federal and state standards may constrain further plan development, exclude certain audiences, and be insufficient to promote innovation. More directives to broaden participation beyond institutional actors and suggestions on how to engage certain groups (e.g., watershed councils, nonprofits, residents) could make these plans more locally relevant and substantive. Better integration with other wildfire planning tools (e.g., hazard mitigation and watershed plans) may also broaden participation across sectors and scales and promote innovative approaches that address a wider scope of vulnerabilities. External factors, like the threat of losing insurance coverage, could also be leveraged to motivate more community members to get involved in CWPP development and enhance individual homeowners’ risk reduction (Athena 2025). Additional guidance for wildfire planning may be most appropriate coming from states, as existing research has shown that content within CWPPs reflected state guidance more than the local context or national policies (Ulibarri et al. 2026, p. 3). 

We also recognize that CWPPs are one of many planning approaches around the world that aim to reduce wildfire vulnerability, such as Australia’s bushfire plans, Forest Intervention Zones in Portugal, and Native Forest Land Use Planning Programs (NFLUPP) in Argentina (Brummel et al.

Within the US, communities may also benefit from considering alternatives or options that can be coupled with CWPPs depending on timing, resources, and other contextual factors. Some communities in Colorado have developed Community Assessments because they take considerably less time and do not require official approval (Bennett et al. 2025, p. 10). Community Assessments were perceived as a helpful “first step” for communities to build their adaptive capacity prior to completing a CWPP. In other states, general plans and hazard mitigation plans also address wildfire hazards and may do so more comprehensively than CWPPs (Zhang et al. 2025b, p. 10). CWPPs will likely continue to be a tool for local wildfire mitigation planning, but this research suggests that greater attention is needed to enable innovation in the face of shifting wildfire behavior, vulnerability, and governance approaches.
