# “Turn the faucet”: right-wing hydrosocial imaginaries of irrigation water in the Western United States

## Abstract

The second administration of United States President Donald Trump has taken an authoritarian, centralized approach to governance in the United States. Water management is one area in which the president has attempted to show dominance. This research theorizes President Trump’s concept of water in the United States West as a “hydrosocial imaginary” in which the release of water from rivers and reservoirs indicates virility, action, and power, instead of liberal cautiousness and prudence. By ordering the release of two billion gallons of water from California’s Central Valley into the dry Tulare Basin one hundred miles north of Los Angeles for the purpose of battling the 2025 Los Angeles wildfires, Trump’s claim to have redirected water “down from the Pacific Northwest” into California to alleviate drought evokes an imaginary Western landscape of underexploited resources, instead of over-allocated river systems in water-stressed areas. Using content analysis of historical and contemporary newspaper articles and other relevant materials, this article first examines both parallel and contradictory rhetorical structures between Trump’s idea of water transfers and those of the past one hundred years. The article further refines this examination through semi-structured interviews of irrigators and water managers in the Pacific Northwest, demonstrating the differences between hydrosocial imaginaries of the Trump administration and those of actual water practitioners. The article concludes that Trump’s concept of what is possible in the development of hydraulic infrastructure represents a top-down technocratic authoritarian imaginary that echoes failed historical attempts at megaprojects. This approach is removed from present-day conditions in the United States West, and ignores the localist and regionally-grounded approaches of water practitioners. Overall, these findings suggest that the politics of water, agriculture, and natural resources do not settle comfortably along right- and left-wing polarities, but instead diverge between imaginaries that center localism and those that take a technocratic-authoritarian approach in relation to their views on potential for change to water systems, resource availability, power, and imagined connectivity.

## 1 Introduction 

During the 2024 government shutdown, federal agencies under the United States Executive Branch posted messages to their websites noting the various cessation of services. Most included innocuous statements—for example, the Bureau of Land Management’s message stated, “Due to the current lapse of federal appropriations: this website may not be up to date; transactions submitted via this website might not be processed until appropriations are enacted; and we may not be able to respond to inquiries until appropriations are enacted” (Bureau of Land Management, 2025). However, a few agencies included heavily politicized statements, including the United States Department of Agriculture’s National Institute of Food and Agriculture (NIFA): “Due to the Radical Left Democrat shutdown, this government website will not be updated during the funding lapse. President Trump has made it clear he wants to keep the government open and support those who feed, fuel, and clothe the American people” (National Institute of Food and Agriculture, 2025). 

NIFA’s statement reveals agriculture as a primary locus of Donald Trump’s politicizing agenda. This politicization mobilizes the judiciary, the military, and agriculture for the purposes of a nativist, authoritarian agenda, categorizable as far right and intended to prosecute Trump’s personal vendettas. This agenda views politics as a friends-and-enemies field where the “radical left” represents enemies of the people obstructing a nationalist golden age. Furthermore, the “radical left” is conflated with “urban-based imaginaries of rural areas,” (Hommes et al., 2019) both of which are set up as targets for the Trump administration. Our study seeks to identify whether and how the turn in Trump’s politicization of resource management represents a rift in the imaginative geographies of the rural United States West, one that cuts across political divisions. Answering this question offers insight into the United States far right as it pertains to resource management and agriculture. In particular, we focus on water resources, a key input for irrigated agriculture which has become particularly politicized under the Trump administration. 

The Trump administration has situated agriculture within the discourse of war, targeting regulations as “agricultural lawfare” and developing a collaborative agency called the Office of Research, Economic, and Science Security that links the Department of Defense and the United States Department of Agriculture (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2026). Despite hazards induced by climate change, like forest fires and drought, the Trump administration has revoked scientific findings that greenhouse gasses cause climate change (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2026). To resolve the impacts of hotter, drier weather in California’s agriculturally productive Central Valley, the Trump administration has made statements advocating for interstate water transfers from the Pacific Northwest, which would require a vast infrastructure of pipelines traversing over a thousand miles—a solution generally considered unfeasible by experts (Center for Environmental Law and Policy, 2025). 

Statements from the White House in recent years involving water resources broadly, and interbasin water transfers in particular, have indicated an imaginary of the United States West consistent with the “high modernist,” settler colonial approach observed during the middle of the twentieth century (Worster, 1985). Emblematic of this imaginative geography is Trump’s famous statement on western water transfers, in which he conceptualized water 

systems as “essentially a very large faucet” (Judd, 2024) that can be turned on or off at will. Water managers, regardless of political leaning, largely recognized this characterization as incorrect and infeasible. This raises the possibility that, although the Trump administration is classified as a far right government (Mudde, 2022), its authoritarian-modernist approach does not universally represent those who identify as politically right wing, especially when it comes to resource management decision making. In this research, we critically examine the assumption that a unified “far right agricultural policy” exists when it comes to water resources and interbasin transfers. We interrogate imaginaries of water resources from across the political spectrum to better understand the tensions and negotiations between local and technocratic approaches, and how these approaches represent diverging hydrosocial imaginaries around agriculture and water. 

We ground our work in a hydrosocial perspective in which water and society are considered relational, co-constituted, and power-laden (Linton and Budds, 2014; Ross and Chang, 2020). As Boelens et al. (2025) assert, conflicting hydrosocial imaginaries “are central to most hydrosocial territorial studies” (p. 434). This involves “reimagining and discourse battles” (p. 439). Given the central importance of water for agricultural production, hydrosocial systems are inextricably connected with agri-food systems, and many struggles over hydrosocial imaginaries and territories are simultaneously about the future of agricultural production. 

To understand how different hydrosocial imaginaries are being leveraged and politicized, we conducted a three-part study to analyze the relationship between the Trump administration’s hydrosocial imaginaries of the United States West, historical hydrosocial imaginaries, and contemporary hydrosocial imaginaries of local water professionals in the region. The first part includes a content analysis of historical newspaper articles, speeches, and quotations about centralized hydrological infrastructure projects from the 1950s through the 1970s. The second part includes qualitative, semi-structured interviews of water professionals in the Pacific Northwest. The third part involves a content analysis of Trump’s speeches about water resources in the United States West. Altogether, this three-part study offers a contextualized understanding of Trump’s modernist approach and responses from regional water practitioners grounded in prior, modernist water regimes. 

Our research finds important contradictions between the multiscalar hydrosocial imaginaries held by different people at varying levels of power and authority in agricultural and water policy, and it examines how these contradictions operate within the water-food-energy nexus. We conclude by suggesting that federal water policy should build on, encourage, and facilitate local water professionals’ self-organization and locally coordinated water infrastructure projects, rather than attempting to impose centrally managed mega-projects.

### 2.2 Semi-structured interviews 

Second, we conducted semi-structured interviews with water professionals in the Pacific Northwest. To locate contacts, we used the Washington State Water Resources Association’s 2025 Directory, reached out to existing regional contacts, and searched the web for Oregon irrigation districts in the Rogue and Willamette River valleys. In total, we reached out to 72 contacts in the states of Washington and Oregon with a preliminary email, followed up by email if they did not respond, and then followed up with a phone call. Of these efforts, nine contacts responded positively to an interview. Eight of these interviews were conducted via Zoom and one conducted via email (at the request of the respondent) between October 2025 to January 2026. Twothirds of the interviewees were irrigation district managers, and the remainder included a conservationist, a public official, and a municipal water manager. 

We crafted our interview questions around three research questions of interest (Table 1). First, we wanted to understand what regional water professionals thought about the feasibility and desirability of interstate water transfers from the Pacific Northwest to California’s Central Valley. This interested us because it offered information about the agreement of opinion between federal and regional 

water policy goals. Next, we explored the imaginaries of the United States West held by the water managers by asking questions about how they envisioned hydrosocial connections between the Pacific Northwest and California’s Central Valley. Finally, we asked questions related to the water professionals’ political identification and theory of power—i.e., how they preferred to operate across political, ecological, and social scales. 

Among respondents, we found a gloomy political environment in the regional agricultural sector. At least two interviewees told the researchers that the funding environment had become more precarious under the present administration, since irrigation districts and watershed groups often depend on federal money from regulatory agencies. As one interviewee reported, “the risk profile associated with federal dollars; it’s, beginning to be too much,” referring to political pressures from the authoritarian bureaucracy of the Trump administration, which rescinded funds allocated to irrigators under the prior Biden administration, canceled the fruits of a years-long negotiation over salmon protection on the Columbia and Snake Rivers, fired myriad federal employees working on such issues, and leveled punitive tariffs that hurt domestic agriculture, contributing to a steep rise in farm bankruptcies (Ayoub, 2026). ### 2.3 Analytical framework: hydrosocial imaginaries and hydrosocial territorialization 

We utilized the conceptual framework of “hydrosocial imaginaries” to analyze the archival data and interviews. The concept of imaginaries plays a crucial role in the theorization of hydrosocial systems, but little consensus exists on its meaning. An October 2025 search query on Web of Science for “hydrosocial AND imaginaries” returned 35 peer-reviewed articles. These papers alternately referred to imaginaries as shared visions of the future (Mulvaney et al., 2025; Flaminio and Reynard, 2023; De Jong et al., 2024), semiotic interpretations of the present (Büscher, 2019), an ontological sense rooted in particular histories (Hommes et al., 2022), a function of values that informs governance (Berry and Cohn, 2023), or all of the above (Hommes, 2022). While a complete literature review of hydrosocial imaginaries lies beyond the scope of the present study, we provide here a brief comparative study of hydrosocial imaginaries. Based on the literature reviewed, we define hydrosocial imaginaries as one or more sets of perceived relations between a given human society and water. 

Few papers in the literature offer conceptual genealogies of imaginaries, making it difficult to locate the provenance of the term in relation to hydrosocial theory. Hommes et al. (2022) helpfully refer to two twentieth century scholars’ use of imaginaries, Edward Said and Benedict Anderson: while Anderson (2006) writes about the development of community based on imaginary linkages, Said (1978) describes the processes by which imperialist European academics created an image of the Orient for their readers’ imaginations. Other scholars (Xu and Swyngedouw, 2023; Manosalvas et al., 2023; Gort, 2024) base their understanding of imaginaries on the work of Cornelius Castoriadis, whose book _The Imaginary Institution of Society_ effectively defines social imaginaries as coherent simplifications of frustratingly complex social phenomena (Castoriadis, 1987, p. 143). Generally, we find that these early, scholarly interpretations view “the imaginary” as an essential, psychological basis for the human consciousness of place, time, and society. 

Social imaginaries can be understood as critical sites of contestation. Approximately one-fifth of our articles on hydrosocial imaginaries utilized definitions of socio-technical imaginaries given by Sheila Jasanoff. While Jasanoff offers two definitions (Jasanoff, 2015, p. 4; Jasanoff and Kim, 2009, p. 120), which offer some nuanced differences, her consistent understanding of the socio-technical imaginary involves the ordering of space and the leading of everyday life. A number of the papers in our survey also utilized Foucauldian models of understanding the ordering and leading of space and life, as exhibited by the terms “governmentality” and “biopolitics” (Bleeker and Vos, 2020, p. 225; Hommes et al., 2016, p. 18). Together, these concepts highlight the changeable aspects of the imagination through its relation to power, subjectivity, and other social relations. 

A related analytical framework consists of the idea of “hydrosocial territories” and territorialization. Hydrosocial territories, as envisioned in Boelens et al. (2016) and further clarified in Hommes et al. (2016), “views water flows and management as physical, social, political and symbolic matters, entwining these domains in particular configurations, actively constructing and producing territoriality in techno-political and socio-ecological interactions” (p. 11). In the context of irrigation and agriculture, hydrosocial territories are multiscalar systems. Hydrosocial territories include a number of integrated scales, such as time and place, as well as society (from the individual to the collective), politics (jurisdictional powers from municipality to

The integration and interaction of these scales and the power structures involved manifests complex, adaptive systems in which interdependent actors co-evolve in response to recurring hazards and difficulties (Cohen, 2016; Lansing, 2003). Given the intertwined nature of the symbolic and the imagination (Hommes et al., 2016; Lacan, 2011), we assert that a hydrosocial imaginary contributes to the production of hydrosocial territories as a phenomenological whole, comprehended in reference to symbolic matters along with interwoven physical, social, and political characteristics. 

For this reason, the development of water infrastructure, like pipelines, to transfer water for large-scale irrigation projects takes part in the process of territorialization and hydrosocial imaginaries as they pertain to biopolitics. As Lopez et al. (2019) write, “Construction of hydraulic infrastructure to materially control access to, and exclusion from, water flows establishes control by one group over another and reinforces or challenges established power structures” (p. 268). Similarly, Zhou et al. (2024) demonstrates how “Ecological Modernization and Authoritarian Environmentalism” (p. 2) can impact hydrosocial territories by imposing “territories-in-territory” and “territorial pluralism” (p. 7). These studies and contexts reveal that new infrastructural features within a hydrosocial territory can complicate social imaginaries and cause biopolitical conflict. 

Because social imaginaries are subtle, labile, and the subject of constant negotiation, the struggles that emerge due to alterations in the infrastructural landscape can take the form of discourses and efforts to reimagine human-natural relations (Boelens et al., 2025). As Xu and Swyngedouw (2023) note in reference to this concept, “social imaginaries, for instance, the alternative imaginaries, can also be subversive in nature, offering new paths for addressing societal contradictions, or they may seek to transgress or fundamentally transform the existing social order” (p. 3). It appears to us, then, that hydrosocial imaginaries comprise _one or more sets of perceived relations between a given human society and water_ , insofar as perception is informed by subjective-phenomenological processes that contribute and are adapted to ideological formation. 

Hence, we find high modernist, neoliberal, and localist hydrosocial imaginaries to exist in overlapping and sometimes competitive relation to one another. Modernism tended to “produce abstract knowledge that claimed universality and objectivity by denying the social conditions of its own production,” leading to a hydrosocial imaginary that excluded locally-important ontologies (Liao and Schmidt, 2023, p. 244). Contrary to the modernist approach is a deeply-rooted hydrosocial imaginary corresponding to a lived relation to time and space and often identified with indigenous peoples (Berry and Cohn, 2023). Whereas the modernist approach develops hydrosocial imaginaries suitable to top-down tasks (technocratic planning of infrastructure, water allocations, etc.), the localist hydrosocial imaginaries tend to perceive social relations to water in light of coordinative processes that include and benefit the impacted communities in which they live. Neoliberal hydrosocial imaginaries have attempted to synthesize the two through a transactional approach to water as a commodity (Feldblum, 2025), furthering a modernist agenda by fostering local consent and often creating messy outcomes by resorting to authoritarian measures when consent is not obtained (Hommes et al., 2019). 

Hence, high modernist imaginaries tend to involve purely technocratic administration through a highly abstract conceptualization of social relations to water; localist and/or indigenous imaginaries 

tend to regard social relations to water as far more intimate, immediate, and integrated in their material lifeworld; and neoliberal imaginaries often accept the abstract concept of water without fully extracting social integration from the equation. Together, these imaginaries are often construed as urban, in the case of technocracy, and rural in localized contexts. However, urban water struggles, authoritarian, extractive movements in rural areas, and the seeking of local consent by neoliberal administration all tend to complicate the dichotomy in relation to high modernism, which does remain almost exclusively urban. What is needed, then, is further research to understand the intersections and conflicts between such differentiated types of hydrosocial imaginaries. 

### 2.4 Situating hydrosocial imaginaries of the United States west in the current political moment 

Repurposing Said’s notion of an Orientalist “imaginative geography” of the East, our study brings the above analytical frameworks together to study imaginative geographies of the United States West (Said, 1978, pp. 68–73). The United States far right tends to envision “the West” as an idealized historical space of Classical civilization, patriarchy, and spiritual dominance (Turner, 2008). The United States West, in particular, has been imagined as an open frontier for utopian expansion and dominion driven by agriculture and the mastery of water under the banner of settler colonialism and white supremacy (Worster, 1985). However, the imagined geographies of the United States West represent contested visions of the past and the future with different governance models representing local collaboration and centralized coercion. 

We understand the Trump administration to be a far right government. The far right is a political association of radical right and extremist actors, organized in this case in the form of a political party (Mudde, 2017). Although different far right groups, movements, and parties maintain different policies on a variety of issues, they largely consolidate around nativism and authoritarianism. While the far right often seeks to engage within the institutional structures of government, they may also use extra-parliamentary political violence and ultimately desire the fundamental transformation or destruction of governmental institutions. 

In reference to the United States West, water policy is one issue on which far right actors diverge—even when part of the same general organization or movement. Trump’s leading constituent base is located in rural areas, such that suburban voters who support Trump often identify as rural rather than suburban. In this regard, the rural identity is often characterized as localized, communitybased, and based on personal rather than economic relationships. Hommes et al. (2020) describe how the urban and rural—and the hydrosocial imaginaries surrounding agriculture—become intricated in relational dynamics through which boundaries are set and often renegotiated. Manosalvas et al. (2023) deploy similar categories in relation to hydrosocial territories of urban and rural, distinguishing between “social imaginaries” of external actors and “local imaginaries of place.” 

The rural and urban are characterized as sites of locally-rooted communities and external, “social” governments, respectively, indicating that their relationality remains defined by the imposition of the “social” onto the “local.” Hommes et al. (2019) further describe the relationship between urban and rural in light of neoliberal

Thus, the urban imposition of administrative and regulative biopolitics against rural hydrosocial territories is also a manifestation of conflicting imaginaries of those territories, as distinguished by practices of “soft” participatory, rural and local management or hard, sovereign, and social governance. 

Within the above clash of imaginaries, urban power is understood as external, modernist, technocratic, sovereign, and liberal, while rural power tends to be coded as internal, rooted and authentic defenders of local sovereignty. In some cases, the far right has tended to offer a similar construction, leveraged against the perceived imposition of environmental regulations, licensing requirements, gun laws, taxes, and civil rights by the federal government. Seminal texts of the postwar United States conservative movement oppose modernist, topdown management in favor of decentralized political organization aimed at gaining and keeping rural constituents (Kirk, 1953; Goldwater, 1960). The clearest example of decentralized, rural resistance to environmental regulation can be found in the militia movement, which has opposed—sometimes violently—representatives of federal regulatory agencies and regional planning (Helvarg, 2004; Levitas, 2004). 

The more centralized, modernist, and authoritarian administrative approach of the second Trump administration to agricultural and water policy has created the need for a new conceptual model to understand a political condition in which the two parties offer dueling modernisms that no longer oppose one another across the axes of technocracy and localism. Some of the previously applicable binaries no longer apply in the current moment. For example, the framework of rural and urban as exploited and exploiter is problematized by rural participation in environmentally harmful activities and resistance to regulations. Meanwhile, the ideation of neoliberalism as complicit in the application of authoritarian sovereignty from the top-down must contend with the rise of authoritarian sovereignty expressed by localist, rightwing militias from the bottom-up, which opposes both federal authority and local ecological activists. Furthermore, anti-neoliberal authoritarian conservatism can emerge with top-down technocratic plans that antagonize both neoliberals and localists from left and right, alike. The Trump administration deploys a centralizing, authoritarian, and modernist form of power. We argue that this is entirely opposed to post-modern neoliberalism as well as the kind of old-fashioned localism championed by traditional conservatism. 

Our study contributes to a better understanding of these political dynamics as both new and also historically rooted. We examine the tensions between local, collaborative and participatory frameworks created to govern water in the context of legislative and institutional struggles against and within neoliberal administrations, on the one hand, and the return to authoritarian, technocratic modernism by Trump on the other hand. In doing so we show how right and left polarities neither persist nor dissolve within contestations over modernist water planning, but instead shape new forms of competing hydrosocial imaginaries.

### 3.1 Historical hydrosocial imaginaries of large-scale western water transfers 

##### 3.1.1 Agriculture, energy, and water collide: the united western investigation 

Hydrosocial territories and imaginaries were core to the settlement of the West, broadly speaking, by white Euro-American settlers. While this process has been well documented in the context of California and the Colorado River system (e.g., Worster, 1985), irrigation projects in the Pacific Northwest, from the Klamath in Southwestern Oregon to the Grand Coulee Dam in Northeast Washington, were conceptualized and carried out from the late 1800s onward to great fanfare. In 1947, Assistant Secretary of the Interior William Warne sponsored research into the feasibility of a massive western water transfer project that involved significant water-power tradeoffs, making it highly controversial in the Pacific Northwest (The News-Review, 1950). From Warne’s impetus, the Bureau of Reclamation created the United Western Investigation (UWI) as an effort “to explore the possibility of diverting large volumes of surplus water from the Northwest southward, thousands of miles, to the arid Southwest” (The News-Review, 1950). The UWI grew to a staff of twenty at their headquarters in Salt Lake City, with field offices in Boise, Idaho, Sacramento, California, and Boulder City, Nevada. The field offices mapped onto four regions comprising twelve western states. However, the avoidance of field offices in Southern California and the Pacific Northwest, where the UWI hoped to focus the redistribution of irrigation waters, evidenced a strangely dislocated spatial distribution of field offices (Hill, 1950). 

Print news media generally framed the investigation in the context of large infrastructure projects involving dams, hydroelectric power, and especially irrigation. As the _New York Times_ reported, “the transfer presumably would be accomplished by a large-scale system of engineering works that would pump and channel water for hundreds of miles through mountain ranges from the coastal region inland” (Hill, 1950). During its early period of investigation, officials imagined a “ladder” bringing water from the Klamath River to Sacramento, substituting the Klamath water with water from the Rogue River, and replacing that with water from the Umpqua (The Bend Bulletin, 1950b). 

Although the UWI pitched itself as an agriculturally-focused project, the terms eventually shifted to municipal water, as local water was to be freed up for residential use through its replacement by out-ofstate water. A UWI press release called for the utilization of “surplus and waste waters” in the Pacific Northwest, described as the water left “after a supply for all possible future demands of the northwest has been reserved.” As the project continued, speculation increased as to water transfers from the Snake River and the Columbia River north of Oregon (The Bend Bulletin, 1950b). 

The leader of the UWI was an engineer named Stanford P. McCasland. Issuing a preliminary report in 1950, McCasland declared, “such transfer would be very small in comparison to the surplus water available in the Northwest. […] It has been found that only a portion of the vast irrigable areas in the Southwest could ever show a sufficient economic return to justify the cost of bringing water

Commenting on the brief “McCasland report,” _Herald and News_ journalist Frank Jenkins (1953) called the UWI “imagination-staggering,” noting that the people of New Mexico and Arizona were “suffering increasingly from lack of water” and “beginning to fear that great climatic changes are in progress which will result in drying up that area as the Sahara was dried up eons ago.” Jenkins emphasized “the FEAR” of climate change in the Southwest, speculating that the UWI could transfer water from the Northwest to California. Far more skeptical, the Beaverton Enterprise (1950) declared, “some means of magic is being sought to tap a bountiful supply for the demands of thirsty California.” This came in a news roll that began with a section called “WAKE UP MAN AND LIVE!” in which the author mourned that “Man has become almost a helpless dependent as the march of Civilization leads on.” Lamenting the “sound of clanking chains upon our habits and thought processes” as the result of apathy coming from “our little narrow lives, our creature comforts” (Enterprise, 1950). The general tone of the newspaper section seems to support the advances of cars and electricity while insisting on a kind of rugged individualism that refuses abject conformity to government diktats. In contrast to Jenkins and the UWI, the _Enterprise_ called for delinking the Western imagination from a progressive construct of civilization, returning instead to a localized relationship with nature. 

In an article titled “The Scheme Unfolds,” _Bend Bulletin_ howled, “Each bit of knowledge […] makes more and more clear the threat to the northwest implicit in this proposal and more and more clear the need for united northwest action in opposition” (The Bend Bulletin, 1950a). Calling the UWI a “California chimera,” the _Bulletin_ declared, “The bureau should drop this united western investigation and study a few ways to economize and reduce its spending” (The Bend Bulletin, 1950a). A joint rejection of the UWI’s imaginative geographies of water as magical or monstrous was emerging in the local papers. 

The Oregon newspapers largely appear to have been negatively inclined toward the UWI, which suffered from difficult public relations and a general lack of communications. The _Bend Bulletin_ in Central Oregon stands out as the most openly opposed to the UWI’s “scheme,” while the _Beaverton Enterprise_ lodged its consternation about the “means of magic,” pushing instead toward rugged individualism. The _Herald and News_ similarly identified the UWI with “imagination-staggering” and somewhat dubious “big, world-shaking projects,” but appears to have sided with the UWI in demanding the use of surplus water. The _Oregon Statesman_ also issued a generally unsupportive editorial grounded in what they believed to be an uneven valuation between Los Angeles and the Northwest. 

Interestingly, the Oregon newspapers enlisted language of the imagination and fantasy—“imagination-staggering,” “California chimera,” and “means of magic”—to point out the problems of UWI’s abstract reasoning and technocratic approach. While some of these 

newspapers were stationed in Oregon-based cities like Salem and Bend, they can be viewed as critiquing “urban-based imaginaries about rural places” (Hommes et al., 2019) in that they critiqued technocratic hydrosocial imaginaries. Oregon newspapers used practical discourses and locally-grounded imaginaries to discredit the feasibility and desirability of the centrally-planned, federal infrastructure project. 

##### 3.1.2 Imaginaries of megatransfers: North American Water and Power Alliance 

Devised by the LA-based engineering and construction firm Ralph M. Parsons Company, the 1964 North American Water and Power Alliance (NAWAPA) proposed drawing water from the Pacific Northwest, including the Columbia, Fraser, and even Yukon Rivers, into artificial reservoirs on the way to the Southwest. Taking 70–80% of water from Canada and the rest from the riparian Pacific Northwest, the NAWAPA proposed a thirty-year construction period intended to bring 300 km<sup>3</sup> to water twenty-two million hectares in other parts of Canada, though mostly to the United States and, to a lesser extent, Mexico (Micklin, 1985). In 1965, the _New York Times_ described the “initial reception” of the NAWAPA by industrialists and government agencies as “generally good” (O’Toole, 1965). For its boosters, the NAWAPA represented a great step forward in a continental imaginary based on white settlement of the North American West, extending toward a future of economic integration. 

Like the UWI, the NAWAPA was generally framed as preventing the “waste” of “surplus water” (Oregonian, 1965a) and, in the emphatic words of United States Bureau of Reclamation head Ellis Amrstrong, refusing to “sit back and let [water] waste away to the ocean” (Kadera, 1972). Unlike the UWI, however, the NAWAPA involved distributing water not only to the Southwest but thirty-three states in total, including the Midwest and prairie states, through a complicated system of dams, tunnels, canals, and pipelines relying largely on northwestern Canadian surface water (Sewell, 1985). Unlike the UWI, the NAWAPA also proposed generating more power, paying for the water withdrawals with new hydroelectric dams, including a dam in Canada’s Rocky Mountain Trench holding a reservoir fifty times the size of the Grand Coulee Dam (Sewell, 1985, p. 21). Another open channel would be constructed connecting Alberta to South Dakota, with the ultimate desire of linking the Pacific Ocean to Lake Superior via an inland waterway (O’Toole, 1965). In their first comment on the matter, the Portland daily newspaper, the _Oregonian_ , printed an Associated Press article dramatically comparing the NAWAPA to “the Great Pyramid of Egypt” (Oregonian, 1964). 

The NAWAPA was only the grandest of several water diversion schemes presented to legislature during the mid-late 1960s. Their appeal resonated not merely on a pragmatic level but on an aspirational belief in the destiny of humanity and the desire to maintain an originary imagination perceived as endangered by modernity. Political figures like Texas Congressman Jim Wright argued for a nationalist vision of great water projects like NAWAPA, worrying that the people of the United States “may have lost some of our capacity for dreaming and acting in those areas concerning our survival upon this Earth” and adding that “we must recapture that capability if we are to survive the effects of our own careless profligacy, our wanton wastefulness in the matter of water” (Wright, 1966, pp. 221-222). Wright insisted, “the crisis of our diminishing water resources is just as severe […] as any wartime crisis we have ever faced,” calling for “bold and imaginative

From April 11–13, 1965, Parsons Company public affairs manager KD McFarland gave a talk to the Pacific Northwest Trade Association at the Portland Hilton (Oregonian, 1965a). Some conference members suspected “that the plan might be dressed up “carpetbagging”” (Hill, 1965). 

The skepticism of Pacific Northwesterners was shared by Canadians across the border. British Columbian premier WAC Bennett rebuked the Parsons Company, calling the prospect of selling water to the United States “ridiculous.” “Water is our heritage; you do not sell your heritage,” he declared (Diuguid, 1965). However, discussions continued in Oregon, with the League of Women Voters screening a movie on the NAWAPA in May (Oregonian, 1965b) and Parsons Company designer Roland Kelly appealing to the Professional Engineers of Oregon annual meeting a few weeks later (Oregonian, 1965c). 

As several other water schemes stalled in the legislature, Utah Senator Frank E. Moss became the driving force behind NAWAPA, pushing the Committee on Public Works of the United States Senate to initiate a feasibility analysis (Micklin, 1985). In November 1965, Moss joined the 80th Annual Meeting of the Oregon Horticultural Society, boosting NAWAPA as “the most imaginative approach to the water shortages in the United States and our greatest hope of solving our water problem from the Great Lakes to the Pacific Ocean and from Canada to Mexico” (Oregonian, 1965c). Despite capturing the imaginations of many in Oregon, difficulty persisted among the Canadians. 

While environmental concerns came to the fore in the 1960s, the Canadian opposition to the NAWAPA was more grounded in a nationalist imaginary that emphasized “the importance of preserving wilderness in maintaining a truly Canadian landscape that enhances the sovereignty of the Canadian state” (Reeves, 2009, p. 5). Reporting on a confrontation at the National Water Conference in January 1966, the _New York Times_ observed, “Canada Reluctant to Share Resources With United States” In fact, the parliamentary secretary to the Minister of Northern Affairs and National Resources had cried out, “do not call it North American water. It’s Canadian—it’s ours.” Implying that Canadian resistance to the enormous project was unfair and even rude, the _Times_ countered, “Many of those at the [conference] were astounded to hear such tough talk from a country that has 14 times as much available water as the United States for each citizen” (New York Times, 1966). 

In his high-stakes speech attempting to win the Royal Society of Canada to the NAWAPA in May 1966, Senator Moss appealed to the frontier histories of Canada and Utah. Drawing from a Department of the Interior publication about Utah, Moss described his home state 

as a “panorama of peaked mountains, lush valleys, and wind-swept plains, where the taming of the wilderness made ‘the history of Utah an epic of hardship, determination, and triumph.’” To this official and rugged portrayal of landscape and colonization, Moss added, “I am sure those same words could have been written about many parts of Canada.” The Utah Senator imagined Canada as a place of “timber, trapping, minerals, farming, and incredible beauty”—a land of raw materials and natural abundance conquered and tamed by western, white men. “One of the first white men to see the Great Salt Lake,” declared Moss to the gathering of academicians, “was a Canadian trapper named Etienne Provost. The first white settler in Utah came from St. Louis, whence he had come from Quebec.” Canada and Utah share the common thread of civilizing, white colonialism, joined in “the nature and intensity of our common interest in the preservation of our natural resources” (Moss, 1967, pp. 3-4). 

Due to their shared white, settler-colonial history, Moss stated that the interests of Americans and Canadians laid in preserving “the sovereign values of both our nations and our societies, and developing our respective talents constructively for the greatest good of the greatest number[…]” At the same time, both nations maintain a duty to not just preserve but “enhance” what Moss described as “a very valuable piece of real estate, a section of the earth which is conducive to life and worthy of our care and affection” (p. 5). Moss’s speech does not appear to have won many converts. In fact, it was followed by a scathing rebuke. However, it did elucidate the ideology of high modernism in its prime—one based on both utilitarian and liberal grounds in explicit reverence toward its white, settler colonial tradition. 

In spite of the Canadian resistance, NAWAPA found a muchneeded local booster west of the Cascades in Oregon’s then-Secretary of State Tom McCall, who voiced support of the project after flying over the proposed site of the Rocky Mountain Trench with three legislators from California (Oregonian, 1966). As governor the following year, McCall continued to press for studies and “intensive talks” with the Canadians, stressing the project’s “practicability” rather than its imaginative nature (Schulz, 1967). While Moss continued to appeal to the imagination (Oregonian, 1968), McCall’s terms emphasized the plan’s “logical” nature, which the Oregonian upheld in a piece referring to NAWAPA as “total water management on a continental scale” (Holm, 1967). 

The pressure from McCall and Californian politicians did little to sway their neighbors to the north, but debates around the NAWAPA continued for years. In 1968, the Oregonian described the proposal as “staggering the imagination” (Schulz, 1968), and United States officials continued to entertain the prospect on junkets both to the Pacific Northwest and to Canada. However, by the end of the 1960s, NAWAPA was effectively finished. A 1972 article in the Oregonian memorialized the project as “the most far-reaching and controversial project of the past” (Kadera, 1972), but some kept the dream alive. In 1981, a study by the editor of Water Supply & Management reported, “NAWAPA is a gigantic project and its environmental and social costs have yet to be comprehensively determined. Its international and political implications are quite enormous and have yet to be fully evaluated” (Biswas, 1978). Few remained to take up its cause during the 1980s’ assault on “pork barrel” and “fiscal irresponsibility.” Having drawn comparisons to Egypt’s Aswan Dam (Schulz, 1967, 1968) on the Nile and Soviet Aral Sea proposals (Bessey, 1972), the NAWAPA gradually faded from memory.

### 3.2 Contemporary hydrosocial imaginaries 1: Trump’s statements on western water 

Ideas of large-scale, interbasin water transfers like the UWI and the NAWAPA fell out of fashion in the late-1970s, when President Jimmy Carter targeted a “hit list” of expensive water projects to relieve pressure on the federal budget. However, they cropped up periodically during recurring droughts in California—for instance, during the 1990s and as recently as the early 2020s. Trump’s imaginative statements about transporting water into California fit into this long-term persistence of high modernist water projects, with special resonance for the long-held ideal of transferring water from Canada and the Pacific Northwest to meet the needs of California’s Central Valley and the Southwest. 

Amid a devastating wildfire season, in September 2024, Trump spent time on the campaign trail speaking about water policies, promising Californians “more water than you ever saw” (Cama, 2024). Trump’s water discourse involved piping water from Canada to the fields of California. “So you have millions of gallons of water pouring down from the north with the snow caps in Canada and all pouring down,” Trump declared at his LA golf course, “And they have essentially a very large faucet. And you turn the faucet and it takes 1 day to turn it. It’s massive” (Judd, 2024). 

The water would go to watering “all those fields that are right now barren, the farmers would have all the water they needed. And you could revert water up into the hills, where you have all the dead forests, where the forests are so brittle.” According to Trump, “the land would be damp, and you’d stop many of these horrible fires” (Cama, 2024). He continued, “Can we not just get it down to where we need it, where we have drought conditions, as in California?” However, the then-presidential candidate concluded, “but of course, the business logic is that it’s all way too expensive to do so, and there’s just no realistic business model for that” (Judd, 2024). 

On November 8, 2024, Trump criticized the State of California for “rerouting of MILLIONS OF GALLONS OF WATER A DAY FROM THE NORTH OUT INTO THE PACIFIC OCEAN, rather than using it, free of charge, for the towns, cities, & farms dotted all throughout California” (Hawkins, 2024). To stop the fires, Trump insisted, “all they have to do is turn the valve” (Gradoni and Partlow, 2025). This statement caused confusion among water managers. 

In apparent attempts to show how to “turn the valve,” Trump ordered the release of some 6,751.5-acre feet of water from the Terminus and Schafer dams into the dry Tulare Lake lakebed in the southern part of the Central Valley roughly 192 miles northwest of Los Angeles. Trump then announced via his Truth Social social media platform on January 27: 

“The United States Military just entered the Great State of California and, under Emergency Powers, TURNED ON THE 

WATER flowing abundantly from the Pacific Northwest, and beyond. The days of putting a Fake Environmental argument, over the PEOPLE, are OVER. Enjoy the water, California!!!” (Trump, 2025a) 

Despite the concerns of local farmers about the loss of large quantities of water, the lack of Pacific Northwest water flowing within the system, and the water release’s distance from the Los Angeles fires, Trump’s declaration signaled a grander construct. The imaginary project lay less in fighting the fires in Southern California than in returning to the high modernist approach to water in the West, a dream of interconnected and abundant water system flowing freely according to the will of engineers unencumbered by regulations or physical limitations. 

A few days later, Trump posted a photograph of water flowing to his account on the social media platform, X: “Photo of beautiful water flow that I just opened in California. Today, 1.6 billion gallons and, in 3 days, it will be 5.2 billion gallons. Everybody should be happy about this long fought Victory! The spontaneous release of thousands of acre feet of water into a dry riverbed almost 200 miles from the site of the fire less than one month into his presidency was now cast as a “long fought Victory.” Trump’s political success lay not merely in the water release but in a whole imaginary architecture of water transfers from the Pacific Northwest to Los Angeles. In the coming weeks, Trump would emphasize the notion of a conflict—a “little skirmish in California”—boasting that “we opened up the water in California,” which “was flowing into a place called the Pacific Ocean, which for the Pacific Ocean is only a drop but for us it’s more water that we can use in California” (Public Broadcasting Service NewsHour, 2025). Trump’s reimagining of the water release now involved a “victory” over the political enemy by “opening up water” and paradoxically preventing the dammed water from being wasted into the ocean by releasing it into a dry lakebed.

Coming out of the 1960s boom in high modernist imaginaries, the State Water Project saw the expansion of large farms and their political power, passing off most of the cost of the enormous enterprise to consumers (Hundley, 2001). 

There is something fantastical about Trump’s imagined version of the United States West as already interconnected by pipelines and canals that make the transfer of thousands of acre feet as easy and as manly as strongly turning a valve on one end to run manifold, firefighting sprinklers on the other. However, the UWI did result in the development of the State Water Project, and that project does include vast infrastructure developed by those whose imaginations mirrored that of Trump. Evoking social realist images of burly workers “strongly” torquing a valve, Trump’s imaginative geography of the United States West is reminiscent of the high modernist infrastructure boosters of the UWI who inspired the State Water Project—and the NAWAPA that sought to move beyond it. 

Regulations and water rights vanish, as arbitrary notions like “the full compliment [sic] of water” emerge from thin air absent any extant agreements. State and federal legislation regulating water usage is a wasteful, false encumbrance (“just an excuse”) to be brushed aside with as much ease as “opening up the water.” Trump’s imaginative geography of the United States West views endangered or threatened species like Sacramento Basin Smelt, which he has referred to as “an essentially worthless fish,” as part of a wasteful practice of allowing water to flow into the ocean (Scoville, 2020; Price, 2025). In relation to ecosystems, Trump’s hydrosocial geography resembles the UWI boosters’ disdain for “surplus or waste” water. 

The United States West and its complicated, interstate systems of water rights and transfers becomes a blank slate tamed and wrangled through power and pipelines. Problems caused by climate change and the uneven distribution and allocation of water resources can be resolved with individual action and bold engineering feats. Water that flows into the ocean unexploited by humans (e.g., a river that reaches 

its terminus) represents a policy failure. These features of Trump’s discourse regarding water resources follow an imaginative geography of the West typical of the high modernist planners, engineers, and politicians who supported the UWI and NAWAPA, involving a territory ranging from the Yukon and Columbia mountains down the Columbia River and flowing south through the Siskiyous and into southern California. Unlike the neoliberal rejection of state-led planning or the traditional, conservative disdain for New Deal style “great projects,” the revived high modernist imaginaries clearly favored federal intervention over state-level politics and local water rights. 

### 3.3 Contemporary hydrosocial imaginaries 2: views of water management practitioners 

Trump’s statements about sending water from the Pacific Northwest to California re-opened the discussion about large-scale, interbasin water transfers, raising concerns about the president’s plans. A few news articles rehashed the history of the UWI and the NAWAPA, and local experts responded to Trump’s contradictory claims, reminding the press of the Columbia River Treaty and the material basis for collaboration between Canada and the United States. However, no studies have yet sought to understand local sentiments among water managers in relation to the revival of an imaginary within the NAWAPA tradition. The second part of our research fills this gap. 

##### 3.3.1 Feasibility and desirability 

Almost everyone we interviewed believed that the effort to pipe water from the Pacific Northwest to the Central Valley for agricultural uses was unlikely. Two-thirds rejected the idea outright as either impossible or undesirable or both, while one-third declared their openness to hearing proposals about implementation, transfers, and compensation. Only one person, who identified as center-left, seemed interested in the potential of such a large-scale water transfer plan. This distribution of responses validated our initial appraisal that largescale water transfers do not necessarily entail right-wing natural resource management. Instead, they involve a level of centralized administration, technological coordination, and sweeping geographical design more aligned with high modernism, which resonates across various political ideologies. 

From those entirely opposed, we heard regionalist opinions about water usage. One interviewee told us: 

They’re going to need to figure it out for themselves and not rely on the Pacific Northwest, because we have […] our own drought conditions and growing populations and situations that are out of our control and crumbling infrastructure. 

This type of common response called on California to use their own water resources for their own agriculture, leaving Oregon’s resources to Oregonians. 

Another response involved doubt as to the project’s feasibility:

Aside from realism and localism, some respondents exhibited skepticism over the support that the project would receive from local water managers and lay citizens. “I just think the days of investing in shiny, new things like massive water diversion infrastructure need to be balanced against the fact that other critical infrastructure is deteriorating and the public’s willingness to pay is stretched,” one told us. The appeal of new infrastructure lost luster in consideration of the continued need for upgrades and repairs. This response seems to align with a generally realist, localist position through a desire to focus on more local pressing needs that are more realistic, and it also seemed to oppose modernist appeals of “new, shiny things” in favor of improving more traditional infrastructure. We might not call this traditionalist or anti-modernist, per se, but “non-modernist.” 

Hence, we view the objections to the modernist concept of interstate water transfers from the Pacific Northwest to California’s Central Valley as non-modernist, localist, and realist. These objections indicate that the local water management systems of the Pacific Northwest largely rely on networks embedded in their particular watersheds, with long histories and lived experience of adjudication, compromise, and collaboration. This hydrosocial closeness, usually involving tightknit communities, relies on networks of trust and reliable relationships to accomplish targeted goals. Large scale connections and region-wide projects tend to involve expanded discussions that require more funding, logistics, and agreements, usually involving lawyers and legislative politics. 

##### 3.3.2 Hydrosocial imaginaries 

When we asked the interviewees to describe “the connection in terms of geography of water distribution between the Pacific Northwest and the Central Valley, if there is one,” six of the respondents told us that no connection existed whatsoever. The two respondents who did see a connection focused on the history of the geomorphology and ecology of the macro-region, in one case, or the shared need for food security, in the other. At the same time, even those who insisted that the two regions were wholly distinct tended to strive for an emotional connection on the level of sympathy. 

According to one respondent, “we are uniquely connected from a transportation and economic and social and cultural perspective,” indicating a kind of shared life-world between northern and southern farmers. “But from a hydrologic perspective, no, we are not connected at all.” The same respondent noted that hydroelectric power generated in the Northwest provides electricity in areas of California, but scoffed at the notion of sending “surplus” water to the same place. “We joke about all that, you know, all that Columbia River water going to waste right going into the ocean, what the hell?” the respondent stated, indicating a widespread rejection of the hydrosocial imaginaries of high modernist projects. 

Others who rejected the transfer of water from the Northwest to the Central Valley argued that California had enough water. “There’s

While conferences and meetings brought them together with other water professionals from different regions, the regional affinity with the Pacific Northwest appeared paramount. Sympathies certainly exist across regions in relation to drought and forest fires, and these sympathies manifested in an eagerness to offer assistance when need presented itself. However, the notion of sending water to California remained undesirable and unimaginable among most. 

##### 3.3.3 Theory of power 

From the above discussion of imaginaries and localism, realism, and non-modernism, it becomes clearer that the theory of power developed within hydrosocial territories of the Pacific Northwest by water professionals is bounded by a mentality of practicability and consensus. As multi-scalar networks are considered in light of the preference for local services and infrastructure improvements, the desire for overarching and centralized authority to plan large-scale goals is diminished. Given the complexity of political authorities ranging from the local to the federal and the perceived unreliability of the latter considering recent financial rescissions, we assessed that water professionals remain largely risk-averse and focused on responsible provision. 

One interview subject described an interest in the evolution of juridical authority in the Northwest. With this in mind, the development of federal agencies, local watershed groups, and collaborative frameworks is seen as powerful and helpful in facilitating advantageous improvements while navigating complex legal structures. At the same time, when asked whether they preferred localized or centralized authority in water management, every interview subject declared support either for more local authority or approval of the present mixture between the two. As one put it, “the warp and weft of governments has to be a very complex quilt for it to be successful and effective.” 

In short, the theory of power shared by water professionals in the Pacific Northwest tended to evoke a bottom-up approach to dispute resolution, infrastructure improvement, and water provision. In approaching regional history, power is built through direct engagement in proprietary provisions and defended on the personal level. In developing regulatory and collaborative institutions, power is further established and distributed through adjudication, group consensus, and/or the triangulation of intersecting, multi-scalar goals. This theory of local power relies on strong trust-centered networks developed through long-term and historically-rooted commitments, and approaches increased centralized power and “shiny, new” projects with skepticism. 

## 4 Discussion 

Comparing our interviews with local water managers in the Pacific Northwest to the early projects associated with high modernism and Trump’s statements helps shed light on the hydrosocial imaginaries of the far right. Most importantly, we found that high modernist projects could be vaunted by the right or the left, relying more on a technocratic-authoritarian theory of power than a grassroots theory. At the same time, we found that local water managers on both right and left rejected the technocratic-authoritarian model in favor of grassroots approaches that closely represented the interests of their service recipients. These findings suggest that the politics of water, agriculture, and natural resources more broadly, do not settle comfortably along right- and left-wing polarities, instead diverging between a mix of localism, realism, and non-modernism versus technocratic-authoritarianism. 

This study also contributes to a general understanding of Trump’s politics and the associated ideological tendency, Trumpism. As with any far-right tendency, Trumpism upholds a nationalism that empowers white males born in the United States over women, non-whites, and naturalized citizens. At the same time, his openly authoritarian interventionism in economic affairs through tariffs and control over 

the Federal Reserve is echoed in the political ecology of his hydrosocial imaginaries. Rather than leaving water policies up to state administrations and local water boards, Trump views water infrastructure—and particularly trans-regional or inter-state transfers—as a way to exercise and exhibit his power and control. The lexicon of water transfers is enregistered into scripts of masculinity, using all capital letters for full effect, deploying physical acts like turning valves and faucets, and “strongly” allowing water to flow in defiance of local authorities. Unlike localist imaginaries of water locked into the complexity of deeply-rooted social relations, Trumpism abstracts the flows of water from their ecological setting, but rather than simply turning water into a commodity, it focuses on the extent to which water can serve his power and that of the United States. 

As with the NAWAPA, the Trumpist imaginative geographies of water enlist the settler-colonial history of the West and the tradition of Manifest Destiny. At the same time, this tradition emerges from eastern capitols to envision the West as a wild place tamed by civilization most easily identified with infrastructure projects. Hence, water transfers become essential to the imaginative geographies of the West utilized by technocratic high modernists to develop ever-larger schemes. While such exploitation-oriented imaginaries are often not totally disconnected from reality, their abstraction and lack of material basis join “manly” tariff regimes and other forms of top-down administrative efforts in setting up authoritarian control in opposition to democratic or market-oriented processes (Media Matters Staff, 2025). As a far-right political tendency, Trumpism is nativist and authoritarian; however, Trump’s particular authoritarianism involves resource management, and specifically water transfers, as an opportunity for the display of power rather than utilitarian economic production. Trumpist hydrosocial imaginaries shift the social relations of water onto the biopolitical terrain of winners-and-losers in which Western water flows at his will. 

Our findings indicate a significant rift in Trumpism between his own hydrosocial imaginaries and those who voted for him in the Northwest. While none of the water managers we interviewed identified as “far right,” one volunteered that they voted for Trump. While they may have aligned with Trump on most other political issues, however, those on the right wing of the political spectrum unanimously disagreed with his eagerness to authorize new water transfers to California from the Columbia River, as indicated by Trump’s speech at the National Prayer Breakfast in February 2025. Even among Trump’s supporters, for those already dealing with long-term droughts, snow droughts, and over-allocated streams, the notion of additional transfers directed by the federal government with little opportunity for consent or engagement seemed undesirable. 

More specifically, we identified differences between local water managers’ perspectives and the high modernist claims with which Trump’s own statements most immediately correspond. Where the local water managers viewed the potential for changing the present legal and structural frameworks that organize their work as limited and bounded by existing agreements, laws, resources, and interests, the high modernist position promotes a nearly limitless potential for change. Here, the high modernists insist on imaginative thinking that, while rooted in a glorious settler colonial past, overcomes spatial and structural blockages for the sake of development framed as progressive. Local water managers tend to rankle at such imaginative figurations, because their work requires difficult decisions, such as keeping water instream for the legally-mandated protection of endangered species despite water over-allocations. ## 5 Conclusion 

The immediate interest of this study is a deepening of the scholarship around hydrosocial imaginaries and the contemporary politics of irrigated agriculture and water resources. Our findings depart from a typical framing of natural resource management and neoliberalism, which tends to describe the farming-out of local participatory systems by regulatory state agencies on the condition that participation does not contravene a top-down development agenda favoring big corporations. In a sense, the local watershed councils and groups in which water managers operate on the local level can be understood as a crucial development of the neoliberal era and its development of participatory, multiscalar frameworks under the influence of regulatory agencies and grant-bestowing authorities. The Trump administration has clawed back the funding promised to those local groups, prioritizing instead a high modernist imaginary of direct, federal control. That high modernist model stands as a rebuke against participatory local water management facilitated by neoliberalism, favoring instead direction by a fully top-down authoritarian technocracy. 

While this paper recognizes the problems of “soft, modern” neoliberalism as a system that privatizes and deregulates resource management, leading to wicked problems of environmental promises and degraded outcomes, we also encourage more precise assessments of economic platforms and their concessions to oppositional environmental interest groups. The developments of participatory and 

multiscalar water management through collaborative groups has emerged amid neoliberalism through struggle and compromise with federal regulatory agencies, conservationists, native tribes, and farmers, among other stakeholders. To qualify such entities as a mere product or instrument of neoliberal hegemony, leveraged indirectly against locals whose only agency is sabotage or subversion, relies on a metanarrative that denies the agency of participation and collaboration through difficult and hard-fought compromise. In short, the autonomous, local watershed groups and collaborative organizations are not merely neoliberal and do not represent a neoliberal ethos; they could exist as freely under alternative economic platforms. Thus, we find the struggle cannot be reduced to neoliberal local watershed groups versus technocratic, right-wing authoritarianism. 

Instead, the story that we found is more complicated. While the Trump administration has not actually implemented its water resources agenda beyond deregulation and a productivist impetus, its hydrosocial imaginary reflects serious high modernist commitments. These commitments favor a vision of the United States West as a “blank slate” born of settler colonial triumph and holding boundless potential for human transformation, especially in disregarding ecological priorities in favor of development prerogatives. The shift to high modernism is not unique to conservatism under Trump, but it does represent a sea change in the ideological position of the conservative movement, which defined itself in opposition to the great projects of the New Deal and Great Society. This transition was facilitated by both frustration with the failure of neoliberal deregulation to maintain critical infrastructure or develop new, desirable projects like highspeed rail, and by the rise of Donald Trump’s authoritarian style. By attacking existing federal institutions and agencies, the Trump administration opened up a top-down, forceful way of doing politics, increasing its appeal to thought leaders and influencers across the political spectrum.
