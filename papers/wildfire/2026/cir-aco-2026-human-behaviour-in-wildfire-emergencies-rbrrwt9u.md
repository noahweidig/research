# Human behaviour in wildfire emergencies: insights from evacuation decision-making and timing across Canada, Australia, and the United States

## Abstract

Wildfires often require rapid evacuation to save lives, leading residents to make quick and context-specific transportation decisions that may differ across countries and cultures. This study examines intended evacuation behaviours during wildfires, focusing on promptness, departure time, and cross-country differences. Surveys were conducted across Canada, the United States, and Australia (with sample sizes of 1,366, 1,490, and 1,173, respectively), and the data were used to run advanced choice modelling techniques to estimate factors influencing time-related decisions. The findings emphasized the importance of preparedness, reasons for evacuating, and risk perception in shaping responses to emergencies. Moreover, results identified country differences and similarities in evacuation preparedness time. For instance, employment, age, and income were factors relevant only in the U.S. context, while gender was significant exclusively in the Australian context. For all three countries, households with older adults were less likely to evacuate promptly, while households with children were more likely to evacuate promptly. The study also revealed that complex models showed minimal or no improvement in fit relative to simpler models. These insights on evacuation decisions and departure times, especially across jurisdictional boundaries, can assist in improving generalized wildfire evacuation plans and implementing more inclusive emergency communication strategies.
