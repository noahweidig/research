# A qualitative content analysis of wildfire smoke preparedness and response plans in the United States and Canada

## Abstract

Wildfires in North America have become more frequent and intense in recent years. This increase can be attributed to climate change, which has led to higher temperatures and altered precipitation patterns, creating conditions that favor wildfires. In addition, human activities, such as land development, forest management practices, and the ignition of fires, further contribute to the rising incidence of wildfires, as they increase the likelihood of fire outbreaks in certain areas. Wildfire smoke can cause adverse health impacts, creating an urgent public health concern. Some strategies to reduce wildfire smoke exposure include indoor air filtration, clean air centers, and cancellations of outdoor events when necessary. Wildfire smoke preparedness and response plans (wildfire smoke plans) play a crucial role in coordinating and implementing these strategies across jurisdictions during wildfire smoke events. This study compares wildfire smoke plans developed by jurisdictions and institutions in the United States and Canada, including counties, cities, states, provinces, and universities. We used qualitative content analysis methods to assess the characteristics of 20 wildfire smoke plans. The majority of plans utilize risk reduction strategies such as air quality monitoring, risk communication, and guidance for household-level exposure reduction. Wildfire smoke plans are a relatively new emergency response strategy; as such, a “gold standard” set of plan components has yet to be established. As a result, there were significant variations in the plan components we reviewed. Future research should evaluate the efficacy of existing wildfire smoke plans to identify best practices and gaps. The findings from such evaluations can inform the development of a standardized framework for preparedness and targeted outreach programs for susceptible populations. By grounding future frameworks in evidence from existing plans, jurisdictions can strengthen their wildfire smoke preparedness and response efforts, ultimately protecting public health more effectively.

# **Background** 

42 Wildfires in North America have become more frequent and intense in recent 

43 years [1]. This is in part due to greenhouse gas emissions driving anthropogenic climate 

44 change, as hotter temperatures create more arid environments, making vegetation drier 

45 and more susceptible to burning [2,3]. This pattern is likely to continue as greenhouse 

46 

gas emissions increase, and the ramifications of already front-loaded emissions are 

- 47 realized, leading to persistent drought conditions, longer fire seasons, and an overall 

- 48 increase in fire severity [4]. Another contributor to increased wildfires is human activity; 

49 land management practices such as fire suppression can lead to fuel accumulation [5]. 

50 In addition, human-caused fires account for the majority of wildfires in the United States 

51 and have expanded the seasonal extent of wildfire events [6].

- 57 alveolar wall, consequently impairing lung function [8,9]. Wildfire smoke exposure can

## **Codebook Development** 

- 111 We developed a survey in REDCap to collect plan characteristic data, with each 

- 112 entry representing one plan and each question representing a plan component or 

- 113 strategy (S1 Appendix). We developed the survey using both deductive and inductive 

- 114 methods. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)’s Wildfire Smoke: A Guide for 115 Public Health Officials and Environmental Law Institute’s Wildfire Smoke Report were 

- 116 used to identify evidence-based wildfire smoke exposure reduction strategies, while 

- 117 FEMA’s Local Mitigation Planning Policy Guide, and A Content Analysis of Heat Action 

- 118 Plans in Local United States Jurisdictions informed the organization and structure of the 

- 119 survey [21, 24-26]. The survey was inductively edited after reviewing three sample 

- 120 plans to identify commonly implemented components and strategies for inclusion. Three 

- 121 team members co-coded the three sample plans independently to ensure clarity of 

- 122 codebook definitions. After reviewing discrepancies and making revisions for clarity, the 

- 123 survey was finalized for data collection.

## 192 **Air Quality Monitoring** 

- 193 Seventeen plans (85%) provided detailed guidance for air quality surveillance or 

- 194 monitoring. Nine plans directed government agencies to conduct air quality monitoring, 

- 195 while seven plans directed the public to air quality resources. Only one plan provided air 

- 196 quality monitoring guidance for government agencies and the public. 

- 197 Nine plans (45%) suggested using the EPA AirNow Fire and Smoke Map as a 

- 198 guide for government agencies to identify areas affected by wildfires. Nine plans (45%) 

- 199 directed the public to the EPA AirNow Fire and Smoke Map to learn about the air quality 

- 200 in their area, which groups of people may be affected, and steps to take to reduce 

- 201 exposure to air pollution. The EPA AirNow Fire and Smoke Map differs from the EPA 

- 202 AirNow mobile app, which was provided as a resource by four plans (20%). The app 

- 203 utilizes GPS for localized air quality information, whereas the desktop typically requires 

- 204 manual entry of location [31]. Only one plan listed the National Interagency Fire Center 

- 205 (NIFC) as an air quality monitoring resource for the public. Five plans (25%) included 206 provisions for deployment of mobile smoke monitors during wildfire events to increase 207 regional air quality monitoring capacity.

## 217 **Risk Communication** 

- 218 All plans included strategies for communicating the risks of wildfire smoke 

- 219 exposure to the public. Thirteen plans (65%) included a communications plan, and six 

- 220 plans (30%) included guidance for creating a communications plan. Many 

- 221 communications plans were structured with different actions corresponding to different 

- 222 AQI levels. For example, Garfield County’s communications plan designates actions for 

- 223 AQI 101-150, including issuing a press release and designating further actions for AQI 

- 224 151-500 such as releasing emergency notifications. Twelve plans (60%) included 

- 225 prepared messaging templates. The most common templates were messaging toolkits 

- 226 and press releases focused on health risks and strategies to reduce smoke exposure. 

- 227 Sixteen plans (80%) provided suggested modes of communication. Common 

- 228 modes included radio, social media, text alerts, and press releases. Nineteen plans 

- 229 (95%) documented the agency or agencies responsible for coordinating public risk 

- 230 communication, and twelve plans (60%) listed suggested partners to help disseminate 

- 231 the information. Agencies responsible for coordination were often public health 

- 232 agencies, while suggested partners included community-based organizations, schools, 

- 233 nursing homes, and a variety of government agencies. Ten plans (50%) included 

- 234 instructions or guidance for disseminating messages in languages other than English. 

- 235 Common strategies included translated messaging templates and targeted outreach to 

- 236 populations with a primary language other than English.

## 352 **Overall Aim of this Research** 

- 353 Our review of 20 wildfire smoke plans identified variation in preparedness and 

- 354 response strategies used for wildfire smoke exposure reduction. Although plans used 

- 355 both response and preparedness strategies, response strategies were more commonly 

- 356 included. Interdisciplinary leadership and coordination was common, with 

- 357 responsibilities distributed to diverse sets of agencies. Common community-level 

- 358 exposure reduction strategies included risk communication, guidance for opening clean 

- 359 air centers, and facilitating closures of schools and public events. Common household-

- 361 indoor air quality. Most plans were activated based on AQI thresholds tied to specific 

- 362 actions, and responsibility was largely designated to local and state government 

- 363 agencies like public health and emergency management. Many plans identified at-risk 

- 364 populations and included targeted outreach strategies, with some notable gaps in 

- 365 populations represented.

## **Gaps in Inclusion of At-Risk Populations** 

- 458 Inclusion of targeted outreach for at-risk populations was common but 

- 459 inconsistent across plans. Eighteen plans (90%) acknowledged that certain populations 

- 460 were at greater risk. Thirteen plans (65%) included targeted outreach strategies for at- 

- 461 risk populations, although there were notable gaps in these populations’ representation. 

- 462 Only one plan incorporated targeted outreach for pregnant individuals and people of 

- 463 color. No plans specifically addressed the communication preferences of Indigenous 

- 464 people or renters. Targeted engagement with at-risk populations has been identified as 

- 465 a best practice for risk communication for extreme weather and climate change [44]. 

- 466 MacIntyre et al. [44] performed a scoping review on evaluations of risk communication 

- 467 strategies, and found that engaging with at-risk populations was a key theme among 

- 468 more successful strategies. The gaps in targeted outreach from our findings are 

- 469 reflected by prior extreme event research, which has found relatively low levels of public 

- 470 awareness and personal interventions among at-risk populations during extreme heat 

- 471 events, especially among older adults [45].

- 525 Wildfire smoke preparedness and response plans can help guide public health 

- 526 responses to wildfire smoke events with strategies including coordinating actions across
