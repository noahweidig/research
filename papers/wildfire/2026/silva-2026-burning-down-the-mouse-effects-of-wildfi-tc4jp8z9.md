# Burning down the mouse: Effects of wildfire and post-fire reseeding on Sin Nombre virus prevalence in its reservoir host

## Abstract

Abstract Worldwide, physical habitats and biological communities are being reshaped by wildfire. Such changes have obvious potential to alter pathogen ecology, yet few studies, outside of those focused on ectoparasites, have tested the effects of wildfire on pathogen prevalence in wildlife. Even fewer have focused on the impacts of strategies for post-fire remediation on pathogen circulation. Here, we investigated the effect of wildfire and wildfire mitigation on the prevalence and viral load of Sin Nombre virus (SNV) infection in its reservoir host, the western deer mouse, using a study design of matched burned, unburned, and post-burn reseeded sites in northern New Mexico. In total, we screened 411 individual deer mice for SNV via RT-qPCR and analyzed the effect of wildfire and wildfire mitigation on relative mouse abundance, individual infection probability, site-level prevalence and viral load. Relative abundance was highest at reseeded sites, and model selection indicated that habitat structure – particularly the gradient from closed canopy to open-herbaceous habitat, was consistently associated with increased relative abundance. Similarly, SNV prevalence was significantly higher in reseeded sites than either burned or unburned sites but did not differ in burned versus unburned sites. Viral load did not differ between burn history types, and zero-inflated gamma hurdle models did not identify any strong predictors of viral load. Serendipitously, we were also able to investigate the impacts of an El Nino Southern Oscillation (ENSO) cycle on patterns of infection in a subsample of sites that were studied during and one year after an ENSO year and found that SNV prevalence increased significantly post-ENSO. Both reseeding and ENSO deliver resource pulses that can support increases in mouse density and thereby enhance SNV transmission. The impacts of post-fire management practices on SNV prevalence in its reservoir host that were revealed in this study should be considered when implementing such strategies and when utilizing treated areas. # 44 **Introduction** 

- 45 Wildfires are increasing in both severity and frequency across the globe, driven by rising 

- 46 temperatures and shifting rainfall patterns (Cunningham et al., 2024; Dennison et al., 2014; 

47 Salguero et al., 2020; Westerling et al., 2006). Wildfires can dramatically reshape natural 

48 landscapes through reduction of ground litter, loss of canopy cover, and transformation of soil 

49 chemistry, among many other effects (Bansal, 2025). Following a wildfire, managers may 

50 implement strategies to prevent soil erosion, such as reseeding, replanting, mulching or chemical 51 treatments. These interventions may alleviate some negative effects of wildfire, such as runoff or 52 mudslides, but may exacerbate others, such as alteration of plant communities (Keeley, 2004). 

53 These wholesale changes in both burned and remediated landscapes have the potential to alter 54 the transmission of pathogens among wildlife, thereby altering the risk of pathogen spillover to 55 humans. In an increasingly flammable world, it is critical to gain an understanding of the effects 56 of fire on wildlife infection. 

57 Albery et al. (2021) have laid out multiple, intersecting mechanisms by which the consequences 

58 of wildfire could influence the exposure of wildlife to pathogens. At the nexus of this framework 59 are the impacts of fire on host movement and resource availability. For directly-transmitted 

60 pathogens, wildfire may enhance inter-individual contact rates, and therefore transmission, via 61 degradation of habitat complexity, concentration of existing resources, or production of resource 

62 pulses that support increases in the host population density. Alternatively, wildfire may reduce 

63 contact rates through habitat fragmentation, fire-mediated mortality, or production of resource 

64 pulses that disincentivize host foraging movements (Fay et al., 2025; Tonelli et al., 2026). Thus, 

65 wildfire may shape infection dynamics through multiple, potentially opposing, mechanisms.

71 To date, the majority of studies investigating the impact of wildfire on the frequency of wildlife 72 infection have focused on ectoparasites (reviewed in Scasta, 2015) or on proxies of disease risk, 73 such as corticosteroid levels (Hing et al., 2017), immune function, or immune proteomes (Black 74 et al., 2017; Bowen et al., 2015) rather than directly measuring wildfire impacts on pathogen 75 prevalence. Two notable exceptions are work by Ecke et al. (2019) and Furtado et al. (2023). 

76 The former showed that wildfire-induced thermal stress may increase host susceptibility to 

77 parasitic infection in _Tropidurus oreadicus_ lizards, and the latter demonstrated that reduced 

78 habitat complexity correlated with increased Puumala virus (PUUV, _<mark>Orthohantavirus</mark>_ 

79 _<mark>puumalaense</mark>_ <mark>)</mark> prevalence in bank voles. 

80 In the current study, we investigated the effects of wildfires and post-fire management in 

81 northern New Mexico on Sin Nombre virus (SNV, _<mark>Orthohantavirus sinnombreense</mark>_ ) infection of 82 western deer mice ( _Peromyscus sonoriensis_ ). Western deer mice serve as a major reservoir host 

83 of SNV. Increases in SNV prevalence in deer mice are associated with increases in spillover of 

84 the virus to humans, in whom it can cause hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS) with a 

- 85 fatality rate of ~35% (Childs et al., 1994; Center for Disease Control, 2024). SNV circulation in 

86 deer mice is sensitive to ecological perturbations that alter mouse density, as the virus is 

87 transmitted via direct, aggressive contacts, primarily among adult males, that can increase with 

- 88 density (Mills et al., 1997; Pearce-Duvet et al., 2006). In particular, SNV prevalence in deer 

- 89 mouse populations, and subsequent human outbreaks, have been linked to El Niño Southern

95 Populations of _Peromyscus_ spp. have generally been shown to increase following both wild and 96 prescribed fires, likely due to release of competition with members of their guild and increased 97 foraging efficiency in the absence of ground litter (reviewed in Jones, 1992; Zwolak et al., 2010). 98 Such increases in host abundance could accelerate SNV transmission and enhance prevalence. 99 Consistent with this paradigm, Mull et al. However, fire effects are unlikely to 104 be universal. Sharp Bowman et al. (2017) found that fires in the Great Basin, USA decreased 105 _Peromyscus_ population sizes, suggesting that severe fires may result in unsuitable habitat and

providing an artificial resource pulse, reseeding may act in an analogous fashion to resource 

108 pulses associated with ENSO cycles, supporting higher densities of deer mice and elevating SNV 

109 prevalence (Figure 1). However, as reseeding also alters habitat composition and vegetation 110 structure, its effects may not be mediated solely by host density.

# 195 _Sampling Sites_ 

- 196 Burned sites were constrained to areas categorized as high severity soil burn, determined by a 

- 197 post-fire BAER (burned action emergency response) survey conducted by the U.S. Forest 

- 198 Service (NM Fire Info 2022). To select burned sites, first 100 random points were generated 

- 199 within high severity burned areas that were within 500 meters of a road and spaced at least 250 

- 200 meters apart. A buffer of 100 meters was created around the 100 points, and sites for which 

- 201 buffered areas were not completely within a high severity burn were excluded. To select 

- 202 unburned sites, 100 unburned points were randomly generated in areas that mirrored burned sites 

203 in terms of elevation and dominant vegetation, after which site selection followed the same 

- 204 protocol as for burned sites, save that unburned sites were also excluded if they experienced any 

- 205 burning after 1980, or if they had any visible burn scars upon visual inspection. Both burned and 

- 206 unburned sites that fit all criteria were assigned a number, and final sites were selected with a 

- 207 random number generator, with each burn type replicated three times, and separate control sites 

- 208 selected for each fire. Reseeded sites at Hermit’s Peak Calf Canyon were selected following the 

- 209 same protocol as for burned and unburned sites from an area that had been previously classified 

- 210 as an area of high severity burn and that had been aerially reseeded in 2022 (Figure 2E). The 

- 211 seed contained a mix of winter wheat ( _Triticum aestivum_ ), cereal rye ( _Secale cereale_ ), western 

- 212 wheatgrass ( _Pascopyrun smithii_ ), and blue grama grass ( _Bouteloua gracilis_ ) (National Resources 

- 213 Conservation Service, 2023).

- 221 _Animal collection and processing_ 

- 222 A power analysis conducted prior to initiation of the study determined that the sample size

- 225 was made to achieve this sample size. Small mammals were trapped in Sherman Traps (H.B. 

- 226 Sherman Traps, Inc, Tallahassee, FL, USA) arranged in grids of 100 traps (10 lines with 10 traps, 227 10 meters apart) to allow estimation of relative mouse abundance. In 2023, to enhance the 228 number of deer mice available for estimation of SNV prevalence, 40-50 traps were added in two 229 trap lines within 300 meters of grids to each site. Individuals from these additional trap lines

- 252 _RNA Extraction_ 

253 Lung samples were transported on dry ice and stored at -80⁰C. RNA extractions were conducted 

254 in a biosafety cabinet in a BSL-2 laboratory with BSL-3 precautions. Samples were placed on 

255 dry ice while completing RNA extractions using Qiagen Viral RNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, GER), 

256 following manufacturer protocols with minor adjustments. Briefly, 20-40 mg of each lung 

257 sample was placed in bead beater tubes with 0.5 mg of 1.00 mm diameter zirconia beads 258 (BioSpec, Bartlesville, OK, USA), 1.0 mg of 2.3 mm diameter zirconia beads (BioSpec, 

259 Bartlesville, OK, USA), and 900 mL of AVL buffer. Tissue was homogenized in a BioSpec 

260 MiniBeadBeater-19 (model 607) twice at 4,350 RPMs for 30 seconds, with a 60 second rest in 

261 between. Homogenates were centrifuged for seven minutes at 7000 RPMs, transferred to a 1.5 

262 mL microcentrifuge tube and 5.6 µL of RNA carrier – re-suspended in AVE per manufacturer 

- 263 protocol – was added. Remaining RNA extraction steps were completed following manufacturer 

- 264 protocol, with RNA eluted in 40 µL of nuclease free water. RNA samples were stored at -80⁰C 

- 265 until cDNA synthesis.

- 318 whether continuous variation in habitat structure was associated with variation in deer mouse 

- 319 abundance or SNV prevalence beyond categorical burn history type. Changes in PC1 in re- 

- 320 sampled areas were compared with a paired t-test, with sites paired across years. 

- 321 <u>Deer mouse relative abundance</u> 

- 322 Trap success in grids was used as a proxy for relative deer mouse abundance and was calculated 

- 323 for each site as the number of deer mice caught in a trapping grid per total number of trap nights 324 (Mariën et al., 2024; Polito et al., 2022). Trap nights were defined as the difference between the 

- 325 number of traps set in grids and the number of traps falsely tripped during the session.

- 332 post-fire recovery and climatic conditions, such as ENSO during the 2023 sampling period. An _a_ 333 _priori_ model candidate list of 20 models (Appendix S1:Table S3) were run in R using package

# 545 **Discussion** 

- 546 Wildfires in the southwestern USA are reshaping vegetation cover and resources availability, 

- 547 which may in turn alter infectious disease dynamics in wildlife. In the current study, we tested 

- 548 the hypothesis that wildfire effects on land cover would drive an increase in the abundance of 

- 549 deer mice and, as a consequence, an increase in the prevalence of SNV, for which deer mice are a 

- 550 major reservoir host. Additionally, we tested the hypothesis that re-seeding burned areas for post- 

- 551 fire mitigation strategy would result in even higher abundance of granivorous deer mice and, 

- 552 consequently, SNV prevalence, than would be found on burned but untreated land. 

- 553 Serendipitously, we were also able to test whether deer mouse abundance and SNV prevalence 

- 554 increased on burned land after an El Niño event, a well-documented phenomenon in unburned, 

- 555 xeric habitats that has been attributed to the surge in piñon nut production supported by the 

- 556 uptick in rainfall during El Niño. 

- 557 Importantly, we were able to replicate our study across two years and the sites of two major fires 

- 558 in New Mexico: the Cerro Pelado and the Hermit’s Peak Calf Canyon fires. We first assessed 

- 559 variation in ground cover among ecologically-matched sites with three different burn histories: 

- 560 burned, unburned, or burned and reseeded. Although both burned and reseeded areas had 

- 561 significantly lower tree cover, categorical burn history classification did not effectively capture 

- 562 variation in vegetation characteristics. This indicates that burn history type alone may only be a 

- 563 coarse level descriptor of habitat types in our study area and cannot fully account for 

- 564 heterogeneity present in post-fire landscapes. 

- 565 Next, we tested our prediction that deer mouse abundance would differ between burn history 

- 566 types. However, habitat features, rather than burn history type _per se_ , were most strongly 

- 567 associated with relative deer mouse abundance, consistent with previous studies showing that 

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.09.02.748549; this version posted September 3, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license. 

- 568 habitat characteristics outweigh burn variables in shaping rodent responses (Ecke et al., 2019). 

- 569 Deer mouse abundance increased as habitats became more open and herbaceous, reflecting this 

- 570 species’ preference for open habitats that provide benefits such as increased sources of food and 

- 571 protection from predators (Connolly & Orrock, 2018). While we were only able to sample two 

- 572 reseeded sites, limiting our ability to conduct robust statistical analyses with respect to this burn 

- 573 history type, deer mouse abundance at reseeded sites was more than twice as high as abundance 574 in the other two burn history types. 

- 575 SNV is transmitted among rodents by direct contact, so based on our _a priori_ expectations 

- 576 regarding deer mouse abundance in different fire history types, we predicted that SNV 

- 577 prevalence would be highest at burned and reseeded sites relative to other land covers. For 

- 578 granivorous deer mice, re-seeding represents a form of resource provisioning, and resource 

- 579 provisioning has been shown to have a wide range of effects on wildlife disease, including 

- 580 transmission enhancement (Beck et al., 2026). SNV prevalence was analyzed at two levels of 

- 581 aggregation (individual infection and site-level prevalence). Burn history type was retained in the 

- 582 top-ranked models of both SNV prevalence among individuals and among sites in GLMs. In line 

- 583 with our prediction, reseeded sites had a higher proportion of infected individuals than either 

- 584 burned or unburned sites, possibly because the resource pulse delivered by aerial reseeding 

- 585 supported greater mouse reproduction, similar to ENSO events in this region that produce 

- 586 bumper crops of piñon seeds. Alternatively, mice may have moved into reseeded areas, driving 

- 587 up abundance. In either case, greater abundance would enhance rates of contact and thereby SNV 

- 588 transmission among mice. Because ENSO-driven increases in deer mouse abundance and 

- 589 infection have been linked to HCPS outbreaks in humans, it is noteworthy that an actively 

- 590 implemented management practice may have a similar effect. Moreover, within the US, wildfire 

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.09.02.748549; this version posted September 3, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license. 

- 591 frequency is increasing most rapidly in the west, which already experiences the majority of 

- 592 HCPS burden. Further investigating these overlapping areas of risk will become increasingly 

- 593 important for public health officials and forest managers, particularly if reseeding proceeds apace 

- 594 with wildfires. 

595 In contrast to re-seeded sites, neither relative mouse abundance nor SNV prevalence differed 596 between burned and unburned sites, contra our initial prediction and several previous studies 597 (Zwolak et al., 2010; Culhane et al., 2022). Interestingly, a similar study in the Jemez Mountain 598 range of New Mexico also found that deer mouse populations in burned areas did not differ from 599 unburned areas one-to-three years after a large wildfire in 2011 (Peyton et al., 2025).  Like the 600 rest of the American west, New Mexico has experienced “megadrought” since 2000; this drought 601 is thought to be one of the proximal causes of the Cerro Pelado and Hermit’s Peak Calf Canyon 602 fires (Natural Resources Conservation Service, 2025). Since drought can limit population growth 603 of _Peromyscus_ species (Kuenzi et al., 2007; Nelson, 1993; Reed et al., 2007), it is possible that 604 the impacts of the megadrought obscured any additional effects of fire on these populations. 605 Moreover, wildfire may influence host pathogen dynamics through multiple competing 606 pathways, in ways that may offset one another. Changes in resource availability and habitat 

607 structure may increase host abundance and transmission opportunities, while severe wildfires and 608 prolonged drought may suppress host populations and reduce contact rates. Consequently, these 

609 opposing mechanisms may produce little net effect of wildfire alone on SNV prevalence. 

- 610 As documented in numerous previous studies in xeric habitats (reviewed in Prist et al., 2017), 611 Sin Nombre virus prevalence in deer mice did respond strongly to ENSO conditions at burned 

- 612 sites, which were the only sites samples during- and post-ENSO. SNV prevalence increased 

- 613 significantly, by ~30%, one-year post-ENSO (2024) relative to the ENSO year (2023). 

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.09.02.748549; this version posted September 3, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license. 

- 614 Herbaceous ground cover also increased at the sites sampled from 2023 to 2024, consistent with 

- 615 the understanding the ENSO alters SNV dynamics through alteration of food availability, mouse 

- 616 density, and mouse aggressive interactions. However, while we observed the expected increase in 

- 617 SNV prevalence, we did not observe the expected increase in deer mouse abundance post-ENSO. 

- 618 This may be due to timing of our field collection in the post-ENSO year, which occurred in late 

- 619 spring, prior to the period when deer mice tend to reach their peak populations. As a 

- 620 consequence, we may have failed to capture the full scope of population dynamics post-ENSO. 

- 621 In addition to ENSO, hantavirus prevalence has been linked to other shifts in environmental 622 conditions, such as differences in habitat types (Kuenzi et al., 2001; Lehmer et al., 2012; Root et 623 al., 1999; Yahnke et al., 2001), landscape structure (Langlois et al., 2001; Suzán et al., 2008), 

624 habitat quality (Pearce-Duvet et al., 2006), and level of human disturbance (Mackelprang et al., 625 2001). The observed increase in SNV prevalence post-ENSO provides independent support for 626 the resource-pulse hypothesis that explains increased prevalence in reseeded areas. Together, 627 these findings suggest that sporadic, episodic increases in resource abundance, either naturally or 628 anthropogenically derived, may play a greater role in shaping SNV transmission than wildfire 629 alone. 

630 We had no _a priori_ expectation that burn history would impact viral load, as viral replication is 

631 shaped by a product of a complex interplay among the genome, condition, immunity, sex and age 

- 632 of individual animals (Bagamian et al., 2013; Hannah et al., 2008). Indeed, none of the habitat or 633 individual characteristics that we measured explained viral load. Notably, SNV RNA levels in 

- 634 deer mice tissue have been found to be extremely variable in laboratory settings (Schountz et al., 

- 635 2012), which may obscure subtle ecological drivers of viral burden. Viral load has been 

- 636 previously correlated with the likelihood of transmitting SNV to an uninfected host (Bagamian et 

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.09.02.748549; this version posted September 3, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license. 

- 637 al., 2012), and based on our findings, infected mice across all burn history types would be 

- 638 equally likely to transmit SNV to uninfected mice. 

- 639 Behavioral and population ecology, independent of habitat, can play a part in disease 

- 640 transmission. Our findings align with previous literature (reviewed in Khalil et al., 2014) and our 

- 641 own prediction, as SNV infection was almost exclusively detected in adult mice. Previous 

- 642 studies have also found that SNV prevalence is generally higher in male than female mice, but in 643 the current study males had higher prevalence than females only in unburned sites. Further, this 

- 644 trend was largely driven by patterns at a single fire – Hermit’s Peak Calf Cayon – where no 

- 645 female mice in unburned sites tested positive for SNV. As our sample size for unburned sites at 

- 646 Hermit’s Peak Calf Canyon was limited (n=29), further study is needed to substantiate this 

- 647 finding. When data were aggregated more generally, sex was not a significant predictor of SNV 

- 648 prevalence. This result contrasts with expectations based on current understanding of SNV 

- 649 transmission ecology, as males are thought to drive transmission through aggressive interactions 

- 650 and thus are expected to exhibit higher prevalence (Root et al., 1999). This lack of sex-bias in 

- 651 infection may reflect temporal or demographic dynamics unique to our study or reflect the 

- 652 critical importance of habitat to SNV infection patterns. Though more wounds would seem to 

- 653 indicate more aggressive interactions, we found no evidence that an increase in wound count was 

- 654 correlated with increased infection probability.. 

- 655 There are several caveats to our findings. First, based on our _a priori_ power analysis, our sample 

- 656 size may not have been sufficiently large to detect differences in SNV between burned and 

- 657 unburned sites with high power. Nonetheless, we did detect robust differences in SNV 

- 658 prevalence between reseeded sites and the other two burn history types, as well as a substantial 

- 659 impact of ENSO on SNV prevalence in a relatively small (n = 53) sample of mice. Second, we 

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.09.02.748549; this version posted September 3, 2026. The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license. 

- 660 were only able to sample two reseeded sites, which may not adequately capture variation in the 

- 661 reseeded landscapes. Third, long-term studies (>10 years) of SNV ecology show evidence of a 

- 662 lag between peak deer mouse population density and peak SNV prevalence – a phenomenon 

- 663 termed delayed density dependence (Douglass et al., 2001; Luis et al., 2015; Yates et al., 2002). 

664 Shorter studies, like ours, may fail to detect the relationship altogether, particularly at local scales 665 (Carver et al., 2011). Finally, our sampling took place one- to two-years post-fire. Sampling over 666 a longer time period may reveal additional nuances to the effects of fire and post-fire 

667 management on SNV prevalence. 

668 This study did reveal that a post-fire mitigation strategy – reseeding – enhanced deer mouse 

669 abundance and SNV prevalence. The similarities in the responses to resource pulses generated by 670 reseeding and by ENSO raise important questions about how post-fire management actions may 671 alter disease transmission in ecologically sensitive host-pathogen systems, such as SNV. The lack 672 of differences in SNV prevalence observed between burned and unburned areas may indicate that 673 wildfire may be less important than sporadic resource pulses to reservoir abundance and disease 674 transmission. Finally, our documentation of exceptionally high SNV prevalence in deer mice in 675 reseeded areas offers a critical caution for land managers and others utilizing reseeded 

676 landscapes. Our findings suggest that future research on the impacts of wildfire on wildlife 

677 disease ecology should incorporate the effects of the various treatments utilized to stabilize soils 

678 and slow runoff in the aftermath of the fire.
