# Forest canopy height change due to demographic and allometric shifts

## Abstract

Forest canopy height is sensitive to shifts in demography (which affect tree size distributions and abundances of short-vs. tall-statured species) and allometry. Demographic and allometric effects on canopy height have not previously been studied across a large region. We analyzed more than two million individual tree measurements from about 60,000 remeasured inventory plots in non-plantation forests across 19 ecoregions of the conterminous United States to quantify changes in aboveground live tree biomass (AGB), canopy height, and its demographic and allometric components from ~2003-2025. We tested the hydraulic limitation hypothesis, which predicts decreasing tree height with increasing hydraulic stress. AGB was strongly correlated with canopy height across ecoregions, and AGB change was strongly correlated with canopy height change over time. Canopy height change was positive in nearly all eastern ecoregions but varied in sign across western ecoregions. Demographic shifts led to increased canopy height in most eastern ecoregions but decreased canopy height in most western ecoregions. In contrast, allometric shifts, averaged across species, caused either positive or no significant change in ecoregion-mean canopy height. Our results were consistent with several predictions of the hydraulic limitation hypothesis: (i) intraspecific allometric shifts were more positive for understory than for canopy trees, (ii) across ecoregions, canopy height was positively correlated with precipitation and negatively correlated with vapor pressure deficit, and (iii) canopy height change over time was positively correlated with precipitation change. However, our results suggest modifications to the hydraulic limitation hypothesis, because allometric shifts tended to be positive, even in ecoregions with decreasing precipitation and increasing vapor pressure deficits. Such allometric shifts, which seem counter to the hydraulic limitation hypothesis, may be caused by CO2-driven increases in water-use efficiency. Whatever their cause, allometric shifts should be accounted for to avoid biased estimates of canopy height change and biomass change.

46 Forest canopy height affects diverse ecosystem and land-surface properties (Jucker et al., 47 2018; Migliavacca et al., 2021; Z. Zhang et al., 2022) and is inherently linked to forest 48 biomass and carbon storage (Chave et al., 2019; Jucker et al., 2017; Lefsky et al., 2002). 49 Canopy height can be directly estimated from remotely sensed data (Lang et al., 2023; 50 Simard et al., 2011; Wagner et al., 2025), providing the potential for enhanced monitoring of 51 biomass and other height-related forest properties (Chambers et al., 2007; Hurtt et al., 52 2004; Jucker et al., 2018; Travers-Smith et al., 2024). However, individual- and stand-level 53 relationships between height, biomass, and other attributes may change over time due to 54 shifts in species composition and allometry. Thus, understanding and accurately 55 monitoring forest properties requires individual-tree-level observations of species identity 56 and allometry that are absent from most remotely sensed data products, particularly those 57 derived from widely available satellite data.

58 Forest demography—the tree-level growth, mortality, and recruitment rates that 59 determine community dynamics—may affect canopy height through multiple pathways. 60 Disturbance-recovery dynamics strongly affect tree size distributions and thus have a 61 strong and direct effect on canopy height (Bormann & Likens, 1979; Peet, 1981). In 62 addition, demography can also lead to shifts in species composition, which can affect 63 canopy height due to species differences in allometry and maximum height. For example, 64 forest maturation may involve shifts towards either taller- or shorter-statured tree species 65 depending on environmental conditions and the traits of early- and late-successional 66 species in a given region (Franklin & Hemstrom, 1981; Pare & Bergeron, 1995; Pastor et al., 67 1987; Van Cleve & Viereck, 1981). Height allometries reflect 73 the balance of the costs and benefits of increased height for a given diameter (Falster & 74 Westoby, 2003). Costs may include hydraulic stress (Koch et al., 2004; McDowell & Allen, 75 2015; Ryan et al., 2006), allocational tradeoffs (Falster & Westoby, 2003), and increased 76 exposure to wind damage (King, 1986; Lohmander & Helles, 1987; Thomas et al., 2015). 77 Benefits may include increased light exposure relative to neighboring competitors (Falster 78 & Westoby, 2003; Pacala et al., 1996) and wider dispersal of pollen and seeds (Nathan et 79 al., 2001, 2002; Okubo & Levin, 1989). 88 Global trends toward rising VPD and intensifying disturbance regimes suggest that 89 forest canopy heights should be decreasing in many regions (McDowell et al., 2020), but 90 empirical tests of this hypothesis are lacking. Forests of the conterminous United States 91 (U.S.) provide a valuable opportunity to study canopy height change because these forests 92 span a wide range of climates and disturbance regimes and because the U.S. Forest 93 Service has conducted systematic, standardized inventories of these forests over multiple 94 decades, including observations of the stem diameters and heights of millions of individual 95 trees (Bechtold & Patterson, 2005; Tinkham et al., 2018). 

Here, we use data from the U.S. nationwide forest inventory (NFI) to quantify changes in canopy height and its demographic and allometric components across the conterminous U.S. from ~2003-2025. Our study is based on measurements of individual trees in forest inventory plots, allowing us to quantify changes in demography (including shifts in diameter distributions and species composition) and within-species shifts in allometry. Our study is complementary to remote-sensing-based studies of canopy height, which have greater potential for spatiotemporal coverage but do not provide the tree-level species and diameter data needed to decompose height change into demographic and allometric components. We address the following questions: ( _i_ ) How are aboveground biomass, canopy height, and their changes over time related across U.S. ecoregions? ( _ii_ ) How have demographic and allometric shifts contributed to canopy height change? ( _iii_ ) How is canopy height related to climate variables (temperature, precipitation, and VPD), and how is canopy height change (and its components) related to climate change? ( _iv_ ) Do allometric shifts differ between canopy and understory trees?

We evaluate our results in the context of the hydraulic limitation (HL) hypothesis, which posits that tree heights are limited by hydraulic stress (Koch et al., 2004; McDowell & Allen, 2015; Moles et al., 2009; Ryan et al., 2006; Ryan & Yoder, 1997; Tao et al., 2016). We define the HL hypothesis broadly to encompass growth, mortality, and allometry, with the general prediction that tree heights should decrease as hydraulic stress increases (e.g., due to mortality of tall trees; McDowell & Allen, 2015). The HL hypothesis is supported by some studies of tree physiology (e.g., Koch et al., 2004; Ryan et al., 2006) and geographic patterns of canopy height (Moles et al., 2009; Tao et al., 2016). Here, we present novel tests of the HL hypothesis in terms of ( _i_ ) changes over time in forest canopy height (and its demographic and allometric components) in relation to climate change, and ( _ii_ ) how canopy and understory allometries shift over time within species. We assume that, on average, canopy trees experience greater hydraulic stress than understory trees (Myers et al., 1987; Niinemets, 2007). Thus, the HL hypothesis predicts that allometric shifts for understory trees will be more positive (or less negative) than for canopy trees.

For each tree, FIA reports a code indicating if height was field measured, visually 281 estimated, or modeled (HTCD in the FIA Tree table). To quantify observed patterns in 282 canopy height and its change, we did not filter on this code, because we aimed to minimize 283 missing data that could bias our results. For example, if the heights of the tallest trees were 284 visually estimated or modeled, excluding these trees would cause negative bias in _H_ mean 285 and _H_ max. Similarly, if all tree heights in a plot were visually estimated or modeled, excluding 286 all these heights would yield erroneous zero values for _H_ mean and _H_ max. We expect errors 287 associated with visually estimated or modeled heights to have little effect on our 288 ecoregion-level estimates of observed canopy height and its change, because 90% of 289 canopy tree heights in the FIA dataset we analyzed were field measured.

Intraspecific shifts in allometry have important implications for monitoring forest canopy height and biomass. First, applying standard (static-in-time) height-DBH balanced by allometric shifts towards greater height (positive ∆𝐻𝐻allom). allometries to western U.S. forests over our study period (~2003-2025) would lead to the erroneous conclusion that canopy heights decreased throughout most of this region. In contrast, our results suggest that allometric shifts roughly compensated for the decreases in western U.S. canopy heights that would have occurred due to demographic change alone. Second, if allometries (on average) are shifting over time towards greater height for a given DBH, as suggested by our results, ignoring these shifts will lead to biased estimates of biomass change. If biomass is monitored by combining remote-sensing-derived height change with static biomass-height relationships, then biomass change will be overestimated (because in reality, biomass for a given height will decrease as allometries shift taller). Conversely, if biomass is monitored by combining DBH measurements with static biomass-DBH allometries that ignore height, then biomass change will be underestimated (because in reality, biomass for a given DBH will increase as allometries shift taller). The FIA program, which conducts the nationwide forest inventory of the U.S., uses biomass allometries that depend on measurements of both DBH and height (Westfall et al., 2024), which in principle would capture the effects of changing allometries. Some other biomass allometries also incorporate both DBH and height (e.g., Chave et al., 2014), but many ground-based studies rely on DBH-only biomass allometries due to the labor costs of measuring tree heights, particularly in tall, dense forests.

637 How large is the bias associated with ignoring allometric shifts? Because biomass is 638 roughly proportional to height (Chave et al., 2014; Domke et al., 2012), it is easy to estimate 639 the magnitude of the potential bias. For example, consider a 30-cm-DBH tree in a 640 population whose height allometry is shifting by 0.1 m yr<sup>−1</sup> (a large but not extreme shift in 641 our dataset; Fig. 4). Assuming a 30-cm-DBH tree is roughly 20 m tall (Figs.
