# Creative destruction: from fire ecology to management principles—a conceptual review

## Abstract

This conceptual review aims to synthesize fire management principles that have the potential to restore and conserve biodiversity and ecosystem processes, avert catastrophic events and ensure the sustainable use of ecosystem resources, goals that are often perceived as conflicting but can be addressed synergistically. To achieve this, we first examine the role of fire in natural systems. Fire regimes operating across specific spatiotemporal scales and exhibiting particular patterns are conceptualized as potentially creative, regulating species diversity, ecosystem resilience and landscape configuration. Within the contemporary Non-Equilibrium ecological paradigm, the structural and creative role of fire is analyzed across four domains: evolution, community assembly, landscape structure and ecosystem functioning. Building on this framework, we identify key conceptual principles for managing anthropogenic fire disturbances as creative ecological drivers. To further substantiate fire management principles and management’s creative potential, we critically assess a series of fire-related case studies in mediterranean-type ecosystems: managed wildfires, prescribed burning, pyrosilviculture, cultural burning and shepherding for wildfire adaptation. The results of ecological theory and empirical evidence highlight the capacity of fire management to regulate community assembly, facilitate species co-existence, generate landscape heterogeneity, influence ecosystem processes, and shape evolutionary trajectories. We argue that these insights support a paradigm shift in contemporary fire management: from a predominantly suppressive and preventive approach to one that recognizes fire as a creative tool for integrated and adaptive interventions in fire-prone landscapes.

## 1 Introduction 

Over the last decades, fire in mediterranean ecosystems has been treated with increasing ambivalence, with policy often lagging behind contemporary scientific understanding (Moreira et al., 2020). On the one hand, in the context of global change, mega-fires and the impacts from fire regime changes have justifiably received major attention from scientists, policymakers and knowledge dissemination bodies (Linley et al., 2022; Nimmo et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2025; Ghasemiazma et al., 2026). Fires driven by extreme weather patterns and acting upon landscapes undergoing significant transformation (e.g., rural land abandonment, fire suppression

policies and the creation of novel wildland–urban interfaces) have had severe socio-ecological impacts and often led to resilience loss. 

On the other hand, during the recent decades fire has been recognized as a major evolutionary and ecological driver in fire-prone landscapes, sculpting ecosystem assembly and distribution from local to planetary scales along with climate and soils (Keeley et al., 2012; Pausas and Bond, 2019; Pausas et al., 2025). Fire regimes coevolving with ecological systems are considered key biodiversity drivers (He et al., 2019), influencing landscape heterogeneity, resilience and the regulation of ecological processes over a range of spatial and temporal scales. Reviews in recent years have recognized explicitly the ecological role of fire, synthesizing contemporary knowledge (Archibald et al., 2018; McLauchlan et al., 2020; Kobziar et al., 2024). 

This understanding of the creative and structural role of fire has only narrowly permeated contemporary fire management, which has largely focused on the catastrophic impacts of fire under global change scenarios. Nowadays, national policies worldwide do not recognize fire as a natural ecological process and continue to strongly discourage its use as an ecosystem management tool (Kobziar et al., 2024). As a result, emphasis is placed on effective fire suppression or, at best, on the controlled use of fire (e.g., prescribed burning) and other measures aimed at mitigating fire risk. 

Despite advances in fire ecology, a gap remains in translating this knowledge into integrated management frameworks. This review aims to reconceptualize fire management from a preemptive measure to a creative ecological process, and a potential medium for holistic ecosystem management in fire-prone landscapes. Specifically, we pursue three objectives: 

- (1) to synthesize current ecological understanding of fire within the Non-Equilibrium paradigm, emphasizing its role in evolution and multiple levels of ecological organization and functioning (community assembly, landscape structure and ecosystem functioning); 

- (2) to develop a conceptual framework for anthropogenic fire use as a form of creative disturbance, identifying the evolutionary implications and key principles through which fire can promote biodiversity, regulate landscape structure and sustain ecosystem processes; 

- (3) to evaluate the applicability of these principles through selected fire-related management practices in mediterraneantype ecosystems (MTEs) (including managed wildfires, prescribed burning, pyrosilviculture, cultural burning and shepherding for wildfire adaptation), in order to assess their creative potential. 

In the context of accelerating global change, this review aims to highlight fire as a potentially creative ecological process and explicitly link this perception with fire management principles, addressing a growing need in the literature for stronger conceptual integration across theory and application. 

## 2 Methods 

This study is a structured, concept-driven narrative review aimed at synthesizing the creative role of fire across ecological scales and translating this understanding into management principles. The 

conceptual starting point of the review lies in Holling’s (1986) notion of _creative destruction_ , which frames disturbance as both a destructive and generative force in ecological systems. Building on this foundation, the review includes a concise examination of the historical emergence of disturbance as a central concept in ecological discourse (Section 3), situating fire within the broader Non-Equilibrium paradigm. 

Guided by this conceptual perspective, the analysis is structured around four key domains (Section 4)—evolution, community assembly, landscape structure, and ecosystem functioning, including resilience—through which fire operates. These domains were used to organize both the literature search and the review synthesis. 

Literature was identified through targeted searches using combinations of keywords such as “ _fire ecology,”_ “ _disturbance,”_ “ _fire regimes,”_ “ _pyrodiversity”_ with the above four domains. The search process was iterative, with key articles leading to additional relevant sources through citation tracking. 

The selection of literature was guided by its relevance to the conceptual objectives of the review. Emphasis was placed on (i) foundational theoretical contributions, (ii) synthesis papers, and (iii) empirical studies that elucidate key mechanisms across the four domains. The aim was not exhaustive coverage, but the inclusion of representative and influential studies that capture current understanding of fire as a creative ecological process. 

Based on this synthesis, conceptual principles of fire management’s creative potential were identified and linked to the above domains (Section 5). Selected case studies from MTEs were subsequently examined to illustrate the application of theoretical insights in real-world management contexts (Section 6). These examples were chosen based on their relevance to the identified domains and their capacity to demonstrate the potential of fire as a creative management tool. 

## 3 The concept and ecological importance of creative destruction 

In 1986, in his paper _“The Resilience of Terrestrial Ecosystems: Local Surprise and Global Change,”_ Canadian ecologist C. S. Holling introduced the term “ _creative destruction_ ” to describe the role of disturbance in ecological systems. Holling’s work, which examined the dynamics of growth and disturbance across various ecosystems, emerged during a period of significant transition in ecological thought, as the traditional paradigm of “Balance in Nature” was being replaced by the paradigm of Non-Equilibrium (Wu and Loucks, 1995). Within this new context, disturbance acquired renewed significance as a driver of biodiversity and its organization within ecological systems: beyond its destructive capacity, its creative and structural roles began to be recognized. 

The term creative destruction is inherently oxymoronic: disturbance is destructive because it reduces the amount of existing biomass and breaks down the established community and landscape structure. However, this destruction, often internally generated within ecosystems (Holling, 1986), is frequently the outcome of the success of certain ecosystem components in outcompeting others: competition tends to homogenize the system, reduce diversity, and ultimately increase its vulnerability to destabilization, as key elements become “ _accidents waiting to happen_ ” (Holling, 1986). Disturbance is creative because by breaking down established structures, releases resources and generates and maintains a diversity of habitats and niches (Shea and Chesson, 2002; Shea et al., 2004). These processes enable species coexistence, sustain biodiversity and heterogeneity, and foster opportunities for novel species assemblages. Disturbance also contributes to the maintenance of resilience (Holling, 1996) by helping avert catastrophic disruptions and by regulating numerous ecological processes. The conservation of a system’s natural variability has long been recognized as a prerequisite for resilience (Holling, 1973; Holling, 1996; Holling and Meffe, 1996), _“the variability producing diversity as a consequence of a cyclic shifting of the competitive advantage between species”_ (Holling, 1986). 

In 1985, Pickett and White proposed what is now considered the classical definition of disturbance: _“A disturbance is any relatively discrete event in time that disrupts ecosystem, community, or population structure and changes resources, substrate availability, or the physical environment.”_ In other words, and emphasizing its creative role, disturbance _“alters niche opportunities that are available to species in a system”_ (Shea and Chesson, 2002; Shea et al., 2004). A disturbance is not an isolated event but part of a broader regime operating across space and time. As Turner and Gardner (2015) state, _“the disturbance regime refers to the spatial and temporal dynamics over a longer time period and is described by characteristics such as the spatial distribution of disturbances; disturbance frequency, return interval, rotation period; and disturbance size, intensity, and severity”_ . 

Historically, ecological thought shifted from succession to disturbance during the second half of the 20th century, before the two were integrated in the conceptual scheme of the adaptive cycle (Holling, 1986, 1987). Both Clements (1916) and Odum (1969) viewed ecological succession as a highly directional process leading to homeostasis, stability, and the optimal fit of ecosystems to their environments. Highlighting selectively some classic texts in the literature, this view was challenged in 1973 by Drury and Nisbet (1973), marking a shift in mainstream ecological thinking. Then, Levin and Paine (1974) and Connell (1978)-proposing the Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis (IDH)-underscored the role of disturbance in generating environmental heterogeneity and highlighted the interplay between competition and disturbance in maintaining species diversity. Sousa (1984) elaborated on the ecological and evolutionary role of disturbance, and Pickett and White (1985) edited the classic collection of essays _“The Ecology of Natural Disturbance and Patch Dynamics.”_ Finally, in 1986, Holling introduced the concepts of creative destruction and the adaptive cycle, integrating the dynamics of ecological succession and disturbance into a unified framework, while reviving earlier ideas proposed by Watt (1947). 

The adaptive cycle (Holling, 1986) conceptualizes ecosystem development through four distinct phases: exploitation (r), conservation (K), creative destruction (ω), and reorganization (α). This framework acknowledges the ecological roles of both succession and disturbance, as well as their interactions (Holling, 1996): succession acts as a stabilizing force, maintaining productivity and biogeochemical cycles, while disturbance serves as a destabilizing force, promoting biodiversity, resilience, and opportunities for novel assemblages. The reorganization phase, by initiating successional trajectories and 

creating opportunities for novel assemblages, transforms what might otherwise be a predictable cycle into an evolutionary mechanism, a “wheel” capable of generating diversity with each turn. 

Adaptive cycles do not operate at a single spatiotemporal scale. Instead, they are nested within one another in a highly dynamic structure that Gunderson and Holling (2002) termed Panarchy. The significance of Panarchy lies in the two-way feedbacks and interactions between hierarchical levels, which make the entire structure both dynamic and robust: this nested system conserves both resilience and the capacity for experimentation, transformation and adaptation. 

Within the overarching dynamics of the adaptive cycle, the following section examines the structural role of fire in natural systems across evolutionary and ecological domains. These domains are intrinsically interdependent: differential trait expression under different disturbance regimes produces spatial and temporal heterogeneity, while disturbance-generated heterogeneity selects among traits, shaping their distribution (Sousa, 1984). The coupling of fire regimes and organism-mediated environmental modifications with selection pressures on various traits in an eco-evolutionary dynamic is an integral part of niche construction theory. 

## 4 The structural role of fire in ecological systems 

### 4.1 Evolution 

Over evolutionary timescales, specific disturbance regimes have driven the selection of adaptations through trade-offs between disturbance tolerance and competitive ability (Díaz et al., 2016; Jentsch and White, 2019). In the case of fire, adaptations (Ne’eman et al., 2012; Tavşanoğlu and Pausas, 2018) can be broadly categorized into two types: those that ensure the survival of individual organisms (e.g., resprouting, thick bark) and those that ensure post-fire recruitment at the population level (e.g., soil or canopy seed banks). 

Although convergent evolution has been observed, particularly in fire-prone biomes such as Mediterranean climate ecosystems (Mooney and Dunn, 1970; Cowling et al., 2015; Rundel et al., 2016), different fire regimes select for distinct suits of traits. For example, within the genus _Pinus_ (Keeley and Pausas, 2022), fire-tolerator pines possess adaptations that allow survival under frequent surface fire regimes. These adaptations include thick bark and self-pruning, which reduce individuals’ vulnerability to surface fires (Keeley and Zedler, 1998; Tapias et al., 2004; Pausas, 2015). Fire-tolerator pines do not form resistant seed banks and their post-fire recruitment relies on seeds dispersed by surviving trees into open, burned areas, as observed in _Pinus nigra_ (Christopoulou et al., 2014). In contrast, fire-embracer pines are adapted to stand-replacing crown fire regimes. These pines exhibit a flammability strategy that includes traits such as slow crown separation from surface fuels, retention of dead branches, and serotinous cones (e.g., _Pinus halepensis_ , _P. brutia_ ; Daskalakou and Thanos, 1996; Spanos et al., 2000; Ne’eman and Arianoutsou, 2021). 

The evolution of flammability as a trait in fire-prone ecosystems was first suggested by Mutch (1970) and later supported by Bond and Midgley (1995). Flammability increases fitness by enhancing recruitment opportunities for fire-adapted individuals when fires eliminate neighboring, less flammable competitors (Keeley and Pausas, 2022).

Thus, flammability can be considered a niche construction trait (Schwilk, 2003; Schwilk and Kerr, 2003). However, that niche construction refers not only to the individual species, but also to the modification of the niche of the coexisting species of the ecological community. All species need to adapt to the fire regime (Pausas et al., 2017) and co-evolve. Fire generates vegetations gaps and landscape heterogeneity, giving opportunities to low-competitive and shadeintolerant species and selects for traits that increase fitness in fireprone ecosystems (Pausas et al., 2025). Animals that present no immediately apparent associations with fire, would have experienced significant selective pressures because of the fire regime shaping their environment and vegetation dynamics over evolutionary time (Jones et al., 2023). 

Fire drives significant evolutionary mechanisms, including stabilizing, directional, disruptive, and fluctuating selection, can inhibit or facilitate gene flow (by eliminating or conserving source populations, stepping stones and corridors) and influences genetic drift, especially under high mortality (Jones et al., 2023). From a genetic diversity perspective, fire can have both direct and indirect effects on population dynamics and genetic structure (Banks et al., 2013). Direct effects include fire-induced mortality and reproduction risks, such as immaturity and senescence risk in obligate seeders. Indirect effects arise from species’ responses to the spatial and temporal heterogeneity generated by fire, which influences habitat suitability, colonization-extinction dynamics, and the permeability of the resulting landscape mosaic (Templeton et al., 2011; He et al., 2019). 

At broader evolutionary scales, species diversity in fire-prone ecosystems may be partly explained by high rates of diversification associated with fire (Cowling and Pressey, 2001; Barraclough, 2006; Pausas and Lamont, 2018). Research indicates that plant speciation rates are generally higher in fire-prone ecosystems, as fire can accelerate evolutionary processes by shortening generation turnover time and reducing generational overlap (He et al., 2019), allowing close tracking of environmental changes (Keeley and Pausas, 2022). 

### 4.2 Community assembly 

At the patch level, disturbance plays a critical role in generating and conserving species diversity by shifting competitive advantages between late-successional and pioneer species, as captured in the IDH (Connell, 1978). The interplay between disturbance and biological interactions, such as competition, mediates the spatiotemporal organization of natural communities (Sousa, 1984). Fire can sustain climax communities – the so-called “fire-climax” or “pyroclimax”- uniquely adapted to specific fire regimes, communities that differ significantly from those predicted solely by the prevailing climate and soils. 

Writing from a Non-Equilibrium perspective, Connell (1978) noted that _“The existence of high local biodiversity in the face of resource overlap is only a problem if one assumes equilibrium conditions.”_ The IDH emerged as one of the mechanisms to explain species coexistence under non-equilibrium conditions. It relies on two key assumptions (Jentsch, 2022): (1) hierarchical competition among species for limited resources, and (2) evolutionary trade-offs between competitive ability and disturbance tolerance. Together, these principles underpin the niche partitioning observed along disturbance gradients (Jentsch, 2022). Criticism over the last few decades (Mackey and Currie, 2001; Fox, 2012), has contributed to the further refinement of the IDH and incorporation of additional factors mediating the disturbance-competition interplay, including community productivity (Huston, 1994, 

2014), scale (Aronson and Precht, 1995), trophic level (Wootton, 1998), and initial landscape and ecosystem conditions (With, 2019). 

The conservation of both pioneer and late-successional species and the fire-climax is exemplified in mediterranean systems where shrublands coexist with open forests recovering from previous fires. This coexistence is driven by distinct regeneration strategies that allow different species to thrive under specific fire intervals (Bond, 2019; Vasques et al., 2022). Modeling studies (Bond et al., 2004; Bond and Keeley, 2005) suggest that, in the absence of fire or other disturbances, many grasslands and shrublands around the world could transition into forests, given the suitable soil and climatic conditions. Indeed, under such conditions, forests currently covering one-fourth of Earth’s surface (Bond et al., 2004) could more than double in area. Thus, fire plays a fundamental role not only in shaping ecosystems locally, but also in shaping biome distribution at regional and global scale. 

Fire, together with the subsequent reorganization phase of the adaptive cycle, creates opportunities for the emergence of new species assemblages. Fire severity, spatial pattern, and seasonality act as ecological filters (Verdú and Pausas, 2007; Pausas and Verdú, 2008; Jentsch, 2022), selecting for species with particular traits, age structures, and life histories strategies. These filters influence how resources are partitioned after disturbance, determining community composition and the nature of species interactions. For instance, priority effects (Fukami, 2015) can play a critical role, as early colonizers — such as flammable grasses (D’Antonio and Vitousek, 1992) —may alter successional trajectories by modifying resource availability and redefining niche space, thereby influencing the composition of subsequent assemblages. 

A related hypothesis to IDH, specific to fire -that will be examined to a greater extent in 4.3. Landscape structure- is “Pyrodiversity begets biodiversity,” proposed by Martin and Sapsis (1992). The authors defined pyrodiversity as the diversity within the fire regime: spatial and temporal patterns, return interval, seasonality, fire severity, etc. Pyrodiversity at the level of a single burn patch recognizes that varying fire intensities, flame lengths, and fuel consumption create a mosaic of microhabitats and fine-scale environmental gradients, including differential heat penetration into the soil, varied removal of biomass, and heterogeneous patterns of ash deposition. These localized variations lead to diverse post-fire recruitment opportunities. Fire fosters structural complexity and generates a variety of niches within patches: it strongly influences vegetation structure, as fire severity correlates with the degree of openness in affected habitats (Kane et al., 2014). Fire also generates standing or lying deadwood (Chafe and Fried, 2025), which provides critical habitats for a wide range of species, contributing to the overall biodiversity of the community (Merganičová et al., 2012). 

### 4.3 Landscape structure 

Fires generate spatial, temporal, and functional heterogeneity at the landscape scale, that influence recovery trajectories, responses to potential subsequent disturbances as well as individual species responses and their distribution in the resulting mosaic. More specifically: 

Spatial heterogeneity refers to the creation of a fire mosaic (Turner and Gardner, 2015), consisting of patches that differ in disturbance severity, biotic legacies, and colonization sources. produce a mosaic of varying severities. The resulting mosaic influences the recovery trajectories of the system: if survival is high (e.g., due to the high presence of resprouters or remaining unburned patches) and the availability of seed bank is abundant, the size of the fire may not significantly affect recovery. In contrast, when survival and propagule availability are low, the size and spatial arrangement of the disturbed areas become critically important, impacting recolonization and recruitment. In such cases, random colonization events may drive succession in an entirely different direction (Turner and Gardner, 2015). Additionally, the resulting mosaic influences subsequent responses to potential disturbances (Chamberlain et al., 2024): landscape configuration forming barriers to disturbance propagation can render subsequent disturbances partially self-limiting (e.g., Collins et al., 2009). 

Temporal heterogeneity refers to the generation and persistence of patches in various successional stages, as a result of spatially and temporally localized fire events. This heterogeneity allows for the coexistence of a diversity of species at the landscape scale. Similar to spatial heterogeneity, the resulting assemblages exhibit varying levels of susceptibility and resistance to fire and other disturbances and offer a variety of colonization sources. 

Functional heterogeneity relates to heterogeneity as perceived through the functional needs of a species. Kelly et al. (2016) point out that the fire mosaic may contribute to the conservation and coexistence of species via four mechanisms: 

- (a) The habitat-complementation hypothesis, where multiple patches across the landscape collectively meet the resource requirements of individual species (configurational heterogeneity). 

- (b) The habitat-heterogeneity hypothesis, where increased variation in fire-generated patches enhances species coexistence (compositional heterogeneity). 

- (c) The habitat-refuge hypothesis, where the quality and spatial arrangement of the fire mosaic influence immediate survival and recolonization of species (e.g., affecting predator–prey dynamics). 

- (d) The fire-season hypothesis, where altering the fire season -for instance, through prescribed burning during cooler monthsreduces wildfire size, maintains a range of ecological ages across the landscape, and enhances the survival of multiple species (temporal variation). 

Thus, the diversity in the fire regime configures fire mosaics, catering to the diverse needs of multiple species. Fires have the capacity to construct niches and structure land mosaics, comprised of habitats of various compositions and successional stages, mediating optimal species coexistence. 

The above categories -spatial, temporal, functional heterogeneitycan be synthesized under the hypothesis that “Pyrodiversity begets biodiversity.” Recent studies (He et al., 2019; Steel et al., 2023) aim to identify the mechanisms behind this relationship, indicating that pyrodiversity is a highly context-specific phenomenon and highlighting how pyrodiversity can increase niche heterogeneity, stabilize community assembly through functional redundancy, and influence colonization and establishment dynamics. These processes are mediated by the evolutionary history of the ecosystem and the scale at which organisms perceive pyrodiversity (Steel et al., 2023). The clarification of mechanisms, the recognition of scale dependence, as well 

as the standardization of metrics are important steps toward the empirical testing of this hypothesis, as the diversity of metrics, scales, systems and taxa studied have resulted in ambiguous results in the literature (Jones and Tingley, 2021). Simple interpretations or correlations between a single biodiversity index and the fire regime are unlikely to identify critical feedbacks between the spatiotemporal fire patterns and the organization of biodiversity in food webs, as the effects of fire may manifest at multiple spatial or temporal scales, may be non-linear or dependent on other factors (Bowman et al., 2016). Often several studies are linking fire regime components to diversity (Densmore et al., 2023). Yet, other studies, due to their design, may fail to detect relationships predicted by the hypothesis that “Pyrodiversity begets biodiversity” (Jones and Tingley, 2021). However, it should be clearly noted that the pyrodiversity-biodiversity relationship is system specific, thus maximizing pyrodiversity does not necessarily lead to increased biodiversity (Kelly and Brotons, 2017). 

### 4.4 Ecosystem functions 

Disturbances contribute to the regulation of various ecosystem functions, including hydrological processes, carbon sequestration and emission dynamics, as well as numerous biological interactions such as pollination, predator–prey relationships, host–parasite interactions, and pest outbreaks. All the above are affecting ecosystem’s resilience and its capacity to self-organize and evolve over time. 

Resilience, being the product of the evolutionary history of the system (Holling, 1973) is maintained through several complementary mechanisms. 

First, during the reorganization phase of the adaptive cycle, the processes of decomposition, mineralization and retention, recycle nutrients making them again available and minimizing leaching (Holling, 1986). During this phase, soils and soil biodiversity are recognized as being of utmost importance for the retention of nutrients (Kobziar et al., 2024). 

Second, through the creation of a heterogeneous mosaic with patches in various stages of the adaptive cycle, which helps avert catastrophic disturbances. This mosaic creates barriers to the spread of subsequent disturbances and preserves recolonization sources for the regeneration of disturbed patches. In this way, interactions and feedbacks between the different organizational levels of the panarchy, which are crucial for the system’s creative regeneration, are conserved (Ghazoul, 2020). 

Third, disturbance is necessary for the reproduction of certain taxa, such as obligate seeders in fire-prone systems, through recruitment from canopy or soil seed banks (Lamont et al., 2020; Pausas and Lamont, 2022). While overly frequent disturbances lead to immaturity risk, the opposite is also true: if certain species are not disturbed within a specific time frame, they face the risk of senescence. Seeds are released but have lost their ability to germinate (Keeley and Pausas, 2022). 

Finally, disturbance enhances functional resilience by preventing competitive exclusion of species or even entire functional groups (Vasiliev, 2022). 

In terms of the remaining ecosystem functions, the restoration of the historic range of natural variability and the resulting heterogeneity can potentially regulate hydrologic processes. For example, Boisramé et al. (2017) demonstrated that the creation of gaps, within otherwise homogeneous forest cover, increased soil moisture and surface runoff. The resulting spatial heterogeneity also helps regulate the spread and intensity of pest outbreaks, improving the overall system health. 

In terms of carbon cycle regulation, Pausas and Keeley (2019) suggest that in a natural fire regime, the long-term carbon balance remains stable, as carbon emissions are offset by prior sequestration (Yue et al., 2016). Although recent research indicates that intensifying wildfire regimes are exceeding biological thresholds of resilience, potentially converting ecosystems to lower carbon-carrying capacities (Hudiburg et al., 2023), fires can contribute to soil carbon storage through the formation of charcoal, an extremely recalcitrant carbon pool (Santín et al., 2015), and, in some cases, by enhancing belowground carbon allocation to roots (Pausas et al., 2018). 

Finally, fire regulates biological interactions. For example, pollinator and flowering plant populations are larger in open habitats than in forests (Campbell et al., 2007; Hanula et al., 2015), and fire is the primary natural mechanism for creating such habitats in fire-prone landscapes. Increasing evidence indicates that landscape mosaics with diverse fire regimes and post-fire ages enhance the diversity of flowering plants and their pollinators (Ponisio et al., 2016; Brown et al., 2017; Carbone et al., 2019; Lazarina et al., 2019; Nakas et al., 2023, 2025). Predator–prey interactions may also be influenced by fire, as fire can improve prey detection by predators through the creation of openings, destroying refuge availability (Doherty et al., 2020). Population collapses (Leahy et al., 2016; McGregor et al., 2015, 2016), can be averted by restoring the spatiotemporal patterns of fire and thus the overall mosaic configuration -with young and mature patches- safeguarding refuge for prey species, and ultimately leading to the regulation of predator–prey interactions (Legge et al., 2011a; Legge et al., 2015). 

## 5 Anthropogenic fire-related creative disturbances: management principles 

As Pulliam and Johnson (2002) note, disturbance theory provides a powerful conceptual framework for interpreting management actions as disturbance regimes and for linking natural disturbance regimes with management practices. 

In relation to fire, from the 1970s onwards, during the transition from the “Balance of Nature” to the “Non-Equilibrium” paradigm, significant changes occurred in conservation goals (Parr and Andersen, 2006). The first transition was from fire exclusion to the tentative use of fire with specific spatiotemporal patterns. This was initially achieved through prescribed burning at regular intervals and later-following the recognition of the importance of heterogeneity and variability-by applying prescribed burns with greater variability and flexibility to create more diverse landscape mosaics (“Patch mosaic burning” in Parr and Andersen, 2006). The second transition involved shifting, in some cases, from a focus on controlling fire ignition risks to broader objectives, such as biodiversity conservation (van Wilgen et al., 2011). The third transition emphasized conserving not only mature communities but the full range of species assemblages across all successional stages (Christensen, 2014). Finally, the fourth transition moved from the management of 

vegetation alone to the management of entire food webs and the ecological interactions within them (Fraser et al., 2015; Bowman et al., 2016). 

As fire patterns are system-specific (Kelly and Brotons, 2017), their diversity may be specified, based on the functional traits of species (e.g., time to reproductive maturity, dispersal range), habitat requirements (fire severity, patch size and arrangement, young/mature patches ratio etc.) and fire thresholds so that fire is a self-limiting process (Collins et al., 2009). The above data may aid in delimiting thresholds for the diversity of spatiotemporal fire patterns (Moritz et al., 2013) for each landscape. This is an essential line of research in order to quantify ecological knowledge and establish intended targets-both regarding pyrodiversity and its ecological consequences- for each system. Such targets can then be translated into specific management actions and their outcomes assessed. 

Management actions upon the “background” of the natural disturbance regime should potentially “cultivate” biodiversity, construct niches, facilitate optimal species co-existence, and regulate ecological processes, guiding the manifestations of disturbance toward desirable spatiotemporal patterns. Management should attempt to manipulate the system using the historic range of variability as a baseline (Landres et al., 1999; Safford et al., 2012; Freeman et al., 2017): either conserving it or creatively transforming it toward a desired direction so that a better fit with the environment is achieved. Resilience provides guidance rather than an intrinsic objective: in some cases, it is desirable to maintain system identity, while in others, disturbance triggers the system’s transformation. Natural variability is an important factor in ecosystems management as both the stabilizing force of succession and the destabilizing force of disturbance (Holling, 1996) contribute to their conservation, self-organization, and evolution. What is maintained are the spatiotemporal relationships between individual components (an organizational structure) not the individual elements per se: _“The players may come and go, but the organization of relationships tends to be preserved and evolve”_ (Kurakin, 2011; Sundstrom and Allen, 2019). 

The following sections link the structural role of fire, as identified across the four analysis domains in Section 4 with creative fire management practices. The results are summarized in Table 1. 

### 5.1 Evolution 

In Section 4.1 we highlighted the intricate relationships of species adaptations with the related fire regime, the niche construction capabilities of the fire regime and the creativity of fire over evolutionary time-scales, in terms of species diversity and speciation rates. Firedriven evolutionary adaptations directly inform effective conservation and restoration strategies, as well as the transformation limits of the ecological system. Although functional traits might present plasticity - recent research has shown that evolution can occur more quickly than previously thought (Thompson, 1998; Hairston et al., 2005; Jones et al., 2023)- they do not always follow the accelerated and unpredictable changes in the context of global change. Species will present varying degrees of “fire naivety” to novel fire regimes, depending on whether they have been exposed to fire over evolutionary time (Guiote and Pausas, 2023) and if yes, on whether the functional traits effectively promote survival under novel conditions (Nimmo et al., 2021; Jones et al., 2023). The affected systems can mask indications of increasing fragility, thereby increasing the likelihood of crisis.

In the context of global change, management should strive to be incorporated in such a way to perpetuate fire’s evolutionary processes and creativity under novel fire regimes: a fundamental principle should be to safeguard evolutionary resilience (Sgrò et al., 2011). Management as a minimum should identify and respect the fire-traits along with the related disturbance regime and ideally strive to perpetuate evolutionary resilience and niche construction capacity under novel conditions. 

### 5.2 Community assembly 

Section 4.2 has highlighted the understanding of fire as a creative disturbance mediating species coexistence and maintaining specific ecological states, “the fire-climax,” an understanding central to managing mediterranean-type landscapes. Land managers can utilize strategies like prescribed burning to move fire prone landscape mosaics toward desired targets, increasing their resilience in the context of global change. Beyond simply restoring historical fire regimes, managers could intentionally shape fire characteristics -such as severity, spatial pattern, frequency and seasonality- to creatively foster specific community outcomes and soil properties. 

The above involves recognizing fire as an ecological filter that can be manipulated to promote the coexistence of species with diverse functional traits, particularly by balancing competitive advantages between pioneer and late-successional species. A central question is how to define this “intermediate” level of disturbance within individual patches so that the fire regime balances community productivity and competitive interactions (Huston, 1994, 2014), thereby maximizing species coexistence. How can one disturb a patch to construct new spatiotemporal niches? This approach should explicitly consider how different fire characteristics influence post-fire resource partitioning, priority effects, and the subsequent successional trajectories, ultimately guiding the emergence of sometimes novel, yet 

ecologically functional, species assemblages that are potentially more resilient to global change. The window of opportunity (Leverkus et al., 2019, 2021) generated by fire disturbance could be utilized to create new assemblages, more fitting to the environment, understanding the dynamic role of the reorganization phase of the adaptive cycle in the self-organization and evolution of ecosystems. 

Management directives can be enhanced by integrating the concept of ‘Pyrodiversity begets biodiversity’, that is by intentionally fostering a diversity of fire effects within individual patches. This targeted, within-patch heterogeneity directly contributes to maximizing species coexistence, structural complexity, functional redundancy and response diversity by providing a broader array of niches for different life-history strategies to exploit. 

All interventions should be of an adaptive nature, highlighting the need for continuous monitoring (e.g., species richness, functional group presence, genetic markers) and adjustments based on observed community responses, ensuring that fire management creatively reinforces biodiversity through both ecological processes and natural selection, rather than leading to ecological degradation. 

### 5.3 Landscape structure 

Section 4.3 has highlighted the diverse manifestations of heterogeneity (spatial, temporal and functional) and the importance of pyrodiversity in structuring landscapes. Management should aim to consciously co-shape spatial heterogeneity through pyrodiversity, varying the characteristics of the disturbance regime -e.g. severity, spatial pattern, and frequency- across broader areas and acknowledging the context-specific nature of “Pyrodiversity begets biodiversity.” This means developing management plans that target optimal levels of pyrodiversity within the fire regime -rather than maximizing it universally- as optimal pyrodiversity varies depending on the ecosystem’s evolutionary history and the scale at which organisms perceive these disturbances (Steel et al., 2023). 

Localized disturbance events at different times foster temporal heterogeneity, creating a landscape with diverse successional stages that support a greater number of species -from opportunistic colonizers to highly competitive species in older patches- as species diversity is directly linked to habitat heterogeneity (MacArthur and MacArthur, 1961). This is the application of the IDH at the landscape level. Management efforts should aim to guide disturbance and successional trajectories to increase both beta ( _β_ ) and gamma ( _γ_ ) diversity, either by aligning with historical disturbance regimes or by actively co-shaping fire patterns to direct landscape development toward desired ecological states. Practical applications could include designing prescribed burning programs that vary in intensity and return intervals or letting managed wildfires burn across a landscape, with the aim of creating a complex mosaic of burned and unburned areas. 

Considering the functional needs of species, managers can consciously use fire in species conservation both for pyrophylic species, as well as those species that have not traditionally been associated with fire but are dependent on land mosaics that fire co-shapes (Jones and Tingley, 2021). As pointed out in Section 4.3, fires can configure multiple patches across the landscape that meet the resource requirements of individual species, enhance species coexistence or provide refuge regulating predator–prey dynamics.

The patch size-configuration and viable seed dispersal is expected to change and play a significant role under future global change scenarios. 

### 5.4 Ecosystem functions 

Section 4.4 identified the creative role of fire in maintaining the system’s resilience and regulating a host of ecosystem functions. By interacting with the system’s natural variability, management can 

intentionally co-shape disturbance patterns to regulate ecosystem processes. These include regulating subsequent disturbances to avert catastrophic patterns, regulating hydrological processes, shaping carbon sequestration and emissions dynamics, as well as biological interactions. All the above are achieved through the establishment of heterogeneous mosaics, with heterogeneity manifesting itself at suitable spatiotemporal scales. 

Regarding the aversion of catastrophic disturbances, through the application of appropriate disturbance patterns-e.g., with the use of prescribed burning, grazing or even mechanical vegetation clearance and selective logging-we may contribute to the formation of a heterogeneous mosaic so that the spread of a subsequent fire is self-limited and its severity regulated (Collins et al., 2009). This creates barriers to its spread and conserves colonization sources for the regeneration of disturbed patches. 

Furthermore, the restoration of historic fire regimes and the formation of a heterogeneous mosaic is also critical for regulating hydrological processes, a fact known to indigenous people guiding cultural burning. Suitable burning patterns lead to a reduction in vegetation water needs and an increase in water availability (surface runoff and soil humidity), renewing water flow in streams and rivers. This has guided the planning of villages of indigenous people and the movement of large mammals in the landscape, ultimately influencing hunting practices (Anderson and Keeley, 2018). 

It is also assumed that restoring the Historic Range of Variability (HRV) can regulate the dynamics of carbon emissions and sequestration. A fire event may convert the system from a carbon sink to a source of carbon; however, if the fire is not severe and catastrophic in extent, emissions are controllable, and carbon balance is restored in the medium term. In forests with fire exclusion, management actions that reduce fuel continuity—and emit carbon—regulate long-term dynamics by averting catastrophic wildfires and the release of large carbon stocks (Hurteau et al., 2008). Strategic fuel reductions can ultimately regulate climate through greater carbon storage and higher albedo, leading to local temperature reductions. However, determining the Carbon Carrying Capacity (CCC; Moritz et al., 2013) should consider multiple factors such as biodiversity conservation, landscape heterogeneity, and the regulation of other ecological processes. One should not blindly aim to maximize it, an attempt that would not be sustainable in the long term anyway. 

Creative destructions present an opportunity to regulate host– parasite interactions: since many parasites spend a stage of their life cycle in the soil, fire may benefit hosts (wild fauna or livestock) by reducing parasite loads and disease transmission (Scasta, 2015; Álvarez-Ruiz et al., 2021). Furthermore, in cases of insect outbreaks, appropriate fire patterns reduce the extent and connectivity of mature forest patches, thereby regulating the spread of outbreaks. 

Landscape heterogeneity permits a variety of habitats and niches, enhancing species coexistence and regulating biotic interactions. In predator–prey dynamics, the heterogeneous mosaic—with conservation of mature patches that function as refuges from predators (Huffaker, 1958)—allows for prey conservation (Doherty et al., 2020). ## 6 Case studies of creative fire management 

We conclude this review with a critical commentary of fire-related case studies from MTEs around the globe (Figure 1): managed wildfires, prescribed burning, pyrosilviculture, shepherding for wildfire adaptation and cultural burning. These examples illustrate how fire management can creatively structure landscapes and construct niches by creating patch mosaics and resetting successional trajectories, altering the competitive hierarchies among species. Management impacts on community assembly, landscape heterogeneity, the regulation of ecosystem functions and overall ecosystem health and resilience are succinctly presented. 

### 6.1 Yosemite National Park, California - Managed wildfires 

Managed wildfires refer to fires with a natural ignition source that are allowed to burn freely and are only controlled under certain conditions, to meet restoration objectives (Boisramé et al., 2017; Davis et al., 2022). Yosemite National Park is an emblematic example where wildfire was re-established as the natural “manager” of the forest, after a long period of fire suppression, fuel accumulation and homogenization. The benefits were numerous, including the restoration of the complexity of the landscape structure, increased 

species coexistence, as well as the regulation of hydrological processes and the enhancement of the overall ecosystem health and resilience. 

Over time, spatial heterogeneity was restored, with discrete early successional patches reappearing among mature homogeneous forest (Boisramé et al., 2017). Forest cover declined by 22%, while meadows increased by 200% and shrublands by 24%. The remaining forest was also more diverse in terms of structure and composition (tree density, basal area and canopy cover) (Collins and Stephens 2010; Collins et al., 2009). Greater spatial heterogeneity resulted in greater functional diversity, increased species diversity and the regulation of biotic interactions, as documented by various studies (refer to Stephens et al., 2021 for references). In terms of community assembly, numerous species benefited from the resulting habitat heterogeneity, different successional stages, burn severity, and the diversity in patch arrangement that covered their needs (feeding, nesting, refuge, etc.) across alternate areas of the mosaic. 

The hydrologic cycle was regulated: increased soil humidity was recorded in the patches of newly created meadows compared to reduced humidity in forest patches, along with an increase in stream water flow relative to neighboring watersheds (Boisramé et al., 2017; Boisramé et al., 2018), but without observed soil erosion (Rakhmatulina and Thompson, 2020). 

Overall ecosystem health and regulation improved substantially (Boisramé et al., 2017): reduced connectivity, tree density, and competition for water led to a significantly reduced tree mortality rate due to pest outbreaks and drought In 2014, only four (4) trees per km<sup>2</sup> of forest died in areas under a managed wildfire regime, compared to approximately 47 trees per km<sup>2</sup> of forest in fire suppressed watersheds (Boisramé et al., 2017). ### 6.2 Chios island, Greece - Prescribed burning 

Prescribed burning (PB) in the Mediterranean basin remains limited to fuel reduction in Portugal, Spain and southern France. The Chios island pilot project (Solomou et al., 2025) aimed to examine in a more integrated way the relationship between intensity of burn and its effects on plant biodiversity, plant water potential and soil properties, using PB as a tool to enhance socio-ecological and climate resiliency, potentially extrapolating the results to low-intensity wildfires. The study results verify the potential of prescribed burning to enhance biodiversity and resilience, while managing fuel loads and wildfire risk. 

In terms of community assembly, it was observed that PB boosted plant diversity after fire, with Asteraceae, Fabaceae and Poaceae being especially abundant in the early regeneration stages. This post-fire pattern is typical of early regeneration assemblages in Mediterranean shrublands and open woodlands, where an early peak in species richness and evenness is driven by the pulse of annual plant forms and the persistence of resprouting shrubs (Carrari et al., 2022; Kazanis and Arianoutsou, 1996; Verroios and Georgiadis, 2002; Kazanis and Arianoutsou, 2004). The increased diversity can be attributed to the re-entry of pioneer species that had disappeared because of competition, fire-cued germination breaking seed dormancy, the coexistence of obligate seeders with resprouters, and the generation of patchiness, promoting niche complementarity: obligate seeders predominate on hotter more open microsites, while resprouters occupy lightly affected patches. Regular low-intensity fires can prevent competitive exclusion and maintain a functional balance between seeders and resprouters (Capitanio and Carcaillet, 2008; Bonanomi et al., 2022). The study revealed a nonlinear relationship between intensity of burn (fireline intensity / length of flame) and Shannon diversity: low intensity fires promote diversity by eliminating dominant species, while high-intensity wildfires reduce diversity by eliminating sensitive species (Weeks et al., 2023), thus diversity peeks at intermediate severity levels (Richter et al., 2019). 

Soil responses showed variability from site to site: in some sites there was a decline in organic carbon, because of a reduction to combustible material. The same was observed for available nitrogen, that takes three to four years to recover (Kazanis and Arianoutsou, 2004). In other sites, an increase in exchangeable cations like calcium and magnesium was observed, as ash enriches the soil. The variability of the study results highlighted the importance of site-specific long-term monitoring. 

Finally, leaf water potential was in line with seasonal differences, implying the interaction of PB with weather conditions. Thus, seasonality should be taken into account when planning prescribed burns, so as to manage more effectively water stress subsequently. 

### 6.3 Dry western forests, California-Pyrosilviculture 

Pyrosilviculture refers to the integration of silvicultural thinning with fire (prescribed burning and managed wildfires) at the landscape level promoting resilience (North et al., 2021), in the dry western United States forests. Following fire suppression and in the context of 

increasingly severe and frequent wildfires and drought, pyrosilviculture is a way to re-instate a complex disturbance regime, emulating variability and transforming potentially catastrophic wildfires to creative fire. More specifically, the practice aims to achieve the combined benefits of fuel reduction for fire adaptation, the harvesting of forest products for revenue, as well as a host of ecosystem services, including habitat creation and landscape heterogeneity. The resulting landscape heterogeneity provides a self-reinforcing pattern that makes forests more resilient to future fires (Koontz et al., 2020). Areas of dedicated silvicultural thinning and prescribed burning and areas where managed wildfires are allowed to burn are distributed spatially based on safety, operability and intended targets. Mechanical thinning takes place near roads and inhabited areas at strategic locations that generate ignition points where fire can both expand and be controlled (Van de Water and North, 2010; North and Hurteau, 2011). Structural restoration is complemented by fire to re-establish the underlying ecological functions (Stephens et al., 2020a), such as nutrient cycling, soil respiration, decomposition and large snag creation associated with niches for a variety of wildlife species (e.g., Steel et al., 2019). Accelerated fire restoration can decrease significantly carbon losses and the area affected by severe forest fire (Liang et al., 2018). Thinning and prescribed burning take place at a smaller area with a dispersed pattern, while managed wildfires run over remote areas. Mechanical thinning achieves precise targets in a controlled manner, while fire achieves large scale density reduction at much lower costs. The repeated application of low-to-moderate severity managed and prescribed fire has the capacity to build faster adaptability and resilience into ecosystems than traditional thinning treatments focused on producing a target stand density and diameter distribution (North et al., 2021). Thus, pyrosilviculture can potentially guide fire’s evolutionary processes, through re-establishing landscape mosaics and driving selection among fire-adapted species, accelerating adaptation in the context of global change. 

### 6.4 Catalonia, Spain - Shepherding for wildfire adaptation 

Shepherding for wildfire adaptation refers to rotational grazing practices that slow down the spread and reduce the intensity of fire, while simultaneously generating desired land mosaics and supporting local bio-economies (Schlickman and Milligan, 2023). In dialog with cultural burning, it pertains to areas where the abandonment of the countryside and the cessation of agrosilvopastoral activities -which have co-shaped the landscape over millennia- have resulted in dense homogeneous forests. A successful example are the “Ramats de Foc” (Fire flocks) initiative in Catalonia, Spain managed by the Pau Costa foundation (Pau Costa Foundation, 2026), engaging a network of stakeholders: landowners, local shepherds, forest services, firefighters, governmental agencies and local retail stores. Schlickman and Milligan (2023) mention the following management objectives: First, the creation of fuel breaks -interrupting horizontal and vertical fuel continuity- to reduce crown fire risk, flame size, fire intensity and slow down fire spread. Second, the revival of agropastoral activities and the protection of local breeds, many of them under extinction risk. Third, acting like an intermediate disturbance, biodiversity and landscape heterogeneity increases by generating in collaboration with firefighters strategic clearings, where firefighters can later intervene and control fire behavior if needed. ### 6.5 Great Western Woodlands - Ngadju tribe, southwestern Australia - Cultural burning 

In the Great Western Woodlands in south-western Australia the Ngadju tribe applied a highly sophisticated fire regime (Prober et al., 2016) up until the disruption caused by western colonization. They burned selectively, maintaining a balance between frequent and constrained use of fire, that was guided by edaphically driven mosaics of fire-prone and fire-resistant vegetation in combination with the intended intensity of use. The selective application of fire is consistent with findings indicating that aboriginal burning was more restrained in the more arid landscapes of Australia (Hallam, 1975; Abbott, 2003) in comparison to tropical and temperate zones. Ngadju paid great attention to fine-scale detail when applying fire, protecting fire-sensitive elements, conserving unburned patches for fauna and demonstrating deep understanding of the ecological effects of fire. Overtime, the landscape they inhabited was “designed” from the bottom-up, as the sum of precise local interventions and purposeful niche construction. 

As a general comment, aboriginal burning was a resource management strategy: it sculpted habitats to cater to the preferences of particular species and optimize the sourcing of food and materials. Indirectly, fire-stick farming “cultivated” land mosaics with greater heterogeneity and biodiversity, constructing niches for a multitude of species to persevere over time (Bliege Bird et al., 2008, 2013; Bowman, 2021). 

While the total burn area seems to be mediated by climate (Gill et al., 2000; Van Wilgen et al., 2004; Archibald et al., 2009), management could and did manipulate the heterogeneity and intensity of fire (Trauernicht et al., 2015). Aborigines all over Australia shaped pyrodiversity by introducing point ignitions and by burning during cooler and wetter months when vegetation was moist enough to regulate the intensity and spread of fire, thus mitigating the impact of subsequent wildfires, when they did occur. In terms of landscape structure, the resulting mosaics were characterized by smaller patch sizes, greater ecotone density, and a higher Shannon diversity index (Bliege Bird et al., 2008, 2013) compared to lightning-generated mosaics. Furthermore, simulations have shown that regimes with many small fires increase the total diversity of patch ages as well as the probability of conserving mature and old-growth patches in contrast to regimes with sparser fires covering the same total area (Bliege Bird et al., 2008; Trauernicht et al., 2015). Plant diversity and abundance also increase as a result of the pyrodiversity introduced through aboriginal hunting practices (Greenwood, 2024). 

Landscapes characterized by long histories of such ecosystem engineering eventually become adapted to the above niche-constructing activities, even dependent upon them. The disruption of aboriginal practices following colonization can be understood as the removal of a key niche-constructing agent, leading to the breakdown of established eco-evolutionary feedbacks, biodiversity loss, increased fuel accumulation and homogenization (Fletcher et al., 2021; Williamson, 2022), and a shift toward high-severity fire regimes (Bowman et al., 2022; Mariani et al., 2022). 

## 7 Discussion, future research directions, risks and challenges 

From 1970 onwards, with the gradual establishment of the NonEquilibrium paradigm, the perception of linear time and evolution, as expressed in Clementsian succession and classic Western science, was debunked. Succession does not tend toward stability and homeostasis, a safe and comfortable belief; often, the opposite is true (Christensen, 2014). Episodic change (disturbance) is recognized as an integral event of dynamic systems and an essential stage in their conservation, development and evolution (Holling, 1986, 1987). The paradox of maintaining natural variability as a prerequisite for the conservation as well as creative transformation of ecological systems is acknowledged (Holling and Meffe, 1996). 

This paradigm shift should not only be established in ecological policy but also communicated to the public. It is necessary for the ecological and evolutionary role of fire and disturbance in general to be understood so that they are not perceived solely as catastrophic events, as undoubtedly, they also are in the era of global change. Changing our mental model is an important first step in addressing critical issues in the context of altered disturbance regimes, mega-fires, and the increasing coupling of climatic extremes with anthropogenic pressures. 

Although large severe disturbances (Large Infrequent Disturbances, LIDs) possess their own ecological function and are part of the historic range of variability in certain systems, among all landscape dynamics described by Turner et al. (1993), the most acceptable disturbance pattern for managing human systems seems to be small and frequent disturbances, achieving what is called a management variability (Landres et al., 1999). This is how a “steady state in a shifting mosaic” is achieved, through feedback mechanisms influencing disturbance frequency (Pickett and White, 1985). This strategy has been tested over millennia by indigenous communities “cultivating” biodiversity at a human scale, increasing the carrying capacity of the landscape they inhabit and enhancing the probability of conserving all successional stages and related fauna in the land mosaic (Bliege Bird et al., 2008, 2013, 2020; Trauernicht et al., 2015), regulating ecosystems function and rendering their management sustainable in the long term. Incorporating traditional ecological knowledge into contemporary management is therefore essential, as convergent findings across indigenous cultures worldwide highlight the effectiveness of small, frequent, and context-sensitive disturbances in sustaining biodiversity and ecosystem function (Huffman, 2013). 

This way, in contemporary societies, disturbance may be expressed in spatiotemporal patterns that are more easily accepted by the community, as both perceived and real negative socioeconomic and ecological impacts are mitigated. Societal and economic values are reasonably protected from catastrophic disturbances, while pursuing optimal species coexistence and regulating ecological processes within the landscape mosaic. Management considers human needs while constituting a designed experiment that attempts to decipher the landscape by interacting with it: “cultivating” niches, “designing” appropriate habitat conditions for other organisms, and influencing the expression of ecological processes. 

The principles presented in this paper aim to guide such management at a conceptual level. Their operationalization requires significant further research. Priority areas include the development and standardization of metrics and models for variations in disturbance regimes, linked with their hierarchical and multidimensional ecological and biophysical impacts, so that the desired range of variability for each system is defined. An important line of research that arises in this context is that of less studied biological interactions with disturbance, moving away from the basal levels of the trophic web and incorporating organisms like animals, fungi and other soil organisms, expanding research that has been undertaken in the past decade (e.g., Pausas and Parr, 2018; Jones et al., 2023). Traditional subjects under continuous study remain the transformation of fire regimes and their socio-ecological impacts in the context of global change, as well as linked and compounded disturbances (such as fire and grazing or fire and environmental stress like drought). 

While this review emphasizes the creative and structural role of fire, the successful application of fire as a management tool depends on a set of critical prerequisites and contextual conditions. Fire does not inherently produce beneficial outcomes; its creative role emerges only when disturbance regimes align with the ecological characteristics, evolutionary history and spatial scale of the system. Under altered fire regimes -such as excessively frequent or severe megafires- disturbance may instead lead to biodiversity loss, ecosystem degradation and transitions to undesired states. 

The implementation of creative fire management therefore requires robust ecological knowledge, including an understanding of species traits, disturbance thresholds, and system-specific responses. Continuous monitoring and adaptive management are essential to evaluate outcomes and adjust interventions over time. 

In addition, governance and cross-sector collaboration play a critical role in enabling such approaches. Effective acquisition and implementation of ecological knowledge depend on institutional arrangements that facilitate collaboration among scientists, policymakers and stakeholders, enabling successful conservation, adaptation and transformation through the conduct of research experiments at suitable scales. Social acceptance (Novak et al., 2023), risk (loss of values) and perception of risk (Bean and Evans, 2023) are key obstacles to managing applied creative disturbances for the benefit of the community and the overall ecological system, as societal perceptions often favor fire suppression over proactive disturbance management. In most cases, trade-offs need to be identified, as no single disturbance regime will be optimal for all ecosystem services (Fernandes, 2013). To this end, the integration of traditional knowledge with formal sciencebased practices along with the definition of clear and measurable objectives will be of the utmost advantage. Overcoming the above barriers is a key challenge in operationalizing the principles outlined in this review, safeguarding the creative capacity of targeted fire management.
