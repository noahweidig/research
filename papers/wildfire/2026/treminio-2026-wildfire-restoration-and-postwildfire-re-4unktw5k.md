# Wildfire, Restoration, and Postwildfire Rehabilitation Effects on Wind Erosion in the Great Basin

## Abstract

Restoration of degraded areas and postdisturbance rehabilitation after wildfire encompass critical approaches for reducing and reversing impacts of wind erosion and sand and dust storms (SDS). However, the broad outcomes of dryland restoration and rehabilitation for wind erosion and SDS remain underexplored. Wind erosion is an emerging issue in the Great Basin of the western United States, exacerbated by invasive annual grasses and associated wildfire. Here, we assess potential wind erosion and SDS responses to wildfire, restoration, and postwildfire rehabilitation treatments at the regional scale in the Great Basin. We used 13 years of rangeland monitoring data, the Aeolian EROsion model, and the Land Treatment Digital Library to produce counterfactual model-predictions to estimate treatment effects. Our results, based on AERO model estimates of aeolian sediment flux, Q , revealed potential reductions across three management contexts compared to controls: 6.76 % decrease in Q in wildfire-affected regions, 54.3 and 58.0 % decrease in Q in for soil disturbance and aerial seeding restoration areas, and 49.8 to 56.0% decrease in Q in postwildfire rehabilitation treated sites. In particular, aerial seeding and soil disturbance restoration treatments, and postwildfire closure-treatments had higher perennial grass cover and the most decreased Q compared to untreated controls. These results represent an important regional scale assessment of wind erosion responses to restoration and postwildfire rehabilitation. Our findings underscore the application of integrating wind erosion and SDS mitigation into restoration and postdisturbance rehabilitation programs to provide land managers with strategies to reduce land degradation while fostering ecosystem resilience.

# **1. Introduction** 

59 Wind erosion and sand and dust storms (SDS) are symptoms and drivers of land degradation that 60 impact agriculture, ecosystems, and societies globally. Policy initiatives and practical guidance 61 for mitigating wind erosion and SDS, including the United Nations Convention to Combat 62 Desertification (UNCCD) Sand and Dust Storms Coalition (UNCCD, 2022), World Overview of 63 Conservation Approaches and Technologies (WOCAT) Sustainable Land Management Database 64 (WOCAT, 2025), and USDA Dust Mitigation Handbook (Smarik et al., 2019), support the 65 challenge of avoiding, reducing, and reversing wind erosion and SDS impacts. However, while 66 integrating wind erosion and SDS mitigation into restoration and rehabilitation programs to 67 address land degradation is increasingly recognized as important (UNCCD Decision 27/COP.16, 68 2024), studies linking land management to wind erosion and SDS remain limited to certain land 69 uses and practices. 

70 Research on wind erosion and SDS mitigation has largely focused on impact reduction for 71 cropland (e.g., Jarrah et al., 2020; Pi et al., 2020), transportation, and energy extraction 72 infrastructure (Middleton and Al-Hemoud 2024; Duniway et al. 2019). Rangelands have received 73 comparatively less attention (van Oudenhoven et al., 2015; Webb et al., 2017). The dearth of 74 rangeland wind erosion and SDS mitigation research contrasts with studies, “on-the-ground” 75 application, and formal programs that support rangeland restoration and post-disturbance 76 rehabilitation (e.g., Allred et al., 2021; Bestelmeyer, 2006; Eba Tebeje et al., 2014; Mganga et al.,

While dryland restoration and rehabilitation objectives often encompass soil stabilization 78 and returning ecosystem functions to a more desirable condition (Copeland et al., 2018; Ma et 79 al., 2017; Pilliod et al., 2021; Shriver et al., 2019), explicitly examining treatment outcomes and 80 co-benefits for wind erosion and SDS mitigation provides an opportunity for direct 81 understanding of these relationships (e.g., Miller et al., 2012; Morra et al., 2024; Pyke et al., 82 2013; van Oudenhoven et al., 2015). As wind erosion and SDS hotspots are typically dispersed 83 across landscapes, understanding the regional-scale outcomes of restoration and rehabilitation is 84 essential for identifying effective practices and for informing policy and programs to combat 85 wind erosion and SDS (Ma et al., 2017; Mganga et al., 2015; van Oudenhoven et al., 2015). 86 Incorporating wind erosion and SDS into assessments of restoration and rehabilitation treatment 87 outcomes could yield insights into treatment practices that effectively enhance mitigation 88 strategies that also reduce wind erosion risk. 

89 In the Great Basin of the western United States, wind erosion and dust storms are emerging 90 issues due to a myriad of interacting factors (Treminio et al., 2024). Exotic annual grasses, such 91 as cheatgrass ( _Bromus tectorum_ ), are expanding in range (Boyte and Wylie, 2016; Pastick et al., 92 2020) and have contributed to more frequent and severe wildfires (Balch et al., 2013), loss of 93 historical plant communities (Chambers et al., 2016; Ellsworth et al., 2020; Svejcar et al., 2017), 94 and increased post-fire aeolian sediment transport (Morra et al., 2024; Sankey et al., 2009a, 95 2009b; Treminio et al., 2024; Wagenbrenner et al., 2013). Agricultural practices (Chambers and 96 Wisdom, 2009), overgrazing (Field et al., 2011), urbanization (Chambers and Wisdom, 2009), 97 and mining (Morris and Rowe, 2014) have further increased wind erosion risk. Extensive 98 restoration and post-fire rehabilitation treatments have been implemented throughout the Great 99 Basin, with a focus on stabilizing soils, controlling invasive species, improving wildlife habitat, 100 and enhancing forage quality and quantity for livestock (Pilliod et al., 2017). However, while 101 many studies have assessed treatment outcomes for plant communities (Peppin et al., 2010) and 102 wildlife (e.g., Knutson et al., 2014; Simler-Williamson and Germino, 2022), overall treatment 103 effects on wind erosion and SDS risk across the Great Basin remain unquantified. 

104 Assessing rangeland treatment outcomes is inherently challenging. Efforts to assess the gains to 105 ecosystem functioning resulting from restoration and post-disturbance rehabilitation have been 106 inconsistent and insufficiently addressed due to large gaps in application of methodology, 107 standardized assessment criteria, and comprehensive long-term monitoring (e.g., Carlucci et al., 108 2020; Kollmann et al., 2016; Prach et al., 2019). Randomized-controlled studies, which may be 109 used to elucidate causal treatment effects, are often constrained by small sample sizes and 110 logistical limitations, making them unsuitable for assessing regional restoration and 111 rehabilitation outcomes. Consequently, observational studies are common. An emerging 112 approach to elucidate causal treatment effects from observational studies is counterfactual 113 analysis, which uses synthetic controls based on sites with similar ecological potential to test 114 treatment outcomes using remote sensing (e.g., Fick et al., 2021; McNellis et al., 2023; Nauman 115 et al., 2017). Recently, propensity score matching (PSM), has been used with field monitoring 116 and remotely sensed data to address the limitations of observational studies by simulating the 117 balanced treatment assignment seen in randomly controlled studies (e.g., Kluender et al., 2024; 118 Simler-Williamson and Germino, 2022). 125 Here, we assess potential wind erosion and SDS responses to rangeland restoration and post126 wildfire rehabilitation treatments across the Great Basin at the regional scale. Applying 134 the AERO model to AIM monitoring plots sampled (2011-2023) across treatments represented in 135 the LTDL, we used PSM and statistical methods to elucidate rangeland restoration and post136 wildfire rehabilitation effects on potential wind erosion. Here, due to sampling constraints, we 137 focus on wind erosion responses to the most recent wildfires and treatments, recognizing that 138 many areas of the Great Basin have experienced multiple wildfires and treatments over the last 139 century.

142 The United States Geological Survey’s Land Treatment Digital Library (LTDL) is a database 143 containing information on known rangeland treatments conducted on US public lands managed 144 by the BLM (Pilliod et al., 2019). # 2.3. Statistical analyses 

271 The _marginaleffects_ R package (Arel-Bundock et al., 2024) was used to conduct G-computation 272 to estimate treatment effects on aeolian sediment transport across the Great Basin. This method 273 — a statistical approach for causal inference when randomized controlled trials are not feasible 274 — enables the estimation of treatment effects from observational data (Naimi et al., 2017; 275 Schafer and Kang, 2008; Snowden et al., 2011). A five-step approach was employed to estimate 276 treatment effects on wind erosion. First, predicted aeolian horizontal sediment flux ( _Q_ , g m � ¹ 277 d � ¹) was natural-log transformed when _Q_ > 0. For _Q_ = 0, a value of 1 was added to allow 278 inclusion in the natural-log transformation. The natural-log transformed _Q_ (Ln _Q_ ) served as the 279 response variable to better fit the assumption of normally distributed errors in multiple linear 280 regression. Second, multilinear regression models with interaction terms between treatment and 281 the covariates used in PSM were fit for each treatment within each category type to predict 282 responses from observed data. Including these covariates in the outcome model ensures a doubly 283 robust estimate of treatment effects by addressing model misspecification and balancing 284 covariates between treated and non-treated plots (Hall et al., 2014). Third, counterfactual 285 outcomes were generated by applying the fitted model(s) to simulate results under alternative 286 treatment conditions. To illustrate, a fitted model is used to predict outcomes for each plot in the 287 dataset as if it had either not been treated (control) or had received treatment. Fourth, the average 288 predicted counterfactual outcomes for both treatment and control scenarios were calculated and 289 tested to determine whether they differ significantly from zero. This revealed the impact of each 290 treatment, or lack thereof, on potential wind erosion. Fifth, the overall treatment effect was 291 determined by calculating the difference between the average predicted outcomes for treated and 292 control scenarios and testing whether this difference was significantly different from zero. We 293 note that under G-computation, the coefficients of covariates from fitting multiple linear 294 regression cannot be interpreted as causal relationships to treatment designations and thus their 295 effect on Ln _Q_ . - 300 For no-treatment plots, we estimated how wildfire influenced sediment transport relative to plant 301 cover by comparing between plots sampled within one-year post-wildfire and plots with no 302 wildfire history, sampled in the same year. - 305 To further investigate the role of the five plant functional groups, post-hoc analyses of 306 statistically significant overall treatment effects (i.e., results of the fifth step above) on _Q_ were 307 conducted using permutation tests for restoration and post-wildfire rehabilitation plots. 308 Permutation tests assessed whether mean cover for the five plant functional groups differed 309 between treated and control plots. The plant groups were chosen because: 1) they provide finer310 grained indices of vegetation cover covariates used in PSM and G-computation; 2) their cover is 311 influenced by, or results from, restoration and post-wildfire rehabilitation treatments in the Great 312 Basin; and 3) they avoid the circularity of reusing covariates employed in PSM and G- 313 computation. Permutation tests were used because they do not require assumptions of normality 314 or homoscedasticity. In this procedure, treatment assignments were repeatedly shuffled within 315 the dataset to simulate a null hypothesis of no difference between groups. For each permutation, 316 a distribution under the null hypothesis was created for the mean difference between groups. The 317 observed difference in mean plant functional group cover between treatment and untreated 318 controls was then compared to this distribution. The _p_ -value was computed as the proportion of 319 permuted test statistics that were as extreme as, or more than, the observed difference. A total of 320 N = 10,000 permutations were used to generate the null distributions, and a significance level of 321 α = 0.05 was applied. All statistical analyses were conducted in R v. 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024) 322 and RStudio v. 27771613 (RStudio Team, 2025). Figures were created using the _ggplot2_ R 323 package (Wickham, 2016).

# 3.1. Aeolian sediment transport response to wildfire without treatments 

335 We present the response of natural log-transformed _Q_ , where a unit change in value represents an 336 order of magnitude change in the sediment transport rate. The overall effect of wildfire on 341 aeolian sediment transport was -0.070 Ln _Q_ ( _p =_ 0.364), suggesting that plots with wildfire 342 history, on average, experience slightly less aeolian sediment transport than those without 343 wildfire history. However, this difference was not statistically significant (Table 4). One-year 344 post-wildfire monitoring revealed that median estimates of _Q_ in burned plots were higher than in 345 plots with no wildfire history in some but not all years (Figure 2b). Elevated _Q_ within 12 months 346 of wildfire resulted from reduced plant functional group cover, but annual and perennial grasses 347 can recover in this time to levels matching unburned plots (Figure � 3) and to percent cover 348 ranges linked to the greatest decline in wind erosion.

Integrating wind erosion and SDS assessments into dryland restoration and rehabilitation initiatives offers valuable insights into treatment outcomes that can enhance mitigation. This study supports such integration by using counterfactual model-estimates from observational monitoring data and the AERO model to determine that some restoration and post-wildfire rehabilitation treatments have mitigated wind erosion risk in the Great Basin over the period 2011-2023. While responses were sometimes small (Ln _Q_ < 0; g m<sup>-1</sup> d<sup>-1</sup> ), the combined effects of wildfire occurrence, restoration and rehabilitation treatments, and their interaction suggest a tendency for reducing mean predicted aeolian sediment transport rates. In most cases, restoration and post-wildfire rehabilitation treatment responses were more negative (i.e., magnitude comparisons) than the predicted mean aeolian sediment transport rates for untreated controls. Although the effects of most treatments were not different from zero (i.e., statistical difference), the direction of the treatment effect consistently tended toward reduced aeolian sediment transport rates across comparisons. Moreover, in monitoring plots where treatment effects were statistically significant, we observed differences in annual and/or perennial grass cover between treated sites and untreated controls, that are described below. These findings suggest that restoration and rehabilitation treatments across the Great Basin, have likely increased annual and/or perennial grass cover and decreased bare ground, which in turn reduced the susceptibility of landscapes to wind erosion and dust emission. 

501 Rangeland monitoring plots with a history of wildfire but no treatments had lower mean 502 predicted aeolian sediment transport rates than plots with no fire history. 568 In this study, we applied propensity score matching (PSM) with model-estimated counterfactual 569 treatment effects to evaluate how dryland restoration and rehabilitation treatments influence 570 potential wind erosion. By maintaining a consistent set of covariates across both PSM and 571 multiple linear regression analyses, we ensured that the causal impact of the treatments could be 572 assessed at a regional scale. While individual effects of covariates on treatment responses remain 573 challenging to interpret (Westreich and Greenland, 2013), the approach presented in our analyses 574 provides a valuable opportunity for refining methodologies that assess wind erosion and SDS 575 dynamics. The covariates — capturing plant cover attributes that influence wind erosion — offer 576 a foundation for expanding studies that compare burned and unburned locations, as well as 577 restoration and rehabilitation treatments, across diverse environmental contexts. Future work 578 incorporating larger sample sizes and mixed-model statistical approaches presents an opportunity 579 to examine how specific factors, such as soil texture, influence treatment outcomes and interact 580 with restoration strategies, ultimately advancing the applicability of treatments and their effect on 581 wind erosion mitigation. 

582 Our use of multiple linear regressions, driven by sample size constraints, provides an opportunity 583 to explore advanced modeling approaches in future studies. While mixed-model frameworks 584 accounting for random effects can enhance population-level inferences, integrating these 585 methods in future research could improve our understanding of group-level heterogeneity. For 586 example, addressing temporal and spatial variability through expanded datasets may allow for 587 the incorporation of time since treatment and preceding treatment effects as variables of interest. 588 With larger sample sizes, within-region variability could be modeled as random effects, 589 contributing to broader generalizability. Additionally, leveraging remote sensing and gridded data 590 presents exciting possibilities for strengthening observational insights (e.g., Fick et al., 2021; 591 McNellis et al., 2023; Nauman et al., 2017; Simler-Williamson and Germino, 2022). Future 592 research could explore interactions among multiple wildfires and treatments, particularly in areas 593 with sufficient sampling, to advance our ability to link fine-scale vegetation treatment responses 594 to wind erosion and SDS. Incorporating mixed-model frameworks would also allow for a deeper 595 investigation of covariates like soil texture, improving our ability to identify the mechanisms 596 driving treatment outcomes. By building upon the connections between standardized 597 observational monitoring data, wind erosion and SDS estimates, and statistical analyses, new 598 studies could further integrate spatial and temporal dynamics, broadening the applicability of 599 findings to restoration and post-disturbance rehabilitation and their effect on wind erosion and 600 SDS mitigation.
