# A mismatch in wildfire impact reduction and ecological restoration in western U.S. fuel treatments

## Abstract

A century of fire suppression has made historically fire-adapted forests across the western U.S. denser and dominated by fire-intolerant trees, leading to severe fires. Fuel treatments aim to reduce wildfire impact and restore fire-adapted forests. Ecological theory suggests these objectives need not align: protecting the existing, fire-maladapted stand may prevent fire-tolerant seedling recruitment. Using Forest Inventory and Analysis data on 81,621 trees and 41,328 seedlings across 11 western U.S. states, we show that fuel treatments increase tree survival, but benefits are largest for fire-intolerant trees, fade within nine years, and come with no seedling composition shift towards fire-tolerant species. Concluding, fuel reduction reduces wildfire impacts but does not restore fire-adapted forests. Without changes in treatment design, long-term resilience will require repeated intervention.

# **Introduction** 

Wildfires have drastically increased in past decades across the western United States, threatening livelihoods, biodiversity, critical water resources and the long-term persistence of forests on the landscape ( _1_ – _3_ ). Climate change further intensifies these challenges, driving more severe wildfires and prolonged droughts that risk the conversion from forest to shrubor grassland after fire ( _3_ – _6_ ). The increased risk of high-severity, stand-replacing fires across much of the western U.S. is driven by decades of fire suppression and logging practices which have made historically frequent-fire forests more dense, homogeneous and dominated by fire-intolerant species with high amounts of accumulated fuel ( _7_ – _9_ ). In response, federal and state agencies invest billions of dollars annually in forest management strategies such as mechanical fuels reduction treatments and prescribed burns to decrease fuel loads and thereby lower wildfire severity ( _10_ , _11_ ). Recent studies have shown that treatments effectively reduce fire severity and associated damages ( _12_ – _14_ ). However, treatments also have a second stated goal: restoring fire-adapted forest composition and structure, so that forests can withstand recurrent wildfire in the long-term without repeated intervention ( _10_ , _15_ – _17_ ). These two objectives of treatments — fire hazard reduction and forest restoration — are usually viewed as complementary, because reducing fire severity in the short term is assumed to promote resilience in the long term ( _18_ ). 

However, ecological theory suggests that the two objectives of fuel treatments need not align. Resilience, which is the capacity to maintain state and function in the face of disturbances, can be decomposed into two components: resistance (the ability to withstand disturbance) and recovery (the capacity to return to pre-disturbance levels) ( _19_ , _20_ ). fire forests, such as ponderosa pine or Sierra mixed conifer forests, recurrent low- and moderate-severity fires maintained a feedback in which each fire reduced surface fuels, preferentially killed fire-intolerant trees and opened canopy gaps in which seeds of fire-tolerant trees, which are often shade-intolerant, could germinate ( _21_ , _22_ ). Resistance to fire and recovery towards a fire-adapted composition thus reinforced each other, maintaining resilience in the long term. In infrequent stand-replacing fire forest types that are naturally dense and dominated by fire-intolerant species such as lodgepole pine, fir or aspen, resistance is low, but intervals between fires were historically long enough to allow recovery ( _23_ , _24_ ). 

Fuel treatments effectively copy the fuel-reducing role of fire ( _11_ ), but we hypothesize that in historically frequent-fire forest types they may fail to restore a fire-adapted forest composition. That is because treatments aim to preserve the existing stand, which in fire-suppressed forests is dominated by fire-intolerant trees, leading to seedling recruitment likely mirroring the preserved overstory rather than shifting towards fire-tolerant species. As fuels rebuild ( _25_ ), the forest then quickly returns to the pre-disturbance conditions that made it vulnerable in the first place (Fig. 1a). This should be especially true for mechanical treatments that lack the element of primarily killing fire-intolerant trees unless directly targeting them for removal. However, they are the dominant form of fuel treatment across much of the western U.S. ( _26_ , _27_ ). Local studies are consistent with this proposed mechanism ( _28_ – _30_ ), but whether this is a general feature of contemporary fuel treatments across the western U.S. is unknown. 

Here, we test whether pre-fire mechanical fuel treatments increase long-term forest resilience not only by making the existing stand more resistant, but also by shifting post-fire regeneration toward fire-tolerant species. Our analysis is based on the Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) tree and seedling inventory across 11 western U.S. states (see Fig. 1b). The tree data comprise 81,621 trees on 3,629 burned plots, and the seedling data 41,328 seedlings on 1,761 burned plots, covering fires between 1990 and 2023. Because treatments are applied in both frequent- and infrequent-fire forests, the data allow us to contrast forests where lower severity and a shift toward fire-tolerant seedlings are goals with forests where neither is expected. Treatments in the FIA data are mechanical, reflecting their dominance across much of the U.S. We use a causal inference approach to separate the total effect of treatment, including its effect through reduced severity, from its effect at a fixed severity. This lets us ask whether treatments shift seedlings toward fire-tolerant species under the surface fire conditions they are designed to promote (see Fig. 1c). Because treatments are not placed at random, we compare treated and untreated burned plots after adjusting for confounders that influence both where treatments are placed and how fires behave (topography, climate, and forest structure). We further assess the longevity of treatment effects in terms of tree survival and seedling composition shift and test regional-scale differences in treatment effectiveness across climate gradients.

# **Results** 

# **Treatments increase tree survival directly after treatments, but benefits decay rapidly** 

Fuel treatments substantially increase forest resistance by enhancing tree survival and decreasing crown fire probability (see Fig. 2a). Treatments nearly triple the odds of tree survival (total effect OR: 2.78, 95% CrI: 1.42-5.45) and treated plots also have 53% lower odds of burning at crown severity (OR: 0.47, 95% CrI: 0.33-0.65). Holding fire severity fixed, the effect is smaller (controlled direct effect OR: 1.82, 95% CrI: 0.91–3.61 at surface fire; OR: 2.45, 95% CrI: 0.91–6.64 at crown fire; Fig. S3a). This shows that part of the benefit comes from lower fire severity, and part may come from other mechanisms, such as decreased competition for resources. Treatment-related benefits in terms of odds of survival are largest for species with low fire tolerance (pooled OR 2.64, 1.69 and 1.34 for low, moderate and high fire tolerance, Fig. 2b). 

An assessment of treatment longevity shows that the survival benefit is short-lived (Fig. 2c). The benefit is large in years directly following treatment (OR = 2.74) but declines quickly, reaching OR = 1 (no benefit) between 8 to 9 years after treatment (see Fig. 2c). Beyond that point, the estimate falls below 1, indicating a potentially negative effect of treatments on survival, although the credible intervals include 1. How long the benefit lasts differs among forest types, from 5.7 years in Douglas-fir and pinyon-juniper to 13 years in ponderosa pine, with Sierra mixed conifer forests (7.4 years), aspen (7.5 years), lodgepole pine forests (9.4 years) and fir (9.5 years) in between, while oak-hardwood forests showed no detectable benefit at any time since treatment (Supplementary Fig. S1).  Within a forest type, treatments help survival most on the driest plots (OR ≈ 6.1 on the driest, versus ≈ 0.8 on the wettest; OR 1.91 per global water-deficit SD, 95% CrI 1.03–3.55; Supplementary Fig. S2). 

The total effect of treatments on tree survival is robust across estimators (Supplementary Fig. S3). To explain away the point estimate, an unmeasured confounder would need a risk-ratio association of about 2.7 with both treatment and survival, stronger than that of any measured confounder (Supplementary Table S1), and a negative-control outcome test on time since fire shows no detectable systematic difference between treated and untreated plots beyond what our models account for.

# **Fire severity, but not fuel treatments, shifts seedling regeneration** 

Fuel treatments do not detectably change the share of seedlings in any fire-tolerance class. The total effect on the highly fire-tolerant fraction is +1.1 pp (-2.1 to +4.6), on the moderately tolerant fraction -2.5 pp (-7.4 to +2.2) and on the low fire-tolerance fraction +1.3 pp (-2.2 to +5.2) (Fig. 3a). All three posterior distributions are centred close to 0. Comparing plots that burned at the same severity gives the same answer. Under surface fire (the fire behaviour treatments aim to promote), treatment shifts no tolerance class by more than 1.2 pp. Under crown fire, the controlled direct effect is larger, with a decrease in the moderately tolerant fraction (−11.9 pp, 95% CrI: −21.8 to −0.4) that is redistributed to both the highly tolerant (+6.9 pp, −1.7 to +16.8) and the low tolerant fraction (+4.6 pp, −3.9 to +13.1). Apart from the moderate fraction under crown fire, all compositional shifts span 0 within credible intervals (see Fig. 3b). Our findings show that across the western United States fuel treatments increase forest resistance to wildfire but do not restore fire-adapted regeneration. Treatments consistently increase tree survival and reduce crown-fire probability, but the survival benefit is largest for

fire-intolerant trees and fades within nine years, well inside typical re-entry intervals ( _12_ , _25_ ). Treated plots, in other words, quickly rebound to their fire-maladapted state. Furthermore, we observe no shift towards fire-tolerant regeneration under the surface-fire conditions that treatments are designed to promote. Reducing fuels buys time, but by itself does not place forests on a trajectory towards naturally self-sustaining, fire-adapted communities. This pattern was previously documented only locally ( _28_ – _30_ ) but we show here that it holds across much of the western United States. Hence, our results suggest that protecting forests from severe wildfire in the short-term and restoring fire-adapted forests are related but distinct management objectives. Current fuel treatments effectively achieve the first, but by themselves do not appear sufficient to achieve the second. 

This should not be interpreted as evidence that fuel treatments are counterproductive. Their primary objective is to reduce tree mortality during wildfire, and achieving that objective provides substantial benefits, particularly where the alternative is stand-replacing fire and potential conversion to non-forest ( _3_ , _5_ ). However, our results suggest a trade-off: reducing wildfire impact by preserving the stand works against the compositional turnover needed to re-establish a fully fire-adapted forest. 

This interpretation is supported by our result that treatments increase tree survival most for species with low fire-tolerance. Because those fire-intolerant species would otherwise experience disproportionately high mortality during surface fires, treated stands can retain a larger fraction of their fire-maladapted pre-fire overstory. We interpret this as the mechanism behind the missing shift towards fire-tolerant species that a self-sustaining, fire-adapted forest would require: by preserving the stand, treatments also preserve its composition. The underlying problem, we argue, is that treatments restore a disturbance to an altered community, rather than restoring a community that is naturally in equilibrium with its own disturbance regime. Historically, many western U.S. forests experienced repeated low to medium intensity fires that continually shaped stand composition over decades by selecting for fire-tolerant species and leading to structural heterogeneity and low density stands ( _8_ , _9_ ). Treatments, by contrast, reduce fuel and stem density only temporarily, and appear neither to shift overstory composition enough nor to create the gaps that seedlings of fire-tolerant species require for germination ( _29_ ). By making the altered forest more resistant, treatments protect precisely the composition that restoration aims to change. 

This argument is further supported by the crown-fire observations in untreated stands. Untreated stands that subsequently burned as crown fires show substantially stronger shifts towards fire-tolerant regeneration, possibly because surviving fire-tolerant seed sources coincided with the high-light conditions created by canopy removal. If this interpretation is correct, treatments that sufficiently open the canopy—for example through intensive thinning combined with prescribed burning—may achieve similar regeneration without stand-replacing fire. Another possibility is that the observed composition reflects a mixture of surviving advanced regeneration and new post-fire recruits. If surface fire retains more of the pre-fire seedling cohort than crown fire, its regeneration layer may more strongly preserve the shade-tolerant, fire-intolerant composition associated with fire-excluded stands. 

Treatment effectiveness varies substantially across climatic conditions, but the patterns differ between the resistance and regeneration component. Within forest types, tree survival benefits increase towards drier plots, suggesting that treatments are particularly effective where drought stress amplifies wildfire impacts. In contrast, shifts in seedling composition towards fire-tolerant species, although never distinguishable from zero, tend to be stronger on wetter plots. These contrasting patterns suggest that maximizing short-term resistance and promoting forest restoration may require different spatial strategies. Locations where

Several limitations of the framework should be kept in mind. First, FIA data do not distinguish mechanical treatments (e.g. thinning) from prescribed burns, which work differently and have been shown to be most effective in increasing tree survival when used together and it also does not record treatment intensity ( _12_ ). Second, FIA treatment records encompass all forms of tree removal, including commercial harvests, so we cannot identify harvests that removed large, fire-tolerant trees. Such harvests would remove fire-tolerant seed sources, and thus would impede fire-adapted regeneration. This would bias our estimates towards no effect rather than inflate them. Third, because our seedling analysis is restricted to plots with regeneration, it characterizes seedling composition rather than recruitment density. Treatments may increase fire-tolerant seedling abundance without strongly altering relative composition. Finally, because fires with minimal impacts are not recorded by FIA, our analysis excludes the least damaging fire events. Future work should further investigate how treatment intensity, spatial arrangement and different combinations of treatments affect treatment outcomes at scale. 

# **Conclusion** 

Our findings suggest a tension between maximizing short-term resistance and promoting long-term resilience in western U.S. forests. By protecting existing stands that are altered by a century of fire exclusion and management for timber production, treatments also preserve overstory communities that remain dominated by fire-intolerant species, limiting the compositional turnover through which recurrent fire historically maintained fire-adapted forests. 

Achieving both objectives of treatments - fire hazard reduction and restoration of fire-adapted forests - may therefore require treatment strategies designed not only to reduce fuels but also to promote compositional change. This could include approaches that selectively reduce fire-intolerant overstory, create gaps in forests that allow for germination of shade-intolerant fire-adapted species, or combine repeated treatments with prescribed fire. We however posit that any spatial targeting faces a further complication: treatments do the most for tree survival on the driest sites, but that is not where they do the most for fire-tolerant regeneration. 

As wildfires become increasingly severe under climate change, fuel treatments will remain a critical tool in reducing fire hazard in the short-term. The ultimate goal, however, is not only forests that survive a single fire, but forests capable of sustaining themselves and being resilient under recurrent fire without repeated treatments. To achieve this goal, our study suggests that current treatment protocols need to change.

# **Materials and Methods** 

# **Study region and data** 

We used data from the U.S. Forest Service Forest Inventory and Analysis (FIA) database ( _31_ ) (last access: February 5th, 2026) for 11 states in the western United States (Arizona, California, Colorado, Idaho, Montana, Nevada, New Mexico, Oregon, Utah, Washington, Wyoming). These states include the majority of dry fire-prone forest area in the western United States. Plots span forest types whose historical fire regimes range from frequent lowand mixed-severity (ponderosa pine, Sierra mixed conifer, Douglas-fir, oak-hardwood, pinyon-juniper) to infrequent stand-replacing fires (lodgepole pine, fir, aspen). To accommodate for differences among forest types, forest type enters every model as a random intercept. Forest-type-specific treatment contrasts are further estimated in separate models (see below). 

# **Data extraction and filtering** 

We extracted plot information from the PLOT table and information about treatments and fire history from the COND table. Tree and seedling composition were extracted from the TREE and SEEDLING tables, respectively. Only sampled plots containing at least one accessible forest land condition on the plot (PLOT_STATUS_CD = 1 and COND_STATUS_CD = 1) were considered. As plots could contain several conditions, plot-level summaries were calculated by weighting slope, southwestness and site productivity class with condition area and taking forest type and time since fire from the plot’s largest condition and classifying a plot as treated or as crown fire if any of its conditions was classified as such. These plot-level summaries were used for the propensity score, matching and inverse probability weighting, the fire-severity model and all seedling composition models in which seedling counts were summed over all conditions so that one observation is one plot. In the tree survival models, each tree carried the covariates, treatment and fire classification of the condition it was recorded in. 

Only inventories measured in 2000 or later were included in the analysis, as this is the year when the FIA database was standardized and inventories hence comparable with respect to sampling procedure and timing of re-inventories. Only plots in which a fire occurred were selected based on the primary disturbance code (DSTRBCD1 = 30, 31 or 32). This was done as FIA records the disturbance judged most important for a condition as the primary disturbance code, so a fire appearing only in the second or third code was not the dominant disturbance at that plot, and this study focused solely on fire effects. Plots whose forest-type code fell outside any of these groups, and plots with a missing code, were excluded, as were hemlock–cedar plots, as they were not the focus of this study. Nonstocked stands (FORTYPCD 999) were retained. Forest type was taken from the post-fire inventory. Plots that were classified as nonstocked after a fire but were assigned a forest type other than “nonstocked” before the fire were assigned the forest type given before the fire, as it was assumed these plots fully burned and could hence not be assigned a forest type after the fire. Plots that were classified as nonstocked after a fire and had no forest type assigned before the fire either but had a FLDTYPCD indicating a forest type that was given by the field crew doing the inventory were assigned that FLDTYPCD. Plots missing key site variables (slope, elevation, site productivity class) or coordinates were excluded, as were plots for which no WorldClim or TerraClimate climate values (see below) could be extracted. In both analyses, plots were finally restricted to the region of propensity-score overlap (see section “Propensity score and positivity”), which removed plots with estimated propensity below 0.01 or above the 99th percentile of the propensity distribution. 

# **Treatment definition** 

Burned plots were considered treated when a treatment record was noted in the primary treatment field (TRTCD1) to occur in or before the year of the fire in the same plot. The analysis record for each plot is always the first post-fire inventory. Treatment information was carried forward to it from earlier inventories. FIA plots are measured on a ten-year cycle, so treatments applied before a fire are sometimes recorded in an earlier inventory than the fire itself. We therefore traced each plot back through its previous-plot records across up to two earlier inventory cycles and used the most recent treatment recorded in the primary treatment field (TRTCD1) of those records. A treatment was only counted when it occurred within 15 years before the fire and for treated plots we recorded the number of years between treatment and fire for use in the temporal analysis (see section “Tree survival model”). Only plots with treatment codes 10 (i.e. cutting, defined as “ _the removal of one or more trees from a stand_ ” in the FIA database manual) and 20 (i.e. site preparation, defined as “ _clearing, slash burning, chopping, disking, bedding, or other practices clearly intended to prepare a site for either natural or artificial regeneration”_ in the FIA database manual) were used as treated plots. Plots with post-fire planting (treatment code 30) in the current or two previous inventories were excluded because we wanted to investigate only the effect of the natural ecosystem response to wildfire disturbance. The FIA database categorizes a plot as treated if the treated area exceeds 1 acre in size.

# **Seedling dataset filtering** 

Seedling records were retained when the plot was measured between 2 and 10 years after the fire. A lower threshold of two years was chosen so that post-fire establishment is detectable, and a maximum threshold of ten years was chosen so that the observed cohort still reflects initial post-fire regeneration. Seedling counts (TREECOUNT) were aggregated to the plot level within three fire-tolerance classes (low, moderate, high), so that one observation is one plot. Species were retained when present on at least 10 plots with at least 30 seedlings in total, and only species for which a fire-tolerance classification could be assigned were used. Plots on which no seedlings of any retained species were recorded were excluded, so the compositional analysis describes which species regenerated on plots where regeneration occurred, and not whether regeneration occurred at all. This excluded 57 plots. The final number of plots and seedlings for treated and untreated conditions, as well as surface and crown fire categories can be found in Supplementary Table S4. 

# **Species fire tolerance classification** 

Species were classified into low, moderate or high fire-tolerance based on species descriptions in the Fire Effects Information System (FEIS) ( _32_ ). All classifications can be found in Supplementary Table S5. 

# **Climate data** 

Mean annual temperature (MAT) and mean annual precipitation (MAP) were obtained for each plot from the WorldClim version 2.1 bioclimatic dataset ( _33_ ) at a resolution of 2.5 arc-minutes (approximately 4.5 km) and climatic water deficit (CWD) was obtained from TerraClimate ( _34_ ) at a comparable resolution (1/24 degrees corresponding to about 4 km). CWD was calculated as the annual sum (mm/yr). This resolution was assumed to be the right scale matching the coordinate fuzziness of the FIA database (approximately 1 mile/1.6 km). MAT, MAP and CWD were used in the propensity-score and matching models to adjust for climatic differences between treated and untreated plots. They were additionally included as covariates after standardization (z-score) in the tree survival and seedling composition shift models and the interaction of CWD with treatment was used in an additional model to test whether treatment efficacy depends on climate. 

# **Causal inference framework** 

Fuel treatments are not assigned randomly but are more likely to be deposited in specific forest locations that are for example more accessible or more likely to be subject to high severity fires due to climate, topography or forest structure ( _35_ ). Hence, any estimate of a causal effect of treatments on tree survival and seedling composition shift needs to address

The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license. 

the influence of these confounders. Furthermore, treatments may act through a direct pathway in increasing tree survival or shifting seedling composition for example through a reduction in competition for resources among trees and seedlings, but they may also act over an indirect pathway by reducing fire-severity which then increases tree survival or shifts seedling composition. Those pathways need to be disentangled to identify treatment benefits on tree survival and seedling composition shift at the surface fire conditions they are intended to promote. 

We start from a directed acyclical graph (see Fig. 1c) that encodes the proposed causal pathway describing how treatments increase survival and shift seedling composition ( _36_ , _37_ ). Next to the direct pathway and the indirect pathway over lowering fire severity we have three blocks of confounders that represent pre-treatment plot-level variables all of which influence the likelihood that treatments are placed in a plot but that also themselves influence crown fire probability and the outcome (tree survival, seedling composition shift) ( _35_ , _38_ ). These three blocks are topography (represented by elevation, slope and southwestness), climate (MAT, MAP, CWD) and stand structure/site capability (forest-type group, site productivity class). 

For correct causal identification of treatment effects, the following assumptions need to be met, and for each one we state how we addressed them: 

1. No unmeasured confounding: Treated and untreated plots need to be comparable given the three confounder blocks. We assessed sensitivity to unmeasured confounding with E-values and negative-control outcomes ( _39_ , _40_ ). 

2. Positivity: Every plot must have a non-zero probability of being treated or untreated. We enforce this by calculating a propensity score, trimming the region without common support and removing any forest type or state observed only in either the treated or untreated plots ( _41_ ). 

3. Consistency: The categories “treated” and “untreated” must be consistently defined ( _42_ ). This is achieved by using the categorization of the FIA database as described above (see “Treatment definition”). 

4. No interference: Treatments are assigned at plot level and a treatment in one plot should not affect causal effects (i.e. tree survival and seedling composition shift) in another plot ( _43_ ). As FIA plots are located roughly 5 km apart from each other ( _44_ ), treatment of one plot is unlikely to change fire behaviour or regeneration in another. We modelled the remaining dependence with plot-level random effects and a spatial field in the Bayesian models.

# **Propensity score and positivity** 

To ensure positivity (see point 2 in section “Causal inference framework”), a plot-level logistic propensity score was estimated from the pre-treatment covariates elevation, slope, southwestness, site productivity class, the climate variables, forest type and state. The model is fitted at the plot level, because treatments are applied to whole plots. First, any forest types or states that either had only treated or untreated plots were removed to ensure every forest type and state has both treated and untreated plots. Second, the propensity score was fitted and plots with a score below 0.01 or above the 99th percentile of all fitted scores were trimmed. Third, the check for forest types and states that only had treated or untreated plots was applied again because trimming can leave a forest type or state with either. Fourth, the propensity score was refitted on this final sample and the fitted scores were bounded to the range [0.01, 0.99]. This refitted score was used for weighting, matching and AIPW (see section “Robustness”). After this procedure, 386 of 4,015 plots in the tree survival analysis and 281 of 2,042 plots for the seedling composition shift analysis were excluded. The retained region is the common support on which every estimate rests (see Supplementary Figures S7a and S8a for tree and seedling data, respectively). 

# **Tree survival model** 

Survival was modelled as a binary response following a binomial distribution with a logit link. Our primary model (Model A) reports the total effect of treatments on tree survival. The linear predictor included the treatment effect as well as an explicit intercept term, a quadratic effect of diameter at breast height (DBH), time since fire and covariates for the site (slope, southwestness, elevation, site productivity class, MAT, MAP and CWD). A quadratic effect for DBH was included because tree size was assumed to non-linearly influence tree survival, as increasingly larger trees are not expected to be more resistant to fire. Southwestness was calculated from the aspect of the plot as: 

# Southwestness = cos((aspect-225)* 𝜋 /180) 

Plots with an aspect value of 0 or a slope of 0 were treated as missing for this calculation and subsequently the southwestness was set to 0 for those plots. The tree diameter was taken directly from the current measurement when available (column DIA) but filled with the diameter from a previous measurement (column PREVDIA) when unavailable. All continuous covariates were standardized (z-scored) before analysis. Plot-level covariates (elevation, slope, site productivity class, southwestness, time since fire, MAT, MAP, CWD) were standardized with the mean and standard deviation across plots, tree diameter was standardized across trees. The hierarchical structure is considered through Gaussian random intercepts for the plot, state, forest type and species. Additionally, species-specific random slopes for treatment were used because different species were expected to differently respond to treatments, especially with respect to different fire-tolerance of species. Species-specific random slopes for DBH were included because the size-mortality relationship was also assumed to differ depending on species, as large trees of fire-tolerant species are expected to withstand fire more successfully than large trees of fire-intolerant species. We then fitted another model (Model B) that adds crown fire and its interaction with treatment and hence estimates the controlled direct effect at surface severity (the treatment coefficient) and under crown severity (treatment plus interaction coefficient). 

We further investigated effects of climate, treatment longevity and forest type on treatment benefits with a series of additional models. These models are extensions of Model B, so the treatment effects they report are controlled direct effects at surface fire. First, we wanted to test whether aridity changes the treatment effect. To separate the effect of aridity from differences in forest types, we performed a Mundlak decomposition. For this, we split CWD into each forest type’s mean (between-forest-type component) and each plot’s deviation from that mean (within-forest-type component), both scaled by the same standard deviation across plots of the analysed forest types and both interacted with treatments. Only the within-forest-type component, which compares drier with wetter plots within a forest type, is interpreted because the between-forest-type component cannot be separated from other differences among forest types and only serves as an adjustment. Each forest type had its own random deviation in the treatment effect. We additionally investigated treatment longevity in a separate model by allowing the treatment effect to vary with the time between treatment and fire using a second-order random-walk smoother over the number of years between treatment and fire (0-15 years, with one node per year). Because this term is only defined for treated trees, it is identified from the treated subset. Forest-type-specific decay was estimated by letting the treatment effect change linearly with treatment age, with independent forest-type random effects on both the treatment effect and its slope. The Mundlak decomposition and the forest-type-specific decay model were fitted only to forest types with at least 10 treated plots, because per-type estimates resting on fewer treated plots are unreliable. Continuous confounders were standardized (z-scored) within this subsample. Residual spatial dependence was modelled with a Gaussian random field over plot locations on the same SPDE mesh used for the survival models. Species terms were not included, as severity is a plot-level property. Fixed effects and the intercept received N(0, 2.5²) priors.

# **Robustness** 

We used several approaches to test the robustness of our results with respect to uneven balance of selection of treated plots as well as the used methodology and unmeasured confounding. 

All these methods start with the propensity score as described in the section “Propensity score and positivity” above. The tree-level survival analysis and the seedling composition analysis each use their own score, fitted to the same site and climate covariates. The balance was summarised as the absolute standardized mean differences against a threshold of 0.1 using the raw pooled standard deviation as the common fixed denominator per covariate. Treated plots are generally lower in elevation, more productive and drier than untreated plots (see Supplementary Figures S7b and S8b). 

With the propensity score we re-estimated the total effect in three ways. First, with inverse probability weighting (IPW) where each plot gets a stabilized weight from the inverse of its propensity score so that the treated and untreated groups become comparable. Second, with propensity score matching, pairing each treated plot with the untreated plot with the closest propensity score (1:1 nearest neighbour without replacement, caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the propensity score). For both, Model A was refitted with an otherwise unchanged specification, once on the matched sample and once on the full sample with the IPW weights entering the likelihood, so that only the sample or its weighting differs from the main estimate. Third, we used augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW), a doubly robust approach that combines the propensity score with separate outcome models for treated and untreated plots and which gives a valid estimate as long as at least one of the two models is correct. For tree survival, the outcome models were logistic regressions of tree survival. For seedling composition, they were binomial regressions of each tolerance class's share of the plot's seedlings, and the three class estimates were rescaled to sum to one. Neither included the random effects or the spatial field. In both analyses the outcome models used the full propensity-score covariate set plus time since fire. Because treatment is a

# **Sensitivity** 

We asked how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to overturn our result. For this we calculated the E-value for the total effect of treatments on tree survival (Model A for tree survival model) which can be interpreted as the minimum strength of association, on the risk-ratio scale, that an unmeasured variable would need to have with both treatment assignment and tree survival, conditional on the measured covariates, to explain the effect away ( _39_ ) and compared it with the strength of the confounders we had actually measured. Because survival is not a rare outcome, odds ratios were converted to approximate risk ratios via the square-root transformation ( _45_ ) before computing E-values. The E-value was 2.72 for the point estimate and 1.67 for the lower credible limit (Table S1). We complemented this sensitivity analysis with a falsification test that would be expected to reveal residual confounding if there was still a systematic difference between treated and untreated plots beyond what our models account for. We tested for a treatment effect on time since fire (the interval between the fire year and the FIA revisit) as this cannot be caused by a treatment applied before the fire. The test regressed time since fire on treatment using weighted least squares with the same stabilized, trimmed inverse-probability weights used above, adjusting for the remaining topographic and site variables (elevation, slope, southwestness, site productivity class, MAT, MAP, CWD) and for forest-type and state. The regression was fitted at the plot level rather than on individual trees, because the tested variables are plot attributes. After weighting, treatment did not predict time since fire in the survival dataset (β = 0.050 SD, p = 0.48) or in the seedling dataset (β = 0.088 SD, p = 0.39), indicating no detectable systematic difference between treated and untreated plots when looking at time since fire.
