## Abstract

### Background

Globally, there are growing demands for evidence-based policies to manage wildfires. Currently, fire management in Northern Ghana relies on policies and projects developed without a comprehensive understanding of fire trends and drivers. This study analyzed spatiotemporal trends in burned areas (500 m MODIS and 30 m Landsat products), active fires, and fire seasonality using linear regression analysis to investigate shifts in fire regimes between 2000 and 2022. Biophysical and social drivers of the observed trends were also examined.

### Results

When averaged across Northern Ghana, MODIS burned area data (large fires) revealed a significant decreasing trend, while Landsat burned area (sensitive to large and small fires) and active fires showed no significant trend. When disaggregated by region, MODIS burned area showed significant decreasing trends for the Savannah Region, Upper East and Upper West Regions. Conversely, Landsat burned area showed no trend in all regions of Northern Ghana. Active fires increased significantly in the Northern Region. Active fire data also revealed a shift in fire seasonality in Northern Ghana towards more mid-dry season fires. Institutional perspectives attributed the decline in large fires (as identified by MODIS) to the success of interventions aimed at reducing uncontrolled burning (locally referred to as bushfires), while persistent burning (as indicated by Landsat burned area and MODIS active fires) was linked to livelihoods dependent on burning. LULC analysis showed increases in agricultural and built-up areas, consistent with cropland expansion associated with fire-dependent land clearing. The study also identified important limitations associated with the existing fire products that obscured accurate fire detection.

### Conclusion

Our results highlight the importance of scale, resolution, and sensor characteristics in interpreting fire regimes. Aggregating data across the whole of Northern Ghana obscured contrasting trends in individual regions. The different trends observed between MODIS and Landsat burned areas suggest that changes in fire activities are occurring unevenly across spatiotemporal scales. Additionally, shifts towards mid-dry season burning suggest misalignment between current early burning policies and actual fire dynamics in Northern Ghana. Effective and sustainable fire management will therefore require integrating local perspectives, recognizing the ecological role of fires in savannas, and adopting monitoring approaches tailored to different fire types.

## Resumen

### Antecedentes

Globalmente, existe una demanda creciente para tener políticas de manejo del fuego basadas en evidencia. Actualmente, el manejo del fuego en el norte de Ghana se basa en políticas y proyectos desarrollados sin un entendimiento comprensivo sobre las tendencias y factores que llevan a producir los fuegos de vegetación. Este estudio analiza las tendencias espacio-temporales en áreas quemadas (con productos de MODIS 500 m y LANDSAT 30 m), fuegos activos y estacionalidad de los fuegos, usando análisis de regresión linear para investigar cambios en los regímenes de fuegos entre el año 2000 y el 2022. Los factores biofísicos y sociales que influencian las tendencias observadas también fueron analizados.

### Resultados

Cuando se promediaron para el norte de Ghana, los datos de área quemada estimados mediante MODIS (para grandes incendios) revelaron una tendencia decreciente significativa, mientras que los datos de áreas quemadas estimados por LANDSAT (sensibles tanto a grandes como pequeños incendios) y los datos de incendios activos, no mostraron una tendencia significativa. Cuando los datos se desagregaron por región, las estimaciones de área quemada mediante MODIS, mostraron una tendencia significativa decreciente para las regiones de *Savannah*, y las de *Upper East* y *Upper West.* De manera opuesta, el área quemada estimada por LANDSAT, no registró tendencia alguna para todas las regiones del norte de Ghana. Los fuegos activos se incrementaron significativamente en la región *Norte*. Los datos de fuegos activos revelaron un cambio en la estacionalidad de los incendios, con una mayor incidencia a mediados de la estación seca. Las perspectivas institucionales atribuyen la declinación de los grandes incendios (identificados por MODIS) al éxito de las intervenciones enfocadas a reducir las quemas incontroladas (localmente llamadas fuegos de matorral), mientras que las quemas persistentes, indicadas por LANDSAT como áreas quemadas y por MODIS como fuegos activos, estuvieron ligadas a medios de vida dependientes de las quemas. Los análisis de LULC mostraron incrementos en áreas para la agricultura y las construcciones, asociadas con el aclareo de las mismas mediante el uso del fuego. El estudio también identificó limitaciones importantes en los productos de datos sobre incendios existentes que obscurecieron su detección de forma más precisa.

### Conclusiones

Nuestros resultados subrayan la importancia de la escala, de la resolución y de las características de los sensores para interpretar los regímenes de fuegos. Los datos agregados para el norte de Ghana obscurecen las tendencias contrastantes en regiones individuales. Las diferentes tendencias observadas entre MODIS y LANDSAT, en cuanto a áreas quemadas, sugieren que los cambios en las actividades relacionadas con los fuegos ocurren de manera desigual a través de escalas espaciales. Asimismo, el desplazamiento hacia quemas a mediados de la estación seca sugiere un desacoplamiento entre las políticas de quemas tempranas y la dinámica actual de los fuegos en el norte de Ghana. El manejo sostenible y efectivo del fuego requiere, por lo tanto, integrar las perspectivas locales, reconociendo el rol ecológico del fuego en las sabanas, y adaptando acciones de monitoreo adaptadas a los diferentes tipos de fuegos.

Northern Ghana’s savanna landscape provides an important context for this study, as 90% of the total land cover is subject to annual bush burning during the dry season (MLFM [2006](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR56 "MLFM. 2006. “National Wildfire Management Policy.” Accra, Ghana.")), prompting the implementation of various policies and interventions to manage fires in these areas. This study uses SRS data to understand shifting fire regimes in Northern Ghana savanna landscapes, quantifying the spatiotemporal trends in burned area, active fires, and fire seasonality. These spatiotemporal trends in fire regimes are analyzed alongside institutional perspectives for a more comprehensive understanding of the drivers, given that the reasoning behind these regimes is often understudied (Laris [2011](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR45 "Laris, Paul. 2011. Humanizing savanna biogeography: Linking human practices with ecological patterns in a frequently burned savanna of Southern Mali. Annals of the Association of American Geographers 101 (5): 1067–88. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/00045608.2011.560063
                  
                .")). Specifically, we asked:

1. i.

   What are the regional (sub-national) spatiotemporal trends in burned areas, active fires, and fire seasonality over the last 21 years (2000–2022) in Northern Ghana as assessed by MODIS and Landsat satellite imagery?
2. ii.

   How does precipitation (biophysical driver) influence shifting fire regimes in Northern Ghana?
3. iii.

   What potential social factors, including land use and land cover, influence these shifting fire regimes?

In the following section, we begin by outlining the study area and then detail the methods used to collect and analyze data on burned area and active fires, biophysical drivers (precipitation) and social drivers. Both the Results and Discussion sections are broadly structured around the research questions, beginning with a presentation of the observed trends in burned area and fire seasonality, followed by a focus on rainfall and social drivers. In the Discussion, we also reflect on the various limitations of the study. Finally, the Conclusion synthesizes the main findings, highlights how they advance our understanding of fire dynamics in Northern Ghana’s savanna landscape, and outlines their implications for future fire governance and research.

## Methods

### Study site

This study focused on the five regions of Northern Ghana (Fig. [1](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#Fig1)), where 90% of the landscape experiences annual vegetation burning (MLFM [2006](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR56 "MLFM. 2006. “National Wildfire Management Policy.” Accra, Ghana.")). Before 2017, Northern Ghana consisted of Northern, Upper East and Upper West Regions. In 2018, two new regions, Savannah and North-East were created by carving them out of the Northern Region. This administrative change led to the five regions in Northern Ghana. Northern Ghana is situated within the Guinea and Sudan savanna ecological zones. The region’s vegetation is characterized by fire and drought-resistant woody species including Shea (*Vitelleria paradoxa*), bushwillow (*Combretum* spp.)*,* wild syringa (*Burkea africana*), and “abogo” (*Isoberlinia doka*), with common grasses such as *Andropogon, Heteropogon,* and *Hyparrhenia* spp. (Amoako and Gambiza [2019](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR1 "Amoako, Esther Ekua, and James Gambiza. 2019. “Effects of anthropogenic fires on soil properties and the implications of fire frequency for the Guinea Savanna Ecological Zone, Ghana.” Scientific African 6. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sciaf.2019.e00201
                  
                .")). It covers a total landmass of about 97,250 km2, with thin soils overlaying an iron pan—a hard soil layer cemented by iron oxides and usually consisting of sand or sand gravel. Burkina Faso bounds the area to the North and North-west, Togo to the East, and Côte D’Ivoire to the West. The southern edge of the zone is bordered by the Oti and Bono Regions. Northern Ghana has a tropical climate with unimodal rainfall patterns influenced by two air masses: the North-east trade winds and the South-westerly. However, because of its proximity to the Sahel, Northern Ghana experiences a dry climate caused by the north-easterly winds (harmattan) between November and April, facilitating vegetative burning.

### Burned area and active fire data collection

We employed the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer’s (MODIS) burned area dataset (MCD64A1, 500 m spatial resolution) to analyze spatiotemporal burned area trends. Additionally, we used MODIS Active fire dataset (MOD14A1 V6, 1 km spatial resolution) to investigate temporal trends in active fires and fire seasonality (Giglio et al. [2021](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR30 "Giglio, L., W. Schroeder, J. Hall, and C. Justice. 2021. MODIS collection 6 and collection 6.1 active fire product user’s guide. USA: Washington D.C.")) between 2001 and 2022. It is worth noting that MODIS active fire products are more sensitive to smaller fires (≥ 10 ha) than MODIS-burned area products (≥ 169 ha) under pristine conditions (Fusco et al. [2019](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR28 "Fusco, Emily J.., John T.. Finn, John T.. Abatzoglou, Jennifer K.. Balch, Sepideh Dadashi, and Bethany A.. Bradley. 2019. Detection rates and biases of fire observations from MODIS and agency reports in the conterminous United States. Remote Sensing of Environment 220: 30–40. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rse.2018.10.028
                  
                .")). We also used the Global Annual Burned Area Mapping (GABAM) dataset, derived from 30 m Landsat 8 Surface Reflectance imagery as described by Long et al. ([2019](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR51 "Long, Tengfei, Zhaoming Zhang, Guojin He, Weili Jiao, Chao Tang, Bingfang Wu, Xiaomei Zhang, Guizhou Wang, and Ranyu Yin. 2019. 30m resolution global annual burned area mapping based on Landsat images and Google Earth engine. Remote Sensing 11 (5): 1–30. 
                  https://doi.org/10.3390/rs11050489
                  
                .")). Although Landsat captures both large and small fires, the GABAM Landsat burned pixels are aggregated into annual maps, which do not distinguish between small and large fire events or classify fires by size. Despite this limitation, Landsat captures burned areas at finer spatial resolution and may therefore include smaller burned patches that are missed by MODIS. However, it cannot independently separate small from large fire events without additional patch-based or event-based analysis. The trend analysis was limited to 22 years (2000–2022) based on the temporal availability of consistent burned area products (MODIS and GABAM). While Landsat imagery predates this period, the restriction ensures consistency and methodological comparability associated with the use of specific processed datasets.

Although Landsat has a fine spatial resolution (30 m), it is constrained by low temporal resolution (with observations only once every 16 days) and for areas like Northern Ghana, where vegetation recovery is very quick after a fire, long temporal gaps usually result in higher omission errors (Bowman et al. [2003](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR22 "D. M. J. S., Bowman Yue, Zhang Angie, Walsh R. J., Williams (2003) Experimental comparison of four remote sensing techniques to map tropical savanna fire-scars using Landsat-TM imagery. International Journal of Wildland Fire 12(4) 341-348. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1071/WF03030
                  
                .")). Additionally, Long et al. ([2019](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR51 "Long, Tengfei, Zhaoming Zhang, Guojin He, Weili Jiao, Chao Tang, Bingfang Wu, Xiaomei Zhang, Guizhou Wang, and Ranyu Yin. 2019. 30m resolution global annual burned area mapping based on Landsat images and Google Earth engine. Remote Sensing 11 (5): 1–30. 
                  https://doi.org/10.3390/rs11050489
                  
                .")) assert that one drawback of the Landsat burned area data is the uncertainty associated with agricultural lands. In these areas, harvested or plowed surfaces can be mistaken for burned areas due to similarities in spectral and temporal characteristics. While Sentinel-2 would have been ideal for this study due to its 10 m spatial resolution and high revisit frequency coverage, the short temporal coverage (data available since 2015) makes it less suitable for long-term fire regime analysis.

### Precipitation data collection

Rainfall data were used as a predictor variable to analyze the mean annual rainfall and its influence on fire seasonality and fuel load (Dwomoh and Wimberly [2017](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR24 "Dwomoh, Francis K.., and Michael C.. Wimberly. 2017. Fire regimes and their drivers in the Upper Guinean region of West Africa. Remote Sensing. 
                  https://doi.org/10.3390/rs9111117
                  
                .")). First, we collected rain gauge data from the Ghana Meteorological Agency at five rain gauge stations in Northern Ghana: Tamale (Northern Region), Yendi (Northern Region), Bole (Savannah Region), Navrongo (Upper East Region), and Wa (Upper West Region) with GPS coordinates. Due to sparsity in rain gauge data at some stations, we relied on Climate Hazards Group Infrared Precipitation with Station Data (CHIRPS) data for rainfall analysis from 2001 to 2022. CHIRPS gridded daily rainfall product (UCSB-CHG/CHIRPS/DAILY) combines satellite infrared data and in situ precipitation station data to produce accurate, detailed, and extended periods of rainfall estimates (Funk et al. [2015](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR27 "Funk, Chris, Pete Peterson, Martin Landsfeld, Diego Pedreros, James Verdin, Shraddhanand Shukla, Gregory Husak, et al. 2015. The climate hazards infrared precipitation with stations - A new environmental record for monitoring extremes. Scientific Data. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2015.66
                  
                .")).

### Data collection on social drivers

To acquire expert knowledge on the perceived trends of burned areas and active fires, as well as the drivers of these spatial and temporal trends in Northern Ghana’s savanna landscapes, we conducted semi-structured interviews with local government institutions (*n* = 6), and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) (*n* = 4) (Table [1](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#Tab1)). While community-level perspectives are valuable, the regional (subnational) spatiotemporal scale analysis of shifting fire regimes necessitated engagement primarily with institutions. The study employed a combination of different sampling approaches to select interview participants. Purposive sampling was used to select government institutions, given their formal responsibility in fire management as outlined in the 2006 Ghana Wildfire Policy. However, NGOs involved in forest and biodiversity conservation-related projects were identified through snowball sampling, while participants’ availability and willingness to participate determined the selection process via convenience sampling (Frey et al. [2000](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR26 "Frey, L., C.H. Botan, and G. Kreps. 2000. Investigating communication: an introduction to research methods. Allyn and Bacon.")). The interviews were conducted between November 1 and December 7, 2022, and took place both virtually and face-to-face. The interviews focused on institutional perspectives of traditional burning practices and perceived changes in fire regimes. Additionally, a stakeholder engagement workshop was conducted on 16–17 October 2024 in Damongo in the Savannah Region to discuss pathways for promoting equitable fire management in Northern Ghana. Participants were presented with burned-area trends derived from MODIS and Landsat data and were invited to reflect on these patterns. Through this process, stakeholders identified additional perceived social drivers that may help explain the contrasting trends observed in the satellite data.

### Data analyses

Following the burned area, active fires, rainfall, and LULC data collection, we used Google Earth Engine (GEE) to analyze all remotely sensed images. Each region’s time series was exported as a CSV file for further statistical analysis and visualization in RStudio. For the statistical analysis in RStudio, we initially performed a Shapiro–Wilk normality test to determine whether the data distributions of burned area, active fires, and rainfall required a parametric statistical analysis. We considered the Shapiro–Wilk test of normality due to its effectiveness in analyzing small datasets (*n* < 50) and being less impacted by outliers (Mendes and Pala [2003](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR53 "Mendes, Mehmet, and Akin Pala. 2003. Type I error rate and power of three normality tests. Information Technology Journal 2 (2): 135–139. 
                  https://doi.org/10.3923/itj.2003.135.139
                  
                ."), Mohd Razali and Bee Wah [2011](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR57 "Mohd Razali, Nornadiah, and Yap Bee Wah. 2011. “Power Comparisons of Shapiro-Wilk, Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Lilliefors and Anderson-Darling Tests.” Journal of Statistical Modeling and Analytics 2 (1): 13–14.")). However, since the assumption of linear regression pertains to model residuals rather than the raw data (Midway and White [2025](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR54 "Midway, Stephen, and J. Wilson. White. 2025. Testing for normality in regression models: Mistakes abound (but may not matter). Royal Society Open Science. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.241904
                  
                ."); Jones et al. [2025](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR36 "Jones, Lee, Adrian Barnett, and Dimitrios Vagenas. 2025. Common misconceptions held by health researchers when interpreting linear regression assumptions, a cross-sectional study. PLoS One. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0299617
                  
                .")), additional diagnostics were performed on the residuals of the fitted models.

Shapiro–Wilk test applied to the residuals indicated no significant deviation from normality for MODIS burned area (*W* = 0.98, *p* = 0.84), Landsat burned area (*W* = 0.96, *p* = 0.43), and MODIS active fires (*W* = 0.92, *p* = 0.08) at a 95% confidence level, although the latter was marginal. Visual inspection of the Q-Q plots confirmed these results, revealing only minor deviations in the distribution tails. As a result, the assumptions of linear regression were deemed sufficiently met, and linear regression models were used to evaluate the spatiotemporal trends in the time-series data. Monthly active fire counts were further analyzed using linear regression to evaluate shifts in fire seasonality over the study period.

In the case of rainfall, the CHIRPS data for Northern Ghana were analyzed on GEE and then exported to RStudio for normality testing and subsequent linear regression analysis. The Shapiro–Wilk test performed on the regression residuals indicated that the data were normally distributed (*W* = 0.95, *p*= 0.34). Additionally, the Q-Q plot demonstrated that most points fall close to the reference line, further supporting the conclusion that the residuals are normally distributed.

Regarding the social drivers, all interviews were transcribed, and the transcripts were then coded into themes based on the principles of grounded theory (Glaser and Strauss [1967](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR31 "Glaser, B. G., and A. L. Strauss. 1967. The discovery of grounded theory: strategies for qualitative research. New Brunswick, USA and London, UK: Aldine Transaction.")). This approach aims to develop theories that emanate from the knowledge and perceptions shared by interviewees concerning the subject matter. The NVivo 14 software aided the analysis of codes, memos, and quotations. Additionally, data from the stakeholder engagement workshop were used to explain the observed spatiotemporal trends and to complement institutional views on changing LULC.

## Results

### Spatiotemporal patterns of burned area and fire seasonality

Figure [2](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#Fig2) presents trends in burned areas, active fires, and rainfall averaged across Northern Ghana from 2001 to 2022. MODIS 500 m burned area (Fig. [2](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#Fig2)A) declined from approximately 3750 km2 in 2001 to 3305 km2 in 2022. The trend was not statistically significant (*p* = 0.076) at a 95% confidence level, with notable peaks in 2005 (4729 km2), 2011 (4279 km2) and 2017 (3859 km2). Conversely, Landsat 30 m burned area data (Fig. [2](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#Fig2)B) showed an increase from 1914 km2 in 2001 to 2250 km2 in 2021, although this trend was also not statistically significant (*p* = 0.54) at a 95% confidence level. The largest mean burned area, about 3400 km2, occurred in 2002, whereas 2004 recorded the smallest mean burned area, 200 km2.

### Precipitation trends

Rainfall in Northern Ghana between 2001 and 2022 showed considerable variability between years, with no significant trend (*p* = 0.68) (Fig. [2](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#Fig2)D). Notable peaks in mean annual rainfall occurred in 2010 (1219.4 mm), 2009 (1189.5 mm), and 2003 (1166.3 mm), while large dips occurred in 2013 (790.2 mm), 2017 (862.9 mm) and 2001 (903.4 mm). Rainfall typically begins in April and ends in October, with peak rainfall months shifting from year to year (Fig. [6](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#Fig6)B). Further analysis of monthly mean rainfall trends revealed no significant changes in rainfall across all years, except for January (*p* = 0.03) (Fig. [1](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#Fig1) in the supplementary document). Rainfall in Northern Ghana showed no significant correlation with Landsat Burned Areas (*r* = −0.04, *p* = 0.86) (Fig. [3](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#Fig3) in the supplementary document). However, a statistically significant but weak negative correlation was observed between rainfall and MODIS burned areas (*r* = −0.42, *p* = 0.05) at a 95% confidence level (Fig. [2](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#Fig2) in the supplementary document).

### Perceived social drivers influencing changing fire regimes

Initial responses from interviews revealed varying perceptions about the changing fire regimes in Northern Ghana. However, in a subsequent stakeholder engagement workshop, participants perceived a decline in large fires (locally referred to as bushfires) in the area. They attributed this decline to several interventions, including intensified public sensitization and government flagship programs such as the Sustainable Land and Water Management Project (SLWMP), Planting for Food and Jobs (PFJ), Ghana Shea Landscape Emission Reduction Project (GSLERP), and Ghana Land Restoration and Small-Scale Mining Project (GLRSSMP). Additionally, the establishment of fire posts at the various Metropolitan, Municipal and District Assemblies, as well as the provision of alternative mechanisms for land preparation using tractors and herbicides, have contributed to managing fires. These perceptions aligned with the observed MODIS burned area trends for Northern Ghana. For instance, an interview with a project manager at a conservation NGO highlighted that:

> Generally, bush burning increased in the landscape during the period and peaked during the logging and charcoal production activities. Burning is used to make the landscape more open for undertaking these activities. Also, with the increasing and expanding agricultural activities, more fires were being used to clear and prepare lands. However, this has decreased a bit within the last five years due to national and local bans on logging (2012–2019) and charcoal burning (2017–2021), respectively. Additionally, within this period, the presence of large-scale multinational programs such as the SLWMP in 2011 and the Landscape and Environmental Agility Across Nation (LEAN) in 2020 has provided financial support for fire management and restoration efforts in Northern Ghana. (Project Manager, A Rocha Northern Ghana Sector, 2023).

In terms of region-specific trends for MODIS burned areas, participants attributed the observed increasing trend in the Northern Region to lower population density, increased hunting activities, and the lack of wildlife-protected areas or community resource management areas (CREMAs). For example, the Environmental Specialist for the Savannah Agriculture Value Chain Development Project (SADEP) noted that:

> During the dry season in the Northern Region, you will find a lot of young men in about 3–5 trucks going hunting. They target a large area for burning to hunt for game. (Environmental Specialist- SADEP, 2024).

Conversely, participants attributed the lack of significant trends in smaller fires (Landsat burned area) in Northern Ghana to livelihood-related activities such as hunting, charcoal burning and using fires to clear new farmlands. These activities are particularly prevalent in the North-East, Northern, Savannah and Upper West Regions. The low levels of burned areas in the Upper East Region were attributed to low fuel availability. This scarcity is largely attributed to the region’s geology, which is predominantly rocky. Additionally, the Upper East Region has seen an increase in built-up areas and a decrease in tree cover due to charcoal production. In this region, many farmers also clear their fields with hoes (rather than using fire) so they can use the resulting crop residues as fodder for their livestock. For instance, a Lecturer at the University for Development Studies emphasized that:

> People are increasingly turning to charcoal production as an alternative livelihood, as farming has become less lucrative due to climate change. About 20 years ago, there was only one community along the route from Tamale to Accra where you could buy charcoal. Currently, numerous charcoal-selling spots exist, contributing to the prevalence of small fires. Moreover, existing studies indicate that increasing populations and built-up areas are limiting the areas available for large fires. As communities expand, they convert land for settlements, which restricts burning activities to small plots used for farming, ensuring that the fires do not spread uncontrollably. In the Upper East Region, for example, the land area is smaller and has a high population density. Therefore, there are not enough farms for burning activities, and there is less tree cover for charcoal production.(Lecturer, UDS).

Expert field observations further suggest that spatial variation in fuel availability, driven by labor dynamics, extractive activities, and longer-term land use change, plays a critical role in shaping these fire outcomes. For example, the Executive Director of Eco Restore Ltd, a firm focused on landscape restoration of woodlands and agroforestry parklands in Northern Ghana and across sub-Saharan Africa, explained that:

> In areas affected by artisanal and small-scale mining …, a substantial proportion of farmlands have been left to fallow over the last decade due to labour shortages, resulting in extensive fallow lands and increased biomass accumulation… In some areas, up to 90% of the usual farmland was not being cleared for cultivation. This leads to more biomass and potentially an increased likelihood of fires. (P. Lovett, personal communication, August 2025).

In contrast, he pointed out that in well-farmed shea parklands, the land has been so heavily cropped and sprayed with herbicides that there is little left to burn. (P. Lovett, personal communication, August 2025).

## Discussion

### Spatiotemporal trends in burned area, active fire and fire seasonality in Northern Ghana’s savanna landscape

Our results highlight the importance of scale and spatial resolution in fire regime analysis. MODIS and Landsat burned areas in Northern Ghana over the last 22 years revealed contrasting spatial and temporal trends. The MODIS dataset showed a decreasing trend in burned area, while the Landsat dataset showed no significant trend. Aggregating to the whole of Northern Ghana masked the contrasting trends occurring in different regions. The decreasing MODIS burned area trend (e.g., for Savannah, Upper East and Upper West Regions) aligns with previous research—also using MODIS—which shows both West Africa and global decreases in burned areas (Andela et al.                   https://doi.org/10.3390/rs9111117
                  
                .")). The coarse resolution (500 m) of MODIS hampers its ability to detect small fires (≤ 25 ha) in heterogeneous landscapes (Laris [2005](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR44 "Laris, Paul S.. 2005.                   https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rse.2005.09.012

This trend cannot be fully understood without conducting patch-based or event-based analyses.

However, our analysis is constrained by the 22-year availability of processed MODIS and Landsat GABAM burned area data. As such, we capture only a relatively short window in the region’s long-term trajectory of fire regimes. This makes it difficult to assess whether the observed pattern—particularly the decrease in large fires—represents sustained shifts or is part of a broader, more cyclical trend. ### The role of precipitation in influencing the spatiotemporal trends of burned area, active fires and fire seasonality in Northern Ghana

To investigate the factors influencing the observed year-to-year variability in the burned areas of Northern Ghana for both MODIS and Landsat, we conducted a correlation analysis to examine the relationship between rainfall and the burned areas (see Fig. [2](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#Fig2) in the supplementary material). The results revealed a weak but significant negative correlation between rainfall and MODIS burned area, suggesting that rainfall inhibited burning. This is consistent with the findings of N’Datchoh et al. ([2015](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR59 "N’Datchoh, E. T., A. Konaré, A. Diedhiou, A. Diawara, E. Quansah, and P. Assamoi. 2015. Effects of climate variability on savannah fire regimes in West Africa. Earth System Dynamics 6 (1): 161–174. 
                  https://doi.org/10.5194/esd-6-161-2015
                  
                .")), who revealed that a wet rainfall season leads to high humidity, suppressing fire spread. As a result, no fires were observed during the rainy season (April to October), but in the dry season (November to March). Conversely, there was no significant relationship between rainfall and the Landsat-derived GABAM burned area. Given the finer spatial resolution of Landsat, this suggests that variability in burned areas captured by Landsat may be less strongly influenced by precipitation and more closely associated with localized anthropogenic activities. However, because Landsat-derived GABAM data do not distinguish individual fire events or classify burn by size, the observed patterns cannot be attributed to specific types or fire size. For instance, participants in the stakeholder engagement workshop perceived the increasing burned areas observed in Landsat in Northern Ghana to be associated with livelihood activities such as hunting, farm preparations and charcoal production activities. Hunting and charcoal production activities were described as providing alternate livelihoods for communities and helping households diversify income sources amid growing food insecurity, poverty and vulnerability to climate change impacts (Yaro and Hesselberg [2010](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR79 "Yaro, J.A., and J.                   https://doi.org/10.4314/rrias.v26i1.56957

                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.landusepol.2023.106589
                  
                .")). This aligns with the findings of Archibald et al. ([2010](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR10 "Archibald, S., A. Nickless, N. Govender, R. J. Scholes, and V. Such studies in Northern Ghana could also enhance effective fire management. Currently, fire policies and projects in Ghana often advocate for early burning without clearly establishing what early burning entails. This has led to various interpretations regarding the timing of early burns and the appropriate times to conduct them. Interviews with local communities in the Savannah Region suggested that a more tailored message around early burning, focusing on the moisture content of the vegetation and weather conditions, rather than the specific months, would be more useful and in line with traditional community decision-making.

### How governance, livelihoods, and LULC dynamics reshape fire regimes in Northern Ghana

In regions (North-East, Savannah, Upper East and West Regions) where large fires were decreasing based on MODIS burned area and active fire data, the workshop participants perceived these changes to be associated with intensified public sensitization and the implementation of government and multilateral flagship programs such as the SLWMP, PFJ, GSLERP, LEAN, and GLRSSMP. These programs have promoted tree planting and protection in selected districts and communities in Northern Ghana. A key aspect of these initiatives is the incorporation of fire management plans to suppress burning that could potentially destroy investments in these trees. The fire management plans typically include bushfire sensitization, the establishment of community fire volunteer groups, and the provision of incentives to discourage landscape burning.

Despite the win-win discourses (e.g., co-benefits for restoration, livelihoods and climate mitigation) that dominate tree planting initiatives, they can potentially promote land grabbing, constraining local people’s access to resources and other resource-dependent livelihoods (Benjaminsen et al. In International Conference on Global Land Grabbing."); Leach and Scoones [2015](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR50 "Leach, Melissa, and Ian Scoones. 2015. “Political ecologies of carbon in Africa.” In Carbon conflicts and forest landscapes in Africa. Routledge. 
                  https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315740416
                  
                ."); Kandel et al. [2021](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR37 "Kandel, Matt, Genevieve Agaba, Rahinatu S.. Alare, Thomas Addoah, and Kate Schreckenberg. 2021. Assessing social equity in farmer-managed natural regeneration (FMNR) interventions: Findings from Ghana. Ecological Restoration 39 (1–2): 64–76. 
                  https://doi.org/10.3368/er.39.1-2.64
                  
                .")). It was, therefore, not surprising to learn from institutional interviews that some tree planting projects in the Savannah Region of Northern Ghana were destroyed by arson. This occurred particularly in communities that lacked sufficient land for farming. Moreover, extensive tree planting projects along with fire suppression policies in these areas can also promote fuel build-up for wildfire risk in the event of drought (Veldman et al. [2019](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR73 "Veldman, Joseph W., J. C. Aleman, S. T. Alvarado, T. M. Anderson, S. Archibald, W. J. Bond, T. W. Boutton, and N. Buchmann. 2019. Comment on ‘the global tree restoration potential.’ Science 366:1–4. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaz0111
                  
                .")).

Participants also perceived population density and its knock-on effects on the establishment of fire posts and intensive agricultural activities (including the use of tractors and herbicides to clear land) as factors shaping burning patterns. The number of people per square kilometer varies greatly between regions: Upper East (147.2), Northern (87.1), North-East (72.6), Upper West (49.0) and Savannah (18.7) (GSS [2021](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR32 "GSS. 2021. “Ghana 2021 population and housing census: preliminary report.” Accra, Ghana.")). The most densely populated Upper East Region had the lowest extent of MODIS burned area and also showed a significantly decreasing trend. This is consistent with previous studies which suggest that high population density is linked to fewer fires, as it limits the amount of available fuel for combustion and spread (Archibald and Roy [2009](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR8 "Archibald, S., and D. P. Roy. 2009. Identifying individual fires from satellite-derived burned area data. International Geoscience and Remote Sensing Symposium (IGARSS) 3:160–163. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1109/IGARSS.2009.5417974
                  
                ."); Bistinas et al. [2013](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR14 "Bistinas, Ioannis, Duarte Oom, Ana C.L.. Sá, Sandy P.. Harrison, I. Colin. Prentice, and José M.C.. Pereira. 2013. Relationships between human population density and burned area at continental and global scales. PLoS ONE 8 (12): 1–12. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0081188
                  
                .")). Conversely, the very low population density and associated lack of intensive agricultural activity of the Savannah Region may help contextualize its relatively high count of active fires and large extent of burned area for both MODIS and Landsat datasets. While these patterns do not establish a direct causal relationship between population density and fire dynamics, they highlight the importance of considering demographic and land use context when interpreting regional variations in fire regimes.

Labor shifts and extractive economies like mining also indirectly shape fire regimes. Personal communications during fieldwork suggested that, in areas affected by artisanal and small-scale mining, particularly in parts of the Savannah and Upper West Regions, agricultural labor shortages have resulted in extensive fallow lands and increased biomass accumulation. Although this observation was not systematically examined in the present study, it points to a plausible mechanism through which livelihood reconfiguration may influence fire risk by increasing the availability of combustible material. Fire risk in such landscapes, therefore, emerges as a secondary consequence of livelihood reconfiguration consistent with findings of Viedma et al. ([2015](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR74 "Viedma, O., N. Moity, and J.M. Moreno. 2015. Changes in landscape fire-hazard during the second half of the 20th century: Agriculture abandonment and the changing role of driving factors. Agriculture, Ecosystems & Environment 207: 126–140. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agee.2015.04.011
                  
                .")) and Salis et al. ([2022](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR66 "Salis, M., L. Del Giudice, R. Jahdi, F. Alcasena-Urdiroz, C. Scarpa, G. Pellizzaro, V. Bacciu, M. Schirru, A. Ventura, M. Casula, F. Pedes, A. Canu, P. Duce, and B. Arca. 2022. Spatial patterns and intensity of land abandonment drive wildfire hazard and likelihood in Mediterranean agropastoral areas. Land. 
                  https://doi.org/10.3390/land11111942
                  
                .")) in Mediterranean countries.

Field observations by one of the authors in the Savannah Region further suggested that the observed high counts of active fires and extensively burned areas were associated with burning activities around the Mole National Park (MNP) to enhance livelihoods. The MNP also extends to the Northern and Upper West Regions. The predominant livelihoods in these regions revolved around charcoal production, hunting, and clearing new lands for yam (*Dioscorea alata*) cultivation, which all required fire use. These activities typically involve small fires, which can grow into large fires if not properly controlled, especially during the windy late dry season. Moreover, prescribed burning takes place in the park to regenerate grasses for grazing animals. There has also been an influx of migrant herders from neighboring countries who bring their cattle to graze in the Savannah, Northern, and Upper West Regions. These herders set fires to resprout grasses for grazing during the dry season (Kpienbaareh and Luginaah [2019](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR40 "Kpienbaareh, D., and I. Luginaah. 2019. After the flames then what? Exploring the linkages between wildfires and household food security in the Northern Savannah of Ghana. International Journal of Sustainable Development and World Ecology 26 (7): 612–24. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/13504509.2019.1640311
                  
                ."); Amoako and Gambiza [2020](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR2 "Amoako, Esther Ekua, and James Gambiza. 2020. “Traditional practices, knowledge and perceptions of fire use in a West African savanna parkland.” BioRxiv 60:53–77. 
                  https://doi.org/10.35979/alj.2020.02.60.53
                  
                .")). While the satellite data cannot attribute the active fires and burned areas to specific practices, these observations illustrate the diverse ways in which fire remains embedded within livelihood systems, socio-economic transformation, and conservation management rather than being influenced by a single driver.

For the Landsat burned area, participants perceived no change in traditional livelihood-related activities such as hunting, herding, charcoal burning, and using fires to clear new farmlands. Nevertheless, most participants at the workshop expressed a shared objective of suppressing all types of fires in Northern Ghana through the range of interventions indicated above. However, existing studies in Northern Ghana suggest that early dry season prescribed burns can promote species diversity and reduce the stem density of woody species (Amoako et al. [2023](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR3 "Amoako, Esther Ekua, Hamza Issifu, and Rikiatu Husseini. 2023. The effects of prescribed dry season burning on woody species composition, Mole National Park, Ghana. Tropical Conservation Science. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1177/19400829231164936
                  
                .")). Therefore, the timing, frequency, and extent of burning have more significant implications for land management (Laris [2002](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR43 "Laris, P. 2002. Burning the seasonal mosaic: Preventative burning strategies in the wooded savanna of Southern Mali. Human Ecology 30 (2): 155–186. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1015685529180
                  
                .")) than merely evaluating the total extent of the burned area.

The Landsat LULC findings showed an increase in agricultural and built-up expansions in Northern Ghana. This increase aligned with the land use changes perceived by stakeholders. It also supports findings of existing studies in the Upper Guinean Region of West Africa and Africa, which reported increases in agricultural lands (Andela et al. [2017](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR5 "Andela, N., D.C. Morton, L. Giglio, Y. Chen, G.R. Van Der Werf, P.S. Kasibhatla, R.S. DeFries, et al. 2017. A human-driven decline in global burned area. Science 356 (6345): 1356–62. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aal4108
                  
                ."); Dwomoh and Wimberly [2017](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR24 "Dwomoh, Francis K.., and Michael C.. Wimberly. 2017. Fire regimes and their drivers in the Upper Guinean region of West Africa. Remote Sensing. 
                  https://doi.org/10.3390/rs9111117
                  
                .")). Unlike these studies, our analysis does not support direct inference about the relationship between land cover change and burned area trends, as LULC was assessed at only two time points. However, the observed patterns provide important context for understanding shifts in fire regimes. The expansion of agriculture and settlements may be associated with the fragmentation of continuous fuel loads and reductions in extensive burned areas, while Landsat-derived burned areas may persist due to livelihood-related practices. This interpretation is consistent with stakeholder accounts that fire continues to be used for land preparation and the clearing of new farmlands. Nevertheless, further analysis using longer-term multi-temporal LULC data and patch-based fire data is needed to robustly examine the relationship between land cover change and fire dynamics in Northern Ghana.

### General limitations of the study

It is important to acknowledge key limitations. The low revisit frequency of Landsat, which occurs every 16 days, can lead to missed burned areas, especially in regions with rapid vegetative recovery, such as Northern Ghana. Although there is an observed increasing trend in burned areas, the actual extent and trends of these areas may be underestimated or overestimated. This discrepancy arises because the radiometric differences in Landsat-derived GABAM algorithms make it difficult to distinguish areas with low reflectance soils or low sun elevation angles (Long et al. [2019](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR51 "Long, Tengfei, Zhaoming Zhang, Guojin He, Weili Jiao, Chao Tang, Bingfang Wu, Xiaomei Zhang, Guizhou Wang, and Ranyu Yin. 2019. 30m resolution global annual burned area mapping based on Landsat images and Google Earth engine. Remote Sensing 11 (5): 1–30. 
                  https://doi.org/10.3390/rs11050489
                  
                .")). These limitations have implications for fire monitoring and environmental assessment. For instance, inaccuracies in burned area estimation can affect the reliability of fire-related carbon emissions calculations and potentially lead to misleading conclusions of regional contributions to greenhouse gas emissions (Qu et al. [2024](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR63 "Qu, G., Y. Shi, Y. Yang, W. Wu, and Z. Zhou. 2024. Methods, progress and challenges in global monitoring of carbon emissions from biomass combustion. In Atmosphere (Vol. 15, Number 10). Multidisciplinary Digital Publishing Institute (MDPI). 
                  https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos15101247
                  
                ."); Wiedinmyer et al. [2011](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR77 "Wiedinmyer, C., S. K. Akagi, R. J. Yokelson, L. K. Emmons, J. A. Al-Saadi, J. J. Orlando, and A. J. Soja. 2011. The Fire INventory from NCAR (FINN): A high resolution global model to estimate the emissions from open burning. Geoscientific Model Development 4 (3): 625–641. 
                  https://doi.org/10.5194/gmd-4-625-2011
                  
                .")). Additionally, errors in detecting burned area may compromise land management and policy decisions in fire-prone Northern Ghana savanna ecosystems, where timely and accurate information is essential for sustainable resource management. Furthermore, the underestimation or overestimation of burned areas can influence ecological interpretations, including assessment of vegetation recovery, biodiversity loss and land degradation (Ouattara et al. [2025](/article/10.1186/s42408-026-00553-w#ref-CR61 "Ouattara, B., M. Thiel, G. Forkuor, F. Mouillot, P. Laris, E.J. Tondoh, and B. Sponholz. 2025. Fire impacts, vegetation recovery, and environmental drivers in West African savannas (2014–2023): A high-resolution remote sensing assessment. International Journal of Applied Earth Observation and Geoinformation. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jag.2025.104783
                  
                .")). These uncertainties highlight the need for integrating higher temporal resolution datasets and improved classification approaches to ensure more robust fire monitoring outcomes. Addressing these challenges is therefore critical for improving the accuracy of burned area products and for supporting informed decision-making in climate mitigation, land management and ecosystem and biodiversity conservation in Northern Ghana.

The rainfall component of this study was constrained by gaps and inconsistencies in rain gauge observations across Northern Ghana, requiring the use of CHIRPS satellite-derived rainfall data from 2001 to 2022. Although CHIRPS offers spatially continuous estimates, its accuracy depends partly on the density and quality of the ground-based stations used to calibrate satellite observations. This can lead to an increasing uncertainty at local scales. In addition, satellite-derived estimates may overestimate low-intensity rainfall ('the drizzle effect'), potentially inflating annual rainfall totals without a corresponding increase in fuel moisture or vegetation growth. Consequently, this may have implications for the rainfall trends and the rainfall-fire relationship reported.

In terms of institutional interviews, because the study was designed at a regional scale (subnational level), the interviews were limited to regional actors and did not incorporate community-level perspectives. These local-level perspectives are explored in a forthcoming paper examining fire dynamics at a local scale to address these gaps.

## Conclusion

This study draws on evidence from remote sensing and institutional interviews to assess changing fire regimes in Northern Ghana. When considering Northern Ghana as a whole, Landsat and MODIS burned areas depicted different fire trends and types; MODIS showed a decrease in large fires, whereas Landsat revealed no change in burned area. These contrasting trends highlight the importance of sensor characteristics and spatial scale in shaping the interpretations of fire dynamics in Northern Ghana. Despite numerous project interventions on fires, there is little agreement on how different fires are defined. This lack of agreement overlooks the various types of fires in the savanna landscape and characterizes them solely as destructive fires. Therefore, local-level decision-makers may need to determine the types of fires they are interested in monitoring and evaluate them using the most consistent and appropriate methods. In this regard, MODIS satellite data would be suitable for monitoring large fires. In contrast, on-the-ground reporting or the use of alternative fine spatial resolution datasets such as Landsat or Sentinel-2 satellite data would be more appropriate for small fires. However, distinguishing fire by size or identifying individual fire events requires patch-based or event-based analysis of these finer datasets. Combining multiple data sources and analytical approaches could therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of fire dynamics and support context-specific fire management in Northern Ghana. Future research should focus on overcoming the limitations of the Landsat-derived GABAM dataset by reconstructing individual fire events from Landsat surface reflectance and strengthening ground-based validation efforts. Using sensors with both high spatial and temporal resolution, such as Sentinel-2, will be beneficial once a longer archive of imagery becomes available for studying long temporal trends. It is also important to note that the MODIS satellite is nearing the end of its operational life. As it is taken out of commission, the Sentinel-3 World Fire Atlas and Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) satellite data will be an alternative for monitoring large and active fires.

Region-specific trend analysis indicates that the Savannah Region stands out as the region with the most extensive burned area and higher active fire counts. Our results highlight the importance of scale in fire regime analysis since aggregating to the whole of Northern Ghana masked the varying trends in different regions. Thus, we argue that disaggregating data is important to ensure fire interventions are cost-effective by responding to region-specific fire trends.

The study also highlighted shifts in fire seasonality in Northern Ghana. It showed a decrease in early burning and an increase in fire activities in the mid-dry season, specifically in January and February. This has important implications for fire management in Northern Ghana, as policies and projects tend to promote early burning without explicitly defining what early burning entails and rely on November and December as months for early burning. This suggests that current fire management strategies may not be aligned with the actual fire dynamics of Northern Ghana. Therefore, there is a need for more adaptable and responsive fire management practices tailored to the changing fire regimes in this area.

Fire regimes were found to be impacted not only by biophysical factors but also mediated through political and socio-cultural factors. Effective and sustainable fire management will therefore require incorporating local perspectives, recognizing the ecological role of fires in savannas, and adopting monitoring approaches tailored to different fire types. Future studies on savanna fire regimes should holistically integrate these multiple interacting factors in human-environment modelling to better capture feedback between climatic variability, vegetation characteristics, and human land use practices.
