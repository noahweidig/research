# In the wake of California wildfires: Promoting psychological resilience for individuals and communities.

## Abstract

Climate change is increasing the frequency, intensity, and duration of wildfires in the United States, with Western states like California heavily impacted.Wildfires pose direct risks to individuals (e.g., property destruction, loss of life) and indirect risks through exposure to harmful fine particulate matter (PM 2.5 ) from wildfire smoke.However, the psychological impacts of wildfire exposure are largely understudied.To address this gap, this article will (a) review existing literature on mental health impacts of wildfires, (b) present new empirical findings on mental health impacts of exposure to wildfires and wildfire-induced PM 2.5 among Californian residents, and (c) discuss key takeaways for mental health practitioners.We conducted a secondary data analysis of California households that took part in NielsenIQ's Annual Ailments, Health, and Wellness Survey from 2011 to 2017.Participants (N = 22,509) reported whether any household member had experienced depression and/or anxiety in the past 6 months.We linked these data, at the zip code level, with past-year environmental data on wildfire and wildfire smoke (PM 2.5 ) exposure.Adjusting for key sociodemographic variables, fixed-effect models show exposure to both wildfires and PM 2.5 was associated with significantly greater probability of household depression and/or anxiety in the following year.Clinicians should be aware of mental health needs associated with wildfire exposure, including wildfire smoke exposure.The need for enhanced monitoring of mental health symptoms during times of acute and chronic exposure is discussed, as well as opportunities to rapidly deploy mental health resources to promote psychological resilience in affected communities. Clinical Impact StatementExposure to wildfires and harmful wildfire smoke is associated with increased risk of depression and anxiety, underscoring the serious mental health consequences of climate change.Practitioners should be ready to implement timely screening and referral systems, as well as provide direct services to affected individuals, both during and after wildfires and periods of wildfire smoke exposure to promote psychological resilience.
