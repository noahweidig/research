# Diverging wildfire regimes and doubling of burn area in western United States water supply watersheds

## Abstract

Recent increases in western United States wildfire activity are raising concerns for regional water supplies. Analyzing the wildfire histories (1940-2024) of 3,100 watersheds, we identify a significant regime shift in the late 20th century. Watersheds with higher runoff ratios, critical for regional water provisioning, have significantly increasing burn area trends. The burned areas double following the late 20th century changepoints. Analysis of 17,000 watersheds reveals a diverging fire regime: a transition from moderate burns (1-20% area) towards minimal (<1%) and extensive (> 20%) events. Vulnerability is greatest in forested and mountainous regions, specifically those providing water to 100,000 or more downstream consumers. An integrated watershed management approach, one that considers land management and downstream water operations, may help secure water supplies and mitigate the cascading hazards from wildfire. Watersheds with higher runoff generation in the western United States have experienced significantly increasing burn areas since the late 20th century, according to a study analyzing wildfire histories from 1940–2024 across more than 3,000 watersheds.

The western United States (U.S.) relies on mountainous and forested watersheds and critical headwaters, with forested lands contributing over half of the region’s water supply<sup>1</sup> . These headwater catchments, although under-represented in streamgage networks<sup>2</sup> , are essential for ecological health and municipal water resources by providing more than half of the surface drinking water for nearly 83 million people across the West<sup>3</sup> . However, as these landscapes face a shifting climate, water provisioning from these systems could become unreliable. Although wildfires are a natural component of these ecosystems, the escalation in fire extent and severity<sup>4</sup> is increasingly altering water supplies and runoff- related risks<sup>5</sup> . 

In recent decades there has been a dramatic increase in wildfire activity across the western U.S.<sup>6–8</sup> , representing a departure from late 19th and 20th century fire regimes and highlighting the evolving fire-climate-human intersections(Fig. 1).Notably,federalpolicieson wildfiremanagementhave prioritized rapid fire containment and suppression throughout the mid20th century, the same period in which much of the region’s primary water infrastructure was constructed<sup>9,10</sup> . Despite being a driver of aquatic impairment, wildfire impacts are not routinely reported by regulatory agencies<sup>26</sup> . This leaves millions of downstream consumers vulnerable to sudden water quality disruptions and supply shortages<sup>24,27</sup> .

### An increase in watersheds with extensive burns 

Across approximately 17,000 watersheds, annual burn area percentages revealed a diverging pattern in fire activity (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 2). The proportion of watersheds experiencing <1% or >20% burn areahad statistically significant increases (τ > 0.2, p < 0.01), while those with 1–19% burn categories declined. Each burn area category represents a separate part of the annual watershed burn area distribution, so changepoints were estimated independently for each class. Therefore, these years are interpreted as descriptive markers of when individual burn categories becamemoreorlesscommonandarenotevidenceforoneabrupttransition year. Pettitt’s test identified changepoints primarily in the late 20th century, 

coinciding with regional shifts toward warmer, drier conditions that extend fire seasons and can increase fire extent and burned area exposure (Supplementary Table 3). We observed a polarization in wildfire impacts, where many watersheds remain largely unburned, while a growing subset is undergoing extreme fire events, potentially exacerbating post-fire erosion and sediment loading in fluvial networks. These trends are consistent with Ball et al.<sup>26</sup> , who estimated that in 1984–2014, wildfires contributed to an annual increase of 342 km of impacted stream and river length within burned areas in the western U.S. Including downstream propagation of post-fire water-quality disturbances, the estimated wildfire exposure increased from 6 to 11% of western U.S. stream and river length. 

Across all burn area categories, the pre- and post-changepoint distributions (Fig. 3; black and white violin plots, respectively) highlight shifts toward lower runoff ratios, indicating that post-changepoint burning became more common across lower-runoff-ratio watersheds. However, the widening and upper-tail structure of the higher burn-area categories also indicates that extensive and repeated burning continued to intersect productive, runoff-efficient watersheds. Thus, the divergence is not a uniform upward shift in runoff ratio across all burn classes. Rather, it reflects redistribution away from intermediate burn fractions toward minimal burninginlower-runoff-ratiowatershedsandextensiveorrepeatedburning that still includes higher-runoff-ratio water-supply catchments. 

Shifting towards higher burn percentages has significant implications for watershed hydrology. A threshold of 20% burned area is associated with measurable increases in watershed yield<sup>5,24</sup> , while a 40% threshold may influence the upper limits of post-wildfire floods<sup>36</sup> . Divergence in the kernel density plot geometry and shifts in the median indicate movement in wildfire towards the extremes or the increasing prevalence of watersheds experiencing ≥20% burns (Fig. 3d, e) and repeated burns (≥100%) (Fig. 3f). This suggests that there are a growing number of catchments subject to multiple fires. The significant increase in the number of watersheds reaching higher burn fractions (20–49.99%, 50–99.99%, ≥100%), driven by increases in extreme fire weather and fuel accumulation, highlights the benefits of reevaluating current management strategies<sup>8,37</sup> . Additionally, vegetation type conversion has been associated with short fire intervals of <10 years<sup>38</sup> . Our analysis demonstrates that high-burn wildfires are more frequently intersecting important water supply catchments, providing actionable insights for prioritizing fire prevention, infrastructure resilience, and ecological restoration to protect critical water resources and downstream communities<sup>34</sup> .

### Increasing annual burned area forested and mountainous watersheds 

Annual burned area increased across a majority of western U.S. level 3 ecoregions, with the most pronounced trends occurring in forested and mountainous landscapes. Nineteen of the 35 level three ecoregions had statistically significant increases in burn area percentage (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 4). The most substantial increases (τ > 0.3) were observed in forested and mountainous ecoregions, including the Wasatchand Uinta Mountains, Southern Rockies, and Klamath Mountains (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 4). These increases are likely due to the abundant biomass and high fuel loads characteristic of dense coniferous forests, whichbecome particularly susceptible to wildfires during prolonged droughts<sup>37</sup> . Madakumbura et al.<sup>39</sup> observed that California’s fire season begins earlier by up to 46 days, with stronger effects in northern forested areas (e.g., Cascades: −46 ± 9 days; Sierra Nevada: −24 ± 7 days) due to earlier snowmelt, elevatedvapor pressure deficit, and reduced fuel moisture. This pattern aligns with documented regional shifts towards larger and more frequent wildfires in the Northern Rockies and Pacific Northwest, where fuel accumulation and aridification exacerbate burn extents<sup>23</sup> . While detected changepoints varied among ecoregions, there was clustering in the late 20th to the turn of the 21st century periods. This timing is consistent with prior western U.S. studies documenting increasing wildfire activity and stronger climate-fire relationships during recent decades<sup>4,40,41</sup> . 

In contrast, arid ecoregions, such as the Great Basin, had stable or declining trends, likely due to fuel limitations, which is similar to observations by Vanderhoof et al.<sup>42</sup> . This spatial heterogeneity highlights the vulnerability of montane forests, where reduced snowpack and earlier melt amplify post-fire risks, such as chronic soil erosion and degraded water quality<sup>31</sup> . Our results identify high water yield forested ecoregions that may benefit from adaptive management, where wildfires could propagate multi- 

year water quality degradation, characterized by elevated turbidity and nutrient loading in downstream systems. 

### Escalating wildfire impacts on downstream drinking water consumers 

We evaluated wildfire trends in critical western U.S. watersheds (Fig. 5), which were defined based on the number of downstream surface drinkingwater consumers and based on five intervals: <100, 100–999, 1000–9999, 10,000–99,999, and ≥100,000 consumers. We observed statistically significant increases in burned area for watersheds serving moderate to very high (1000 to ≥100,000) consumers. For example, watersheds supplying ≥100,000 downstream consumers had nearly doubled in average burn area, increasingfrom4.7 to 9.1% afterthe detected changepoint. Similar increases were observed in watersheds serving 10,000–99,999 consumers (4.08–8.29%)and1000–9999consumers(4.34–10.91%).Inthelast30years, as the number of watersheds experiencing burned areas grows, the average number of consumers depending on these fire-prone watersheds has increased by 80% (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 5). 

These trends indicate that wildfire activity is increasingly concentrated in watersheds critical for surface water supplies. This intensification is exacerbated by the intersection of warming temperatures and the forested high-yield headwaters. While droughts are known to intensify wildfire regimes in forested systems<sup>37,43</sup> , our results demonstrate that wildfires are now disproportionately concentrated in catchments that are most critical for water supply. In contrast, watersheds serving smaller numbers of consumers (<1000) had no significant trends (p > 0.05), likely due to their location in more arid, fuel-limited landscapes or differences in local land management practices<sup>44</sup> .

## Discussion 

Our results reveal a concerning divergence in fire behavior within the western U.S., characterized by a transition from frequent and small-extent burns (1–20%) to a diverging regime of either minimal fire or extensive, high-magnitude events (>20% of watershed area). The observed changepointsand increasing prevalence of high-magnitude burn years suggest that the underlying distribution of watershed disturbance has shifted. This polarization suggests that small-extent fire, which plays a critical role in healthy ecosystems<sup>45</sup> , is being replaced by large-scale disturbances. Smallextent wildfires can preserve mosaics of fuel structure and stand age, whereas extensive burns can homogenize cover and soil properties, amplifying hydrologic and biogeochemical responses<sup>5,24</sup> . In this work, we highlight a compounding effect in which critical water-provisioning catchments are experiencing greater burned-area exposure. 

The shift is most pronounced in watersheds that support the largest number of downstream drinking water consumers. For watersheds serving ≥100,000 consumers, the near-doubling of burn areas after the late 20thcentury changepoints represents a direct escalation of risk to municipal water supplies. Our findings reveal a growing and important spatial 

alignment between the headwater catchments that are essential for water provisioning and the landscapes experiencing the greatest escalation in wildfires (Fig. 6). This increasing frequency of wildfire disturbance in highyield water supply watersheds portends substantial challenges for water resources planning. 

Thepatternsobservedacrossapproximately3100watershedsprovidea cautionary framework for other Mediterranean and semi-arid climates globally, such as parts of Australia and southern Europe, where water security is increasingly tied to fire-prone headwaters<sup>34</sup> . Our findings suggest that traditional water management strategies based on historical stationarity<sup>11</sup> may need reconsideration. Mitigating the risks posed by this evolving wildfire regime requires a new management framework that emphasizes resilience, spanning from the burning headwaters (forest management) to infrastructure (reservoir capacity and treatment flexibility) to the final distribution network. 

The fiscal implications of this regime shift extend across capital, operations, and risk financing. ## Methodology 

### Data sources 

Analyses conducted in this study were applied to all watersheds within 11 western States (Washington, Oregon, California, Idaho, Montana, Utah, Colorado,Arizona,NewMexico,Nevada,andWyoming).Watershedswere delineated at the Hydrologic Unit Code 12 (HUC12) scale according to the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) Watershed Boundary Dataset (WBD). We used the National Interagency Fire Center (NIFC) Interagency Wildland Fire Perimeter History (IFPH) dataset from 1940 to 2024, clipped by the WBD HUC12 boundary to calculate annual burn area for each watershed. The IFPH is a longitudinal compilation of agency-authoritative perimeter sources. We acknowledge that data collection methodologies have shifted over the 84-year study period. For example, the pre-satellite era (<1984) relied primarily on ground-based observations and hand-drawn maps, which are subject to regionalinconsistencies across state agenciesand USGS regions. In contrast, modern records utilize high-fidelity incident geographic information system (GIS) products supported by global positioning system(GPS),aerial,infrared,andsatellite-basedmapping(e.g.,Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity (MTBS)). To mitigate potential biases introduced by the long-term data, we performed a sensitivity analysis that compares different time intervals. Our results confirm that shifts in fire frequency and magnitude were consistent across the 1940–1983 and 1984–2024 data eras (Supplementary Table 3). This inter-era consistency suggests that the identified trends reflecta true shift in wildfire regimes rather than an artifact of improved reporting. 

ERA5, a state-of-the-art global reanalysis, provides high-resolution (0.25°) data with reduced biasesin precipitation and temperature compared 

to predecessors, such as ERA-Interim, making it suitable for hydrological applications. ERA5 provides land-surface total runoff (surface plus subsurface) as a gridded flux generated by the land surface model. We therefore interpret ERA5 total runoff as a proxy for long-term water yield potential. However, its accuracy diminishes in complex western U.S. mountainous terrains due to orographic effects, potentially underestimating precipitation by 10–20% in regions like the Rockies<sup>49</sup> . ERA5 runoff estimates do not include wildfire effects and therefore represent the long-term watershed behavior that represents water supply suitability.

We obtained the number of downstream drinking water consumersforeachHUC12watershedfromtheUSDAForestServiceForeststo Faucets version 2.0 (F2FV2)<sup>51</sup> . The consumer data estimate the number of peopleservedbydownstreamsurfacedrinkingwaterintakesassociatedwith each upstream watershed and not the total resident population within the watershed.F2FV2usesHUC12-scale geospatialmodelingto identifycritical watersheds for surface drinking water, clean-water production, and threats to water yield, including wildfire. Using the HUC12 scale allowed for a finer spatial resolution (average area ~100 km<sup>2</sup> ), providing site-specific sampling points and maintaining an equal number of records per group. This makes the HUC12 scale suitable for analyzing localized wildfire effects with respect to water resource planning and fire risk assessment<sup>5</sup> . 

### Data processing 

A time series of burn areas was made for each watershed in Python (v3.12) using the Pandas (v2.3.2) and GeoPandas (v1.1.1) data analysis libraries. The xarray (v2024.08.01) Python package project was used when working with labeled multi-dimensional arrays, which in our case was the GRIB file containing monthly runoff values from 1940 to 2024. This multidimensional array was aggregated to a single raster of annually averaged runoff values at a 0.25° by 0.25° latitude and longitude grid. No resampling or interpolation was conducted to preserve data integrity. To ensure stable classifications independent of annual variability, watersheds were assigned to runoff ratio quantiles (20th, 40th, 60th, 80th, and 99th percentiles) based on their long-term average runoff ratio over the full study period (1940–2024).Thisstaticassignmentpreventsshiftingcategoriesduetoyearto-year precipitation influences and isolates trends in burn area relative to inherent watershed hydrology. GeoPandas was used for overlaying and spatially joining burn area polygons, watershed boundary polygons, and ecoregion shapefiles. All files were converted to EPSG 5070 (Albers Equal Area projection, NAD83 datum) to preserve accuracy of area by km<sup>2</sup> . Pandas was used for left joining database files and attribute tables for the F2F. 

### Statistical analysis 

Trend and changepoint tests were used as complementary descriptive diagnostics rather than mechanistic attribution tools. The Mann–Kendall (MK) and Theil–Senanalyses summarize monotonic changes acrossthefull record, whereas Pettitt’s test identifies approximate shifts in distributional central tendency. Annual burned area has high interannual variability and regional heterogeneity; thus, changepoints are interpreted as group-specific indicators of distributional change rather than abrupt causal transition years. 

Temporal trends in burn area percentages were evaluated using the MK test, a non-parametric method for detecting monotonic trends<sup>52,53</sup> . To account for potential autocorrelation in the time series (e.g., due to multiyear fire-climate linkages), we tested for serial correlation using the Ljung–Box test (at lag 1). Where significant autocorrelation was detected (p < 0.05), we applied the Hamed and Rao<sup>54</sup> variance correction modification of the MK test (implemented via the pymannkendall Python package). This provides conservative p-values under serial dependence. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05, with Kendall’s τ interpreted as follows: τ > 0.15 indicated an increasing trend, τ < − 0.15 a decreasing trend, and | τ| ≤ 0.15no trend. In this study, runoff ratio classifications serve as static indicators of relative long-term runoff generation potential across watersheds. This framing enables the comparison of wildfire trendsacrosshydrologic settings.It doesnotaccount for seasonal drought vulnerability, reservoir operations, groundwatersurface-water exchanges, or post-fire hydrologic response. As runoff ratio classes are based on long-term average ERA5 runoff and precipitation, statistical results describe burned area trends across relative runoff generation settings. Analyses do not estimate changes in actual streamflow, water availability, or hydrologic response after fire.Forthe assessmentof the number of watersheds in different burn percentage interval categories, burn area percentage intervals were derived from the cumulative burn area in a watershed in a year divided by the total area of the watershed. Thus, watersheds with ≥100% burn area have burned multiple times in the year. For assessing the temporal trends of burn area with respect to ecoregion, the observation points were the cumulative burn area divided by the total ecoregion area in our study area. 

Statistical power was assessed via Monte Carlo simulations (1000 replications) following Yue et al.<sup>57</sup> and Holbert<sup>58</sup> . These simulations demonstrated high power (>90%) for detecting trends across the range of observed Kendall’s τ values (0.15–0.35) at our sample size (n = 85), even under moderate autocorrelation. This supports confidence in both significant trends and the absence of trends in certain groups.
