# Fire history and atmospheric drying jointly control forest reburn severity

## Abstract

Abstract Background Wildfire activity is increasing across western United States forests, leading to more frequent reburns, or areas that burn multiple times within short intervals (approximately two decades). Prior fire can reduce subsequent burn severity through fuel limitation, but this effect is not consistent across landscapes or climate conditions. Understanding when reburns burn less severely and when they do not is critical for predicting future fire behavior and informing management. Here, we evaluated how reburn severity varies with fire history, time since fire, and atmospheric drying across reburn events in 23 EPA Level III ecoregions in the western U.S. Results Using 755 small and large forested reburn events from 2001–2024 and hierarchical Bayesian models of modeled burn severity (Composite Burn Index, CBI, range 0–3), we found that predicted severity was about 15% higher in previously unburned areas than in reburned areas. However, this effect was structured by interactions between fuel history and atmospheric drying (vapor pressure deficit, VPD). Within reburned areas, reburn severity increased with time since fire and atmospheric drying, with both effects depending on initial fire severity. Predicted severity ranged from ~0.6 CBI under low VPD and shorter intervals to ~1.7 CBI under high VPD and longer intervals of the observed time range. Atmospheric drying had a stronger effect following low-severity initial fires, whereas time since fire had a stronger effect following higher-severity initial fires, indicating a shift from climate-mediated to fuel-mediated controls. Across ecoregions, these interactions reveal multiple pathways to elevated reburn severity, with interval effects strongest in more productive systems and weaker in drier interior regions. Conclusions Reburn severity is governed by interacting effects of fire history, time since fire, and atmospheric drying, rather than prior fire alone. While prior fire often reduces subsequent burn severity, this buffering effect weakens over time and under extreme fire weather. The moderating effect of prior fire therefore diminishes over time and is contingent on environmental conditions. Strategies that rely on recent fire, such as managed wildfire or prescribed burning, to reduce future severity may provide short-term benefits but are unlikely to remain reliable as fire weather becomes increasingly warm and dry.

### **Background** 

Climate warming, fuel accumulation, and human ignitions have collectively increased the frequency, size, and severity of wildfires, pushing many fire regimes beyond their historical range of variability (Westerling et al. 2006; Miller et al. 2009; Balch et al. 2017; Parks and Abatzoglou 2020). At the same time, a century of fire suppression has produced a substantial fire deficit across much of western U.S. forests, resulting in fuel accumulation and altered forest conditions despite recent increases in burned area (Parks et al. 2015, 2025). As fires become larger and more numerous, they increasingly overlap in space and time, causing many landscapes to burn multiple times within just a few decades and creating increasingly common reburns (Collins et al. 2009; Turner et al. 2019). Although such fire-fire interactions were likely more common historically in some ecosystems, their re-emergence under altered fuels and a warming climate may produce ecological outcomes that differ from their historical patterns (Hagmann et al. 2021). These short-interval (i.e., less than approximately two decades) and overlapping fires highlight that wildfires should not be viewed as isolated disturbance events, but rather as interacting processes in which one fire can influence the behavior and ecological effects of subsequent fires. Understanding how such firefire interactions influence ecosystem trajectories is critical for anticipating shifts in vegetation resilience, carbon storage, and fire behavior (Brown and Smith 2000). It also holds direct relevance for management, where anticipating the cumulative effects of repeated fires can inform strategies for restoration, fuel reduction, and risk mitigation across fire-prone landscapes. 

Fire severity is a central measure of wildfire effects on ecosystems, reflecting the magnitude of vegetation mortality, organic matter loss, and ecological change caused by burning (Lentile et al. 2006; Keeley 2009). In forests, severity is commonly quantified using both field-based metrics such as the Composite Burn Index (CBI) (Key and Benson 2006) and remotely sensed indices of spectral change such as the delta Normalized Burn Ratio (dNBR) (Miller and Thode 2007). Because CBI integrates vegetation and substrate responses across multiple forest strata, it provides a particularly useful measure of ecological fire effects in reburned forests. Increasing attention has focused on reburn severity, or how prior fires influence the severity of subsequent burns. Previous studies have reported contrasting reburn outcomes, with some reburns burning less severely due to fuel limitation and others burning more severely following vegetation recovery or under extreme fire weather conditions (Collins et al. 2009; Holden et al. 2010; Parks et al. 2014; Coppoletta et al. 2016; Stevens-Rumann and Morgan 2019; Whitman et al. 2019; Jaffe et al. 2023). These apparently 

inconsistent findings suggest that reburn severity is governed by interacting feedbacks among initial fire effects, post-fire fuel recovery, and climatic conditions rather than by prior fire alone. 

Initial fire severity and time since fire appear to play especially important roles in shaping these feedbacks. Areas that previously burned at high severity may initially reburn less severely because combustion and vegetation mortality reduce available fuels (Collins et al. 2009; Parks et al. 2014; Whitman et al. 2019). However, as time since fire increases, fuel recovery and vegetation change can instead promote more severe reburning, particularly where flammable shrub or grass fuels accumulate following high-severity fire (Thompson et al. 2007; Coppoletta et al. 2016; Stevens-Rumann and Morgan 2019). At the same time, these fuel-related feedbacks are not static, as extreme fire weather can override the dampening effects of previous burns. Hot, dry, and wind-driven fire weather, such as high vapor pressure deficit (VPD), has been shown to strongly influence whether prior fires act as barriers to spread and severity or are effectively reset by extreme fire behavior (Collins et al. 2009; Parks et al. 2015; Coppoletta et al. 2016; Whitman et al. 2024). Together, these studies suggest that reburn severity emerges from interacting pathways involving initial fire effects, fuel recovery through time, and atmospheric drying. We synthesize these pathways into a conceptual framework (Fig. 1) that illustrates how fire history and climatic conditions jointly determine whether prior fires dampen or amplify subsequent fire severity. 

Although numerous studies have shown that prior fires influence subsequent burns, major uncertainties remain regarding the general mechanisms governing reburn severity across diverse forest ecosystems of the Western United States. Existing studies have often reported context-dependent or contrasting results across regions, ecosystems, and fire conditions (Collins et al. 2009; Holden et al. 2009, 2010; Parks et al. 2018; Tortorelli et al. 2024), but broad-scale synthesis linking these outcomes into a unified framework remains limited. In particular, uncertainties persist regarding how initial fire severity, recovery time, atmospheric drying, and environmental context interact to shape reburn severity across diverse landscapes. Many previous analyses have also relied on pixel-level approaches that are well suited for identifying local relationships but may be less effective for capturing how fire-fire interactions emerge across entire fire events, where fuels, weather, and spatially connected fire behavior interact to shape outcomes. For example, Tortorelli et al. (2024) analyzed 726 fire pairs across four western U.S. ecoregions and found that low- to moderate-severity prior fires can reduce subsequent severity depending on prior fire severity and climatic conditions. Our analysis uses an event-level, within-fire comparative framework leveraging multi-decadal (2001–2024) satellite records and modeled CBI estimates across 755 forested reburn events spanning 23 western U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Level III ecoregions. Rather than treating individual pixels as independent observations, we treat each reburn event as an integrated ecological and fire-behavior process. Fires are shaped by spatially connected fuels, shared weather conditions (including wind), suppression activities, and fire spread dynamics that collectively influence severity patterns across entire perimeters. By comparing reburn and nonreburn areas within the same second fire, we isolate how 

fire history influences subsequent severity while minimizing confounding from event-level weather and environmental context, though we do note that reburn and non-reburn areas can still differ systematically in fuel type, topographic position, fire spread pathways, localized weather (particularly wind), prior management, and suppression activities. This framework allows us to evaluate how initial fire severity, time since fire, atmospheric drying, and environmental context interact to shape reburn severity at the scale at which fires spread, ecological feedbacks emerge, and management decisions are often made. Our goal is not to replace pixel-level analyses, which remain valuable for identifying local-scale relationships, but to complement them with an eventscale perspective that captures broader fire-fire interactions across western U.S. forests. 

Prior fires can leave lasting legacies that influence fuel availability and flammability, motivating an examination of how fire history and environmental context jointly shape reburn severity. Specifically, we ask: (1) whether reburn severity differs from non-reburn severity within the same fire events, (2) how the severity of the initial fire and the time between fires are associated with subsequent reburn severity, (3) how fire-weather conditions during the reburn event interact with these legacies, and (4) whether these relationships vary across ecoregions

with differing forest types, climate regimes, and fire histories. We predict that reburn severity will generally be lower than non-reburn severity because initial fires can reduce fuel loads and alter fuel structure. However, we expect this effect to depend strongly on both the severity of the initial fire and the time elapsed since that fire, which together influence post-fire recovery trajectories. High-severity initial fires may promote ecological state transitions (e.g., forest to shrubland or grassland) that alter vegetation structure and subsequent fire behavior, whereas longer intervals between fires may allow forest regeneration and fuel accumulation that increase the potential for high-severity reburns. Although ecological state transitions are outside the scope of our analysis, which focuses on forest-to-forest reburns, high-severity fires can substantially alter forest fuel structure, continuity, and composition, including changes in understory vegetation, with implications for subsequent fire behavior and reburn severity. We further expect that fire-weather conditions, defined as the short-term atmospheric conditions during a fire event (e.g., temperature, vapor pressure deficit, and precipitation deficits), will interact with these post-fire legacies to influence reburn severity by affecting fuel moisture and fire behavior. Finally, because vegetation composition, climate regimes, and historical fire dynamics differ across western U.S. ecoregions, we expect the strength and direction of these relationships to vary regionally. By quantifying how fire history and environmental conditions jointly shape reburn severity across diverse ecoregions, this study provides a predictive framework for understanding when reburns are likely to amplify or dampen fire effects under increasingly active fire regimes as well as advances understanding of how interacting disturbance legacies and environmental conditions shape ecosystem responses to repeated fire. 

### **Methods** 

In order to assess patterns of fire severity in forested reburned areas, we compiled event-level data on fire history, day-of-event weather, and topography from 2001–2024 in the western U.S. for 935 fire events (542 unique initial fires and 393 unique subsequent fires that reburned portions of those initial events), resulting in a total of 755 forested, distinct reburned areas and associated non-reburned areas, called “reburn events” moving forward (Fig. 2A). We defined reburned area as the intersecting area between a single initial fire and a single subsequent fire and non-reburned area as the nonintersecting area of the subsequent fire and initial fire during the study period (Fig. 2C). We note that a small proportion by area (3.75%) of non-reburned areas were previously burned prior to 2001, but that those prior fires had little effect on inference. Our approach reduces 

confounding from shared environmental context, timeof-event weather conditions, and event-level fire behavior but does not control for systematic differences in fuel type, topographic position, fire spread pathways, prior management, and suppression activities. Additionally, we restricted our analysis to cases where both initial and subsequent fires occurred in areas that remained forested, excluding post-fire transitions to non-forest vegetation. This focus allowed us to isolate reburn severity dynamics within persistent forest systems, but as a result, our inferences apply to reburn dynamics within persistent forest systems rather than to post-fire regime shifts. Among the 542 unique initial fires, 104 (19.2%) contributed to multiple reburn fires, and among the 393 unique subsequent fires, 140 (35.6%) reburned multiple initial fires. To account for this non-independence among observations arising from shared first and second fire conditions, we included random effects in our models, which are discussed in more detail in the Data analysis section. 

#### **Fire perimeters and reburn event delineation** 

We extracted 2,960 fire event polygons from the Fire Event Delineation (FIRED) data product (Balch et al. 2020) that occurred in 2001–2024 in EPA Level III ecoregions that intersected with the 11 western U.S. states (Omernik and Griffith 2014). FIRED fire perimeters were derived using a spatiotemporal moving window algorithm from the Collection 6 MODIS Burned Area Product (MCD64A1) and includes final event-level perimeters, daily event-level perimeters, ignition dates, and the total fire duration for each event as well as accompanying metadata such as land cover class, making it an ideal dataset to examine reburn events in forested areas. FIRED also includes fires as small as 62 acres (~ 25 ha), whereas the Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity (MTBS) perimeter dataset, another commonly used fire perimeter dataset, only includes fires greater than 1000 acres (~ 405 ha) in the U.S. west. We identified reburn events within the FIRED dataset as separate event-level fire perimeters with overlapping areas that did not occur in the same year. To reduce positional uncertainty associated with the 500 m resolution of the MODIS Burned Area Product from which FIRED is based, and to avoid treating very small overlaps as meaningful reburn units, we restricted reburn and non-reburn events to those with an intersecting area of 1  km<sup>2</sup> or larger. Additionally, any second fires that were entirely contained in the perimeter of the first-fire were removed due to the lack of nonreburn control areas. All fire polygons were analyzed in an equal-area projection (EPSG:5070).

#### **Fire severity** 

To measure ecologically meaningful fire severity in forested systems across western ecoregions, we used Landsat imagery to estimate a modeled bias-corrected CBI 

Bitters _et al. Fire Ecology          (2026) 22:170_ 

Page 6 of 16 

for FIRED polygons following methods from Parks et al. 2019. CBI is the most commonly used protocol for field estimation of burn severity in forested systems, providing a balance between field efficiency and ecologically meaningful estimates of vegetation injury and mortality that align with independent quantitative measures (Dragozi et al. 2016; Saberi et al. 2022). This field measurement has been linked to ecologically naive but commonly used remotely sensed burn indices (e.g., dNBR), with random forest modeled estimations of CBI performing well in the western U.S. (Parks et al. 2019). Parks et al. developed a random forest model that predicts field-measured CBI from Landsat-derived spectral indices and geographic context, allowing CBI to be estimated continuously across burned landscapes. The model was trained and validated using field-measured CBI observations collected across the western United States. Because the initial model tended to underpredict high-severity burns and overpredict low-severity burns, Parks et al. developed an empirical bias-correction procedure to reduce these systematic errors, resulting in improved estimates across the full severity gradient. We used Google Earth Engine (Gorelick et al. 2017) and the algorithm designed by Parks et al. (2019) to estimate CBI across fire perimeters and apply the bias correction suggested to reduce residual bias at high and low CBI values, resulting in 30 × 30 m resolution data for all fire events. In this paper, we refer to these estimations of modeled bias-corrected CBI as ‘CBI’ for simplicity. We then averaged all pixelbased CBI values to calculate an event-level mean CBI for the reburn area of the first fire, reburn area of the second fire, and the non-reburn area of the second fire for each fire event. 

#### **Environmental variables** 

We also collected climatic, topographic, and forest context data related to reburns. We downloaded daily gridMET data to characterize atmospheric conditions for each day of each fire event (Abatzoglou 2013). Because gridMET has a spatial resolution of approximately 4 km, some small fire polygons did not contain the centroid of a gridMET cell and therefore did not return any values during the initial extraction. For these cases, we buffered the daily fire polygon by one gridMET pixel width and repeated the extraction. This approach ensured that at least one gridMET cell intersected the buffered polygon while preserving the original extraction method for all larger fire polygons. Depending on the spatial alignment between the buffered polygon and the gridMET grid, the buffered extraction could include one or multiple gridMET cells. For each day during the duration of the fire, we separately averaged all pixel values to calculate daily vapor pressure deficit (VPD; kPa), precipitation 

(mm), and minimum and maximum temperature (°C). We then averaged these daily values to calculate a mean event-level value for each metric, representing the average atmospheric conditions experienced during each fire event. To characterize the variability underlying these event-level averages, we summarized fire duration and within-fire daily weather variability (standard deviation and range) across all events (Table S1). We focused on these variables because they characterize broad atmospheric drying and moisture conditions that are expected to influence burn severity at the scale of entire fire events. We did not include wind because our objective was to characterize event-level climatic conditions rather than short-duration fire behavior, and spatially averaged wind is unlikely to capture the localized, episodic wind events that often drive extreme fire behavior. While event-level averaging facilitates comparisons among fires and supports inference at management-relevant spatial scales, it does not capture short-duration periods of extreme fire weather that may disproportionately influence burn severity, particularly for VPD. To calculate topographic variables, we used the 30 × 30 m spatial resolution digital elevation models from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) 30 m dataset (NASA Jpl 2013). We calculated the mean elevation (m), slope (rise/run), and aspect (circular degrees) for each fire polygon using the _richDEM_ package in Python version 3.10.13 (Barnes 2016). Aspect was then transformed into northness (cos(aspect × π/180)) and eastness (sin(aspect × π/180)). To account for differences in pre-fire forest cover, we calculated the proportion of forested NLCD classes in the reburn area of the first fire, reburn area of the second fire, and the non-reburn area of the second fire for each fire event. Figure 3 shows density distributions of fire history, climatic, topographic, and forest-context variables included in our final model. 

#### **Data analysis**

Candidate models increased progressively in complexity and were compared using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO), after which the highest-performing and most interpretable model was selected for inference. Fire history predictor variables included fire area type (categorical: reburn, non-reburn), first-fire CBI (reburns only), years between fires (reburns only), and log-transformed fire area. To account for variation in baseline vegetation context, we also included percent forest cover for each fire area (reburn and non-reburn) and, for reburns, percent forest cover during the initial fire. Including percent forest as a covariate allowed us to account for variation in how well CBI represented burn severity across observations, particularly in areas with lower forest cover, as well as to reduce bias from differences in vegetation context and from the context dependence of CBI interpretation. Non-reburn observations were assigned structural zero values for first-fire CBI, years between fires, and first-fire percent forest cover because these variables are undefined where no prior fire occurred during the study period. These values served only as placeholders to 

permit inclusion of reburn and non-reburn observations within a single modeling framework and were not interpreted as measured values. The main effect of fire area type captured the average difference between reburn and non-reburn observations, while the coefficients for firstfire CBI, years between fires, and first-fire percent forest cover were estimated only from variation among reburn observations. Thus, these predictors were interpreted exclusively within reburn contexts, where prior fire history existed. Candidate climatic predictor variables included mean daily VPD, mean daily precipitation, mean daily minimum temperature, and mean daily maximum temperature. Because minimum and maximum temperature were highly correlated with VPD (|r| > 0.8), they were excluded from the analysis to reduce collinearity among predictors. Candidate topographic predictor variables included mean elevation, mean slope, mean northness, and mean eastness. Models including northness exhibited persistent convergence problems (e.g., poor

chain mixing, R̂ values exceeding acceptable thresholds, and low effective sample sizes) that were not resolved through standard model tuning. Because eastness did not exhibit these issues and improved overall model stability, northness was excluded from subsequent analyses. All continuous predictors were standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1) prior to analysis to improve model convergence and facilitate comparison of regression coefficients. 

We fit a series of 21 candidate hierarchical Bayesian mixed-effects models that all shared the same underlying modeling framework but differed in their combinations of fixed effects, interaction terms, and random effects structures (Supplementary Text S1 and Table S2). Models ranged from a null model to progressively more complex formulations that evaluated the influence of fire area type, prior fire severity, inter-fire interval, fire area, and environmental covariates on burn severity, quantified as modeled CBI. Because multiple first fires contributed to multiple reburn observations and multiple second fires were associated with multiple first fires, we evaluated model structures including first-fire ID, second-fire ID, and event ID (the combination of first- and second-fire IDs) as random intercepts. Models including first-fire ID or event ID as random intercepts did not improve predictive performance relative to models including secondfire ID alone, and the estimated variance associated with these grouping factors was negligible. Thus, all candidate models just included second-fire ID as a random intercept, which accounted for clustering at the level of the second fire and represented the dominant source of non-independence in the data. Additionally, all candidate models included ecoregion as a random intercept to account for ecoregion-specific ecological variance. Candidate models also differed in their random effects structure. Simpler models included only random intercepts, whereas more complex models additionally included random slopes for fire area type, first-fire severity, and/or years between fires by ecoregion to allow these relationships to vary among ecoregions. The likelihood for CBI was modeled using a Student’s t distribution to accommodate heavy-tailed residual distributions commonly observed in burn severity data, with weakly informative priors for fixed effects and hierarchical variance parameters. We fit models using four Markov chains, with the most complex models run for 12,000 iterations (6,000 warmup) using the No-U-Turn Sampler. Convergence was assessed via R̂ values (~ 1.0) and effective sample sizes (> 2,000), and no divergent transitions were observed. 

Model comparison was based on leave-one-out crossvalidation (LOO) using the _loo_ package in R (Vehtari et al. 2017). Throughout model selection, several models including fire history variables and environmental 

covariates performed similarly in predictive performance (ΔELPD < SE; Table S2), so we selected the most parsimonious and interpretable model among those with similar predictive performance for subsequent inference. To evaluate potential nonlinear relationships, we also fit an alternative final model using spline-based smooth terms for years between fires and its interaction with first-fire CBI. This model showed improved predictive performance based on LOO, but because the spline model yielded the same direction and qualitative structure of the main effects and interactions, we retained the linear model for interpretability and reporting consistency. 

Our final selected model included fire area type, firstfire CBI (reburns only), years between fires (reburns only), log-transformed fire area, VPD, precipitation, elevation, slope, eastness, percent forest cover (reburns and non-reburns), first-fire percent forest cover (reburns only), an interaction between first-fire CBI and years between fires, and an interaction between first-fire CBI and VPD as fixed effects, random intercepts for secondfire ID and ecoregion, and random slopes of fire area type and years between fires (reburns only) by ecoregion. The interaction terms allowed the effects of inter-fire interval and atmospheric drying to vary depending on the severity of the initial fire, reflecting our hypothesis that prior fire severity modifies how fuels recover through time and respond to subsequent fire-weather conditions. We did not include interactions between fire area type and all predictors because our primary interest was in estimating the average difference in severity between reburn and non-reburn fires, and because several predictors (e.g., first-fire severity and years between fires) were undefined or ecologically constrained in non-reburn contexts. The selected model explained a large proportion of variation in burn severity, indicating strong explanatory power while accounting for hierarchical structure (Bayesian R<sup>2</sup> = 0.883, 95% credible interval [0.876, 0.888]). 

Posterior predictive checks (density overlays and scatterplots of predicted vs. observed CBI) confirmed that fitted distributions captured observed variability, and residual diagnostics revealed no major violations. Random effects diagnostics indicated reasonable variation among ecoregions without extreme outliers. We assessed spatial autocorrelation in model residuals using Moran’s I calculated from centroid coordinates of reburn polygons with a $k$-nearest-neighbor spatial weights matrix (k = 5, chosen to balance local neighborhood representation and computational efficiency). Residual spatial autocorrelation was weak (Moran’s I = 0.008, _P_ = 0.288), indicating that most spatial structure was captured by the environmental predictors and hierarchical random effects. ### **Discussion** 

Across 755 forested reburn events from 2001–2024 and 23 Level III ecoregions spanning the western U.S., we found that reburn severity was lower on average in marginal predictions than severity in areas that had not previously burned, supporting findings from pixel-based and case-study analyses across multiple regions (Collins et al. 2009; Parks et al. 2014; Tortorelli et al. 2024), but that this pattern was strongly conditional on fire history and atmospheric drying. The lower severity of reburns in marginal predictions reflected the distribution of fire history and environmental conditions in the dataset, rather than a strong independent effect of reburn when holding covariates constant. Marginal predictions showed a consistent reduction in reburn severity (Fig. 4), but this difference varied systematically with time since fire, initial fire severity, and day-of-reburn VPD (Fig. 5). These results demonstrate that fire-fire interactions are not uniform across space or time, but instead emerge from the interaction between fuel dynamics and climatic constraints. Rather than a single dominant effect, reburn severity reflects shifting limitations imposed by fuels and climate, with multiple pathways leading to high-severity outcomes (Fig. 5C). The large variance associated with the second-fire random effect further indicates that characteristics of the reburning fire event itself exerted a strong influence on severity outcomes, supporting the value of event-level approaches that explicitly account for fire-specific context beyond prior fire history alone. More broadly, our results show that relationships commonly reported at pixel scales remain detectable when the unit of inference is the fire event, indicating that fire-history signals scale up to the level at which many management decisions are made. 

#### **Fuel recovery governs long‑term reburn severity** 

The lower reburn severity observed in marginal predictions is consistent with well-established fire-fuel feedbacks, in which recent fire limits fuel availability and continuity, thereby constraining subsequent fire behavior 

Bitters _et al. Fire Ecology          (2026) 22:170_ 

Page 13 of 16 

(Parks et al. 2014; Stevens-Rumann et al. 2016; Stevens-Rumann and Morgan 2016; Yocom et al. 2022). Short-interval reburns, in particular, often occur before fuels have re-accumulated sufficiently to support highseverity fire, resulting in lower severity relative to adjacent unburned areas. However, our results show that this feedback weakened with increasing time since fire across the ~20-year interval range represented in our study. Reburn severity increased with longer fire-free intervals, with interval effects on the order of ~0.18 CBI units under moderate conditions and up to ~0.28 CBI units under high atmospheric drying. These increases were most evident in productive ecoregions such as the Klamath Mountains and North Cascades, where longer intervals substantially elevated predicted severity (Fig. 6). This pattern is consistent with fuel recovery and increasing structural connectivity following fire (Coppoletta et al. 2016), and aligns with prior work showing that vegetation regrowth can restore the potential for highseverity fire within one to several decades, depending on productivity and disturbance history (Tepley et al. 2017). Although we did not explicitly model fuel structure or vegetation recovery, these interpretations are consistent with established post-fire successional dynamics. 

Importantly, the effect of time since fire depended on initial fire severity. In short-interval scenarios, increasing initial fire severity was associated with slightly lower reburn severity (e.g., −0.33 CBI units under high VPD), suggesting persistent fuel limitation following higherseverity fire. In contrast, under longer intervals and lower atmospheric drying, the effect of initial severity weakened or reversed, indicating that fuel recovery can eventually offset these limitations, likely due to the regeneration of flammable understory vegetation and dense, even-aged stands that can promote crown fire once fuels accumulate (Tepley et al. 2018). Together, these results suggest that the legacy of initial fire severity persists for years to decades but interacts dynamically with recovery processes. Although the fitted model predicted that this suppressive effect may continue under more severe initial fires, these predictions extended beyond the observed range of first-fire CBI because our analysis excluded many standreplacing fires that transitioned away from forest cover. Additional work including such fires is needed to evaluate whether this relationship persists under the highest burn severities. 

#### **Multiple pathways to higher‑severity reburns** 

Atmospheric drying emerged as one of the strongest controls on reburn severity. Across scenarios, increasing VPD from low (0.5 kPa) to high (3.5 kPa) increased predicted reburn severity by ~0.71 to 1.01 CBI units, depending on fire history. These effects were largest 

following low-severity initial fires, where fuels remained abundant, and somewhat weaker following higherseverity fires, where fuel limitation constrained the response. In these low-severity initial fire cases, fuels likely remained abundant after the first-fire, allowing subsequent extreme fire weather to drive higher burn severity (Abatzoglou and Williams 2016; Holden et al. 2018). Predicted values illustrate these combined effects (Fig. 5C). Under low atmospheric drying, reburn severity remained relatively low across scenarios (~0.59–0.62 CBI for short intervals and ~0.73–0.87 CBI for long intervals). Under high atmospheric drying, predicted severity increased substantially, reaching ~1.30–1.63 CBI under short intervals and ~1.58–1.74 CBI under long intervals. Across all combinations of fire history and climate, the difference between the lowest- and highest-severity scenarios was ~0.96 CBI units [0.60, 1.31], indicating nearly a full-unit shift in severity across observed conditions. As warming increases atmospheric drying and lengthens fire seasons, the climatic conditions under which prior-fire buffering weakens may become increasingly common. 

These patterns point to two primary, but not mutually exclusive, pathways to high-severity reburns. First, reburns following low-severity initial fires can burn severely under high atmospheric drying, where abundant residual fuels allow climate to dominate fire behavior. Second, reburns following higher-severity initial fires can burn more severely after longer fire-free intervals, where fuel recovery reduces initial limitations. Rather than discrete mechanisms, these pathways represent ends of a continuum in which the relative importance of fuels and climate shifts across space and time. 

#### **Reburn responses vary across ecosystems** 

Substantial spatial variability in reburn severity further underscores the context dependence of fire-fire interactions. Across standardized scenarios, predicted reburn severity varied widely among ecoregions (Fig. 6). Under short intervals, reburn severity was generally lower than non-reburn severity across most ecoregions, with the largest reductions observed in systems such as the Canadian Rockies (− 0.61 CBI [− 0.77, − 0.40]) and Central Basin and Range (− 0.55 CBI [− 0.78, − 0.25]). Under longer intervals, these differences diminished or reversed in some regions, with several ecoregions showing weak or positive differences between reburn and non-reburn severity (e.g., Central California Foothills and Coastal Mountains, Northern Rockies, and Northern Basin and Range). Ecoregions also differed in their response to firefree interval. In productive systems such as the Central California Foothills, Klamath Mountains, and North Cascades, increasing the interval from 5 to 20 years increased reburn severity by ~ 0.42–0.68 CBI units. contrast, drier interior systems such as the Columbia Plateau and Mojave Basin and Range showed little change with interval, indicating persistent fuel limitation. These differences highlight strong geographic variation in how quickly fuels recover and how responsive systems are to climatic forcing. 

At the same time, uncertainty varied substantially among ecoregions, particularly those with small sample sizes (e.g., ≤ 5 events; Table S8). In these regions, credible intervals were wide and often overlapped zero, indicating that inferences about reburn effects should be interpreted cautiously. The hierarchical model partially mitigates this limitation through partial pooling, but estimates in sparsely sampled ecoregions remain less certain than those in well-sampled regions such as the Southern Rockies or Cascades. 

These regional differences likely reflect variation in productivity, fuel structure, and climate regimes, as well as differences in historical fire frequency and vegetation composition (Coop et al. 2020; Buma et al. 2020; Wasserman and Mueller 2023; Tortorelli et al. 2024). The strong ecoregion- and event-level variability observed in our model (Figure S2) further suggests that local conditions and fire-specific dynamics play a critical role in shaping reburn outcomes beyond regional averages. 

#### **Management implications** 

From a management perspective, our findings suggest that prior fires can reduce subsequent fire severity under certain conditions, particularly over short intervals, but that this effect weakens as time since fire increases and under conditions conducive to extreme fire behavior. Strategic use of managed wildfire or prescribed fire may therefore help provide short- to medium-term reductions in fire severity, especially in forest types where fuel recovery is slow. However, reliance on past fires as longterm buffers against severe reburning may be misplaced, particularly in a warming climate that promotes accumulation of flammable and dense fuels and more extreme fire weather (Abatzoglou et al. 2019). These findings emphasize the importance of regularly maintaining forest restoration treatments at broad scales following initial managed wildfire or prescribed fire, including repeated treatments or managed fire within windows when prior disturbance is most likely to constrain severity. 

#### **Limitations and future directions** 

Our analysis focused on event-level severity patterns and did not explicitly model fine-scale vegetation recovery, fuel composition, or suppression activities, all of which can influence reburn outcomes. In particular, we did not directly quantify post-fire ecosystem transformation. Exploratory analyses suggested that some 

reburn areas experienced reduced forest cover relative to the initial fire, consistent with potential transitions to shrubland or grassland. Although we included forest cover covariates to partially account for this variation, these variables primarily served to standardize comparisons, improve interpretability of CBI, and improve robustness of inference, rather than fully capturing dynamic vegetation change or interpreting forest cover as a primary ecological driver. Another limitation is that both field-based CBI and remotely sensed severity metrics (e.g., dNBR) can be inconsistent in reburned areas following high-severity fire (Saberi and Harvey 2023), potentially leading to underestimation of reburn severity in such contexts. However, highseverity initial fires were absent in our dataset, due to deliberately removing fire events with less than 20% of forest, limiting the influence of this bias. Finally, our event-level weather variables necessarily simplify the temporal variability experienced during individual fires. Although daily weather conditions varied within fires (Table S1), we summarized these conditions using event-level means because our response variable (mean event-level CBI) was likewise aggregated to the event level. Consequently, short-duration periods of extreme fire weather associated with rapid fire growth may be underrepresented. 

Despite these limitations, the consistency of patterns across fire events, climate gradients, and ecoregions suggests that the primary results, particularly the interacting effects of fire history and atmospheric drying, are robust. Future work integrating post-fire vegetation trajectories, fuel dynamics, alternative severity metrics, and daily fire progression with temporally explicit weather data could more directly resolve the mechanisms underlying reburn severity and improve predictions of ecosystem change. Importantly, lower reburn severity should not necessarily be interpreted as ecosystem resilience, as repeated burning may still drive long-term compositional or structural change even when subsequent severity is moderated. 

### **Conclusions** 

Reburn severity across western U.S. forests is governed by interacting effects of fire history, time since fire, and atmospheric drying, rather than by prior fire alone. While reburns are typically less severe than non-reburn areas in marginal predictions, this effect weakens or reverses under conditions of prolonged fuel recovery and high atmospheric drying. Rather than a single dominant pathway, reburn severity reflects shifting constraints between fuels and climate. As climate change intensifies fire weather and alters vegetation recovery, these interactions will play an increasingly important role in shaping fire behavior and ecosystem outcomes across western forests.
