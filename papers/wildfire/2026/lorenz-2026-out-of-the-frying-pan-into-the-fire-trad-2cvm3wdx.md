# Out of the frying pan, into the fire? Tradeoffs between drought adaptation and wildfire risk under cropland loss and urbanization in the western United States

## Abstract

Abstract The western United States faces two escalating, co-occurring hazards in the form of persistent water scarcity (drought) and increasingly frequent wildfires in places humans live and recreate. Drought adaptation has emphasized water savings from cropland retirement, while wildfire adaptation has emphasized the fire risks of urbanization. By overlooking what retired cropland becomes and what development replaces, neither has fully examined cropland's role as a landscape-scale buffer to wildfire in the western US. We demonstrate this buffering capacity across 11 states and 39 years of data, showing that cropland is less likely to burn than other vegetation classes. Within 477 watersheds where cropland has interacted with wildfire in the western US, approximately 4.29 million hectares of cropland was lost between 1985 and 2024. Cropland loss in most watersheds (77%) was consistent with fallowing transitions to more burnable herbaceous and scrubland vegetation across the Pacific Northwest, Inland Northwest, Intermountain West, and Great Basin. In a smaller portion of watersheds (19%), losses occurred through cropland-to-developed transitions clustered around major metropolitan areas in California, Idaho, Oregon, Washington, Utah, and Colorado, where development simultaneously removes buffering capacity and adds ignition sources. The remaining watersheds saw cropland transitions to wetlands and forests. Results highlight emerging tradeoffs between drought adaptation and wildfire risk in the western US, with retirement-driven fallowing and development-driven conversion reshaping where wildfires stop at landscape-scales. Integrated planning tools that evaluate water use savings and wildfire risks jointly, and expanded agricultural conservation programs that incorporate fire-buffering value, can help manage tradeoffs.
