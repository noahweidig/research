# Holocene ecosystem succession and fire regime evolution at Tannersville Bog, northeastern United States

## Abstract

Abstract. Northern peatlands are among the largest long-term terrestrial carbon reservoirs on Earth, with carbon accumulation jointly regulated by climate, hydrological conditions, and vegetation composition. Future climate warming is expected to accelerate the transition from lakes to peatlands while increasing the likelihood of peatland fires, which can rapidly release carbon accumulated for millennia. However, our understanding of how fire and peatland ecosystem development co-evolve over millennial timescales remains poorly understood. Tannersville Bog (TANB), a temperate boreal peatland in northeastern Pennsylvania, provides an ideal natural laboratory for investigating how northern peatlands are shaped and affected by fire, post-glacial ecosystem succession, and climate. Here, we integrate pyrogenic polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (pyroPAHs), n-alkane biomarkers, and sediment composition analyses to reconstruct the Holocene fire and environmental changes of TANB. The combined proxy records identify four major stages of ecosystem succession, from a shallow lake to a rich fen, a fen–bog transitional system, and ultimately a Sphagnum-dominated bog. Correspondingly, multivariate classification of PAH assemblages identifies four distinct fire regimes, which we link to vegetation and peatland-development proxies through ordination analyses. These fire regimes evolved alongside ecosystem succession, reflecting shifts in fuel composition, combustion processes, and fire-source characteristics. The lake-to-peatland transition also reveals that changing depositional and preservation conditions influenced PAH distributions without obscuring major fire-regime transitions. Our results demonstrate that long-term fire-regime evolution was jointly controlled by changes in vegetation composition, peatland hydrology, and basin development, highlighting strong mutual feedback between ecosystem succession and fire over millennial timescales.

Introduction** Northern peatlands cover only ~3 % of the Earth's land surface yet store nearly one-third of the global soil carbon pool, making them one of the largest long-term terrestrial carbon reservoirs and a key component of the global carbon cycle (Loisel _et al._ , 2014; Le Quéré _et al._ , 2018; Loisel and Bunsen, 2020). Their carbon storage capacity is regulated by primary production and organic matter 

- 35 decomposition (Clymo, 1984; Gorham, 1991), which are influenced by climate, hydrology, and vegetation composition (Rydin and Jeglum, 2013; Yu, Beilman and Jones, 2013). However, this balance can be abruptly disrupted by fire, as highlighted by recent extensive wildfires in Canada (Van Gerrevink _et al._ , 2026), which can release substantial amounts of stored carbon and greenhouse gases, with losses that may require centuries to millennia to recover through peat accumulation (Rodríguez 

- 40 Vásquez _et al._ , 2021; Zheng _et al._ , 2021; Wilkinson _et al._ , 2023; VanderRoest _et al._ , 2024). Under future climate warming, continued peatland development, declining water tables (Salimi and Scholz, 2021; Chaudhary _et al._ , 2026), and increasing lightning activity are expected to elevate peatland fire risk in northern regions (Xiao and Zhuang, 2007). Despite the importance of these interactions, the mechanisms linking ecosystem succession, fire regimes, and carbon dynamics over millennial 

- 45 timescales remain poorly understood. Most northern peatlands originated from post-glacial lakes and gradually evolved through shallow-lake, marsh, fen, and bog stages as organic matter accumulated and hydrological conditions changed (Tuittila _et al._ , 2013; Brosius _et al._ , 2021). This process of lake ontogeny fundamentally reshapes groundwater influence, vegetation composition, nutrient availability, and carbon accumulation, ultimately 

- 50 determining the long-term carbon sequestration capacity of peatlands (Schuller and Sullivan, 2024; Folhas _et al._ , 2025; Gao _et al._ , 2025). However, peatlands are highly dynamic systems that often undergo nonlinear transitions in both ecosystem structure and function (Belyea and Baird, 2006; Yu, 2006). Understanding these long-term ecological transitions is increasingly important because climate warming is expected to influence peatland hydrology and ecosystem structure, thereby affecting future 

- 55 carbon–climate feedback. Further, fire is not only a response to ecosystem change, but also an important driver of ecosystem succession (Clark‐Wolf _et al._ , 2023; Bonk _et al._ , 2026). As peatlands evolve from lakes to fens and bogs, changes in vegetation composition, hydrology, and peat accumulation modify fuel availability, combustion conditions, and ecosystem resilience (Van Bellen _et_

### **5. Conclusions** 

This multi-proxy reconstruction from TANB provides new insight into the long-term interactions among ecosystem succession, and fire-regime evolution in the northeastern Appalachian region during the Holocene. By integrating PAH molecular compositions with _n_ -alkane biomarkers, and sediment 1135 composition analysis, we illustrate that changes in fire regimes were closely coupled to ecosystem succession rather than responding directly to climate alone. 

● Four major stages of ecosystem development were identified, representing the transition from a shallow lake to a rich fen, a fen–bog transitional stage, and finally a _Sphagnum_ - dominated bog. Progressive changes in vegetation composition, hydrology, and terrestrial 1140 organic matter input fundamentally altered fuel availability and combustion environments throughout the Holocene. ● PAH assemblages reveal four distinct fire regimes characterized by different combustion conditions, fuel composition, and fire source areas rather than simply variations in fire magnitude. High-temperature, woody-fuel combustion dominated the early Holocene under 1145 conifer-rich landscapes, whereas mixed-fuel burning became increasingly important during rich-fen development.
