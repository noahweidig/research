# Timber harvesting and wildfires drive long-term modification in forest structure of Nothofagus pumilio: resilience characterization based on disturbance legacies

## Abstract

Abstract Background Forests are intrinsically resilient to natural disturbances but can be vulnerable to anthropogenic impacts (e.g. fires and harvesting), especially in high latitude temperate ecosystems. Changes in the natural dynamic pathways can lead to changes in the ecosystem structure and function, where legacies left after disturbances can play a major role in recovery. The objective was to characterize the resilience of different natural forest types in Tierra del Fuego, Argentina ( Nothofagus pumilio, N. antarctica , and mixed) based on disturbance legacies and long-term natural recovery of the forest structure after human impacts. Results Forest structure values significantly decreased in impacted stands compared to non-impacted areas (F between 13.50 to 25.23). Some woody legacies increased after impacts (e.g. snags, F = 2.12–3.20) while others tend to decrease (e.g. coarse-woody-debris, F = 3.49–3.90) in the long-term. Regeneration values generally decreased, depending on the impact type (e.g. wildfire decrease regeneration occurrence, F = 1.63). The impact severity changed according to impact type (wildfire > harvesting), where negative synergies were found in multivariate analyses for each forest type. Some variables presented greater changes across the impact gradients, where some thresholds can be used to determine points of no return in the forest recovery, e.g. Nothofagus pumilio was less resilient to the combination of impacts (wildfire and harvesting) than N. antarctica forests. Conclusions Natural landscapes have gradients related to forest structure from closed Nothofagus pumilio to open N. antarctica forests and grasslands. The human derived impacts (e.g. harvesting and wildfires) significantly modified the forest structure, but legacies remain after impacts according to the type and magnitude of these impacts. Wildfire and harvesting generate negative synergies, being more severe in N. pumilio forests than in stands with N. antarctica (mixed or pure forests). Indexes based on forest structure, legacies and regeneration values allow us to identify potential ecological thresholds related to these human impacts and the generation of hybrid or novel ecosystems linked to ecological and economic degradation. These findings can be allowed to design more efficient long-term monitoring to implement better adaptive management.

### **Abstract** 

**Background** Natural forests are intrinsically resilient to natural disturbances but can be vulnerable to anthropogenic impacts (e.g. wildfires and harvesting). Changes in natural dynamic pathways can modify the ecosystem structure and function, where legacies left after disturbances can play a major role in recovery. The objective was to characterize the resilience of different natural forest types in Tierra del Fuego, Argentina ( _Nothofagus pumilio_ , _N. antarctica_ , and mixed) based on disturbance legacies and natural recovery of the forest structure after 60 years of anthropogenic impacts. 

**Methods** Study was conducted across a 300-ha landscape located in Tierra del Fuego (Argentina) where 130 stands were sampled, with forest structure, woody legacies and regeneration cover quantified. Generalized Least Squares models, indexes and Principal Component Analyses were used to compare forest types and determine thresholds in the recovery patterns after 60 years of the impacts (wildlife and harvesting). 

**Results** Forest structure values significantly decreased in impacted stands compared to non-impacted treatments. Some woody legacies increased after impacts (e.g. snags) while others tend to decrease (e.g. coarse-woody-debris). Regeneration cover generally decreased but it was associated with the impact type (e.g. wildfire was more aggressive than harvesting). The impact severity also modified the recovery values (wildfire > harvesting), where negative synergies were found in multivariate analyses. Some variables presented greater changes across the impact gradients, where some recovery thresholds can be identified and can be used to determine points of no return in forest recovery, e.g. _Nothofagus pumilio_ was less resilient to the combination of impacts (wildfire and harvesting) than stands with _N. antarctica_ trees. 

**Conclusions** Natural landscapes presented gradients in their forest structure from closed _Nothofagus pumilio_ to open _N. antarctica_ forests and grasslands. Human related impacts (e.g. wildfires and harvesting) significantly modified forest structure and legacies of the stands, which varied according to the type and magnitude of these impacts. Wildfire and harvesting generated negative synergies, being more severe in _N. pumilio_ forests than in stands with _N. antarctica_ trees. Indexes based on forest structure, legacies and regeneration values allow us to identify potential recovery thresholds related to human impacts after 60 years of the impacts (wildlife and harvesting). These findings can be allowed to design more efficient long-term monitoring to implement better adaptive management.

### **Introduction** 

Natural forests can maintain ecosystem functioning over time a concept formally defined as temporal ecological stability—which has emerged as a primary focus in both theoretical and empirical research in forest ecology and management (Schnabel et al. 2021; Qiao et al. 2022, 2023; Shan et al. 2025). This ecological stability of the natural ecosystems can be affected across the diverse spatial scales and latitudes (Wang et al. 2019; Gonzalez et al. 2020). The main threats include natural environmental changes and different human pressures (Dieler et al. 2017; Pandey et al. 2023; Chaves et al. 2023; Fajardo et al. 2024), which can significantly diminish the recovery capacity in combination with external factors (Gauthier et al. 2015; Boucher et al. 2018). 

Over the past century, human activities have become the primary driver of change in native forests of Southern Patagonia (Martínez Pastur et al. 2021a; Peri et al. 2022), where main economic activities alter natural vegetation cover at the landscape level (Soler et al. 2022). Furthermore, anthropogenic disturbances (e.g. wildfires and harvesting) also alter forest structure, soil properties, key ecological processes, and floristic composition (Hudson and Alcántara-Ayala 2006; Martínez Pastur et al. 2022). Wildfires in Southern Patagonia are mostly related to human activities and follow a different ecological pathway from that in Northern Patagonia (e.g. Kitzberger et al. 2016, 2025; Gowda et al. 2026). Therefore, alternative forest management approaches must be developed specifically for Southern Patagonia to mitigate the increased risks of disturbances, such as adaptive forest management based on the natural forest resilience (Triviño et al. 2023). Here, we understand resilience as the ability of natural forests to reorganize after external pressures while recovering or maintaining the same functions, structures, identities, and feedback, as well as their synergistic effects (Walker et al. 2004; Messier et al. 2019). Changes in natural dynamic pathways and disturbance regimes (e.g. frequency and severity of disturbances) threaten forest resilience and ecosystem services over time (Seidl and Rammer 2017; Forzieri et al. 2022). If disturbances surpass the recovery thresholds, they may impair the process of recovery (e.g. forest structure, composition, and function), modify the resilience characteristics of the natural ecosystems (Nikinmaa et al. 2020), and trigger successional shifts toward altering the different forest types and openlands (Young et al. 2019; Coop et al. 2020; Cerioni et al. 2024). 

Natural disturbances enhance habitat (e.g. recruitment of early-stage species) and resources (e.g. deadwood) for different species (Kuuluvainen 2002; Liu 2025), while 

human-related disturbances might endanger the ecosystem recovery (Lindenmayer et al. 2016; Senf and Seidl 2021). Adopting a holistic perspective to account for diverse disturbance types is essential for forest sustainability (Triviño et al. 2023; Poorter et al. 2023). Changes in natural dynamic pathway can threat forest resilience and can lead to changes in ecosystem structure and function (Coop et al. 2020; Triviño et al. 2023; El-Beltagi et al. 2025). For example, low-resilient forest ecosystems could easily be tipped into an alternative irreversible state (Boers et al. 2017; Dakos et al. 2019). These permanent changes can lead to generate novel ecosystems, which will present different tree species assemblages, structure and functions distinct from original landscapes (Hobbs et al. 2006; Radeloff et al. 2015; Ramírez et al. 2025). 

Managers and decision makers must identify temporal recovery threshold points between stable stages (Peri et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023), where forest landscapes usually reflect the consequences of natural and human disturbances (Yeboah and Chen 2016; Senf and Seidl 2018). The potential for disturbance regime shifts between alternative stable states or new states remains unknown, making identifying controls on ecosystem resilience a global concern. Recent research indicates that many regions present declining vegetation resilience (Feng et al. 2021; Smith et al. 2022; Forzieri et al. 2022), however, the drivers altering spatial heterogeneity in resilience and its trends remain unconstrained (Smith and Boers 2023). 

Promoting forest resilience is key to adapting to global change (Messier et al. 2016) and is frequently mentioned as one of the main goals of forest management and restoration (Rist and Moen 2013; Seidl et al. 2016; Chaves et al. 2024). Most temperate forest ecosystems can recover from major disruptions within decades (Rebertus et al. 1997; Gea-Izquierdo et al. 2004; Martínez Pastur et al. 2021a) with the help of some natural legacies (e.g. isolated patches of trees, coarse-woody-debris, seeds) (Hlásny et al. 2021). These legacies in the impacted forests can lead to positive or negative synergies (Ahrens et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2026). The structure of disturbed landscapes, including different patterns of disturbance legacies (Franklin et al. 2002), is an important driver of forest vulnerability and resilience to future stressors (Bače et al. 2015; Johnstone et al. 2016; Senf and Seidl 2018). However, some effects of forest disturbance can persist and alter the original forest understory (Bače et al. 2017; Cerioni et al. 2024), modifying forest stand structure (Martínez Pastur et al. 2021a, 2022), and increasing the susceptibility to subsequent disturbances (Janda et al. 2017; Zhu et al. 2026). 

The pressures over natural forests can be abiotic (e.g. extreme climate events) or biotic (e.g. insect outbreaks)

with synergistic effects (Messier et al. 2019; Triviño et al. 2023). To understand how natural forests will respond to future conditions, it is necessary to determine the recovery thresholds and quantify the synergic interactions between natural and human impacts (Ummenhofer and Meehl 2017; Liu 2025). Declined forest resilience after severe disturbances has been reported in temperate forests around the world (Bowd et al. 2023; Turner et al. 2019; Cerioni et al. 2024). In Southern Patagonian forests, natural regeneration occurred in a great range of disturbance variability (Frangi et al. 2015; Peri et al. 2017, 2022), which is generally characterized by frequent low-severity gap-scale events (Rebertus and Veblen 1993; Gea-Izquierdo et al. 2004; Martínez Pastur et al. 2011, 2023), and periodic disturbances caused by windstorms or insect outbreaks (Rebertus et al. 1997; Martínez Pastur et al. 2024). 

The lack of recovery in natural forests is closely related to the magnitude of the anthropic impacts (Peri et al. 2022), e.g. historical land use and forest management practices that can affect disturbance legacies such as organisms and biologically driven patterns that persist following disturbance (Seidl et al. 2014; Johnstone et al. 2016; Cerioni et al. 2024). Besides, anthropogenic impacts modify the assemblage of plant species in the managed landscapes of Southern Patagonia by modifying the forest structure and the inclusion of exotic species (e.g. wide naturalized exotic species, pastures) into the natural landscapes (Huertas Herrera et al. 2018; Lencinas et al. 2019; Alonso et al. 2020; Chaves et al. 2024). At these latitudes, native forests lack natural adaptations to other disturbance agents, such as wildfires (Alauzis et al. 2004; Cafourek et al. 2021; Ruggirello et al. 2023) and invasive species (e.g. _Castor canadensis_ ) (Speziale and Ezcurra 2011; Martínez Pastur et al. 2021b; Rosas et al. 2024). These disturbances disrupt dynamic pathways and lead to the development of novel ecosystems (e.g. meadows and grasslands dominated by plant exotic species) (Pauchard and Alaback 2004; Rauber et al. 2013; Lencinas et al. 2024), which is the main gap knowledge necessary to improve better forest management and conservation practices. In this context, the objective of this study was to characterize the resilience and recovery thresholds of different natural forest types ( _Nothofagus pumilio_ (Poepp. et Endl.) Krasser, _N. antarctica_ (G. Forst.) Oerst., and mixed) based on disturbance legacies after 60 years of impacts (wildfires and harvesting). We want to answer the following questions: (i) Can the forest structure characteristics of non-impacted areas be recovered 60 years after impacts? (ii) Do impact type (wildfire and harvesting) and their combination generate negative synergies in the natural forest recovery? (iii) Is it possible 

to detect recovery thresholds for these impacts (wildfires and harvesting) that can be used to improve management and conservation proposals? 

### **Methods** 

#### **Study area** 

Sampling was conducted at La Catalana ranch (− 54.1946 S, − 67.3224 W) located in Tierra del Fuego (Argentina) at 50–100 m a.s.l. and 15 km away from seashores (Fig. 1A). This ranch manages 10,339 ha for cattle breeding (4290 ha of natural grasslands, 5926 ha of native forests, and 123 ha of degraded forests). Natural forests are dominated by _Nothofagus antarctica_ (75%) and _N. pumilio_ (20%), but also, it is possible to find mixed stands of these two species (5%). Both tree species naturally regenerated by seeds following simple gap dynamics, however, _N. antarctica_ can also resprout from roots after disturbances (Cafourek et al. 2021; Martínez Pastur et al. 2023). Usually, these tree species grow in pure stands, but in the ecotone areas can occur together as an intermediate stage, e.g. _N. pumilio_ advance over _N. antarctica_ (Soler et al. 2022). In the studied area the mixed stands presented on average 71% of basal area of _N. antarctica_ and 29% of _N. pumilio_ trees. We selected a forested landscape with a well-known history of human related impacts and uses, covering 300 ha that was partially used for timber harvesting and firewood extraction during 1960–1966, when wildfire affected the area. Based on data obtained during the field work, we reconstruct the vegetation types before (Fig. 1B) and after the impacts (Fig. 1C) into a Geographical Information System (GIS). After the impacts, these paddocks were mainly used for sheep grazing for 50 years, followed by land abandonment and conversion to cattle breeding during the last decade.

The gap dynamics predominates in this forest type, allowing the recruitment of isolated _N. pumilio_ trees in the disturbed forests, generating on-time mixed stands of both tree species (MIX) (Soliani et al. 2021; Martínez Pastur et al. 2023; Zamorano-Elgueta and BecerraRodas 2025). _Nothofagus pumilio_ trees grow faster and reach higher heights than _N. antarctica_ trees, leading to pure _N. pumilio_ stands (NP) due to mortality of _N. antarctica_ trees by crown competition or eco-physiological adaptations (Peri et al. 2009; Gyenge et al. 2011; Martínez Pastur et al. 2021a; Fajardo et al. 2024). The described succession model can follow a natural inverse pathway when the environmental conditions change (e.g. extreme climate events or climate change in the long-term) (Rebertus and Veblen 1993; Toro Manríquez et al. 2022).

timber ( _Nothofagus pumilio_ forests) and firewood ( _N. antarctica_ and mixed forests), where intensity is usually related to site quality, wood quality, and stand accessibility. _Nothofagus antarctica_ forests are frequently lightly harvested (remnant basal area BA > 50%), in both closed and open forests (ONA-H and CNA-H). It is possible that harvested forests recover on time the original values of natural forests, but this depends on the occurrence of natural regeneration, e.g. CNA can be converted into ONA stands if natural regeneration fails (Cafourek et al. 2021; Martínez Pastur et al. 2021b). The same pathway occurred for mixed and _N. pumilio_ forests, both for light (NP-LH, remnant BA > 50%) and heavy harvesting (NP-HH, remnant BA < 50%) (Peri et al. 2009; Toro Manríquez et al. 2022). Harvesting in mixed forests does not present a significant difference between both tree species (e.g. in our study 50–50% of basal area for both tree species), where target trees were selected by the tree size (< 30 cm diameter at breast height) to obtain firewood products. 

- (iii) The influence of wildfires: Some harvested forests were affected by wildfires (ONA-H+F, MIX-H+F, NP-LH-F, NP-HH+F). The impacted stands can recover on time or can be degraded due to natural regeneration failure leading to openlands dominated by grasslands and shrublands (Ruggirello et al. 2023, 2025). 

Each plot was sampled through a 25 m transect from south to north of the geographic point center defined by the grid. We characterized each plot by their forest structure, and forest floor cover following Rosas et al. (2024). Forest structure was measured by one plot in the center of the transect using the point sampling method (BAF = 1 to 4) (Bitterlich 1984), obtaining basal area of alive (BA, m<sup>2</sup> ha<sup>−1</sup> ) and dead trees (BA-D,  m<sup>2</sup> ha<sup>−1</sup> ) with a Criterion RD-1000 (Laser Technology, USA) and dominant height (DH, m) by the two tallest trees, using a TruPulse 200 (Laser Technology, USA). With these inputs we calculated total volume of live (TV,  m<sup>3</sup> ha<sup>−1</sup> ) and dead trees (TV-D,  m<sup>3</sup> ha<sup>−1</sup> ) using equations proposed by Lencinas et al. (2002) and Martínez Pastur et al. (2002). Hemispherical photos were taken at the center of each transect at 1.5 m above ground level with an 8-mm fisheye lens (Sigma, Japan) mounted on a 35 mm full-frame digital camera (Nikon, Japan), and were analyzed with Gap Light Analyzer software v.2.0 (Frazer et al. 2001) to define overstory cover (CC, %) and relative leaf area index (LAI) using input variables proposed by Martínez Pastur et al. (2011). To characterize forest floor cover, we used the point-intercept method (Levy and Madden 1933) with 50 intercept points (every 0.5 m) along each transect. At each point, we recorded coarse-woody-debris (CWD) and tree regeneration (REG), which allowed us to calculate their cover (CWD-C and REG-C, %) based on the number of hits along the transect. Diameter of each CWD was also measured, allowing to calculate the volume per hectare (CWD-V) following Martínez Pastur et al. (2021a) and Chaves et al. (2023). 

#### **Sampling design and data collection** 

A total of 130 plots were measured in the study site (300 ha in total), using a systematic grid of 150 × 150 m across forestlands and openlands (Fig. 1C). A total of 41 plots corresponded to non-impacted environments: _Nothofagus pumilio_ forests (NP, 6 plots), mixed _N. pumilio_ and _N. antarctica_ forests (MIX, 5 plots), closed _N. antarctica_ forests (CNA, 9 plots), open _N. antarctica_ forests (ONA, 16 plots) and grasslands (G, 5 plots). The other plots corresponded to different combinations of forest types and impacts: _N. pumilio_ forests with light harvesting (NP-LH, 9 plots), _N. pumilio_ forests with heavy harvesting (NP-HH, 5 plots), _N. pumilio_ forests with light harvesting and wildfires (NP-LH+F, 7 plots), _N. pumilio_ forests with heavy harvesting and wildfires (NPHH+F, 26 plots), mixed forests with harvesting and wildfires (MIX-H+F, 6 plots), close _N. antarctica_ forests with harvesting (CNA-H, 10 plots), open _N. antarctica_ forests with harvesting (ONA-H, 18 plots), and open _N. antarctica_ forests with harvesting and wildfires (ONA-H+F, 8 plots). 

#### **Statistical analysis** 

Differences among non-impacted (NP, MIX, CNA, ONA, G) and impacted (NP-LH, NP-HH, NP-LH+F, NP-HH+F, MIX-H+F, CNA-H, ONA-H, ONA-H+F) vegetation types were analyzed using Generalized Least Squares (GLS) models. These models were used to analyze: (i) forest structure values (DH, CC, LAI, BA, TV), and (ii) dead trees, coarse-woody-debris, and tree regeneration cover (BA-D, TV-D, CWD-C, CWD-V, REG-C). The GLS approach was chosen to evaluate variance heterogeneity (heteroscedasticity) among treatments. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means with Tukey’s adjustment at _p_ < 0.05. These analyses were conducted using the nlme (Pinheiro et al. 2023), emmeans (Lenth 2023), and multcomp (Hothorn et al. 2008) packages in R software (R Core Team 2023). Besides, we develop two indexes to compare the nonimpacted and impacted vegetation types: (i) one based on forest structure (variables of Table 1), and (ii) another based on dead trees, coarse-woody-debris and tree regeneration cover (variables of Table 2). ### **Discussion** 

The original landscapes within the study area alternate natural forestlands and openlands (Fig. 1A), where long-term succession dynamics involve the replacement among vegetation types: (i) _Nothofagus pumilio_ trees intrude into _N. antarctica_ forests, forming mixed stands which over time can be derived into pure _N. pumilio_ stands; and (ii) _N. antarctica_ forests advance into openlands, forming open _N. antarctica_ forests which over time can be derived into closed stands. This is consistent with those theoretical models informed by these temperate ecosystems in Southern Patagonia (Rebertus and Veblen 1993; Gutiérrez 1994; Frangi et al. 2015). The natural vegetation types presented different stand conditions with gradients in their productivity (e.g. dominant height), occupancy (e.g. basal area or total volume), and light availability at the understory level (e.g. crown cover and leaf area index) from closed _N. pumilio_ forests to grasslands. The species assemblage (e.g. plants, insects and birds) (Lencinas et al. 2024; Benítez et al. 2024; Bottan et al. 2025) and natural cycles (e.g. decomposition and nutrient immobilization) (Toledo et al. 2024, 2025) varied across these gradients, as well as the forest resistance to species invasion and resilience to face different types of impacts (Pérez Flores et al. 2019; Mina et al. 2022; Rosas et al. 2024; El-Beltagi et al. 2025). In this study, the magnitude and impact types after 60 years impacts greatly modified the studied forest structure values (Table 1), where productivity and occupancy decreased, and light availability increased at the understory level, as were widely described before (Martínez Pastur et al. 2011, 2021a; Peri et al. 2017, 2022; Soler et al. 2022; Zamorano-Elgueta and Becerra-Rodas 2025). The harvesting impacts presented higher recovery values compared to wildfires after 60 years impacts, where greater changes in forest structure were observed when both impacts occurred together (wildfires affecting harvested stands). The harvested stands did not fully recover the original forest structure values, however, presented the expected forest structure after 60 years of harvesting impacts (e.g. Martínez Pastur et al. 2000). In harvesting proposals in _Nothofagus_ forests emulating natural dynamics, e.g. gap dynamics or small windthrow events, half of the overstory remains as shelterwood for natural regeneration (Peri et al. 2022; Huertas Herrera et al. 2023; Martínez Pastur et al. 2024). However, wildfires usually affect a greater portion of the overstory, removing some critical components for fully forest recovery (e.g. seed and seedling banks, or living roots for sprouts) (Ruggirello et al. 2023, 2025; Barberá et al. 2025; Kitzberger et al. 2025). In this context, wildfire areas showed a delay in the 

recovery compared to only harvested areas according to the expected natural dynamic model (e.g. Martínez Pastur et al. 2000). For this, many authors suggest that these forest ecosystems, both _N. pumilio_ and _N. antarctica_ in Southern Patagonia, are not prepared to recover after this kind of impact, promoting the shift from forestland to openlands (Veblen et al. 2011; Martínez Pastur et al. 2021a; Hernández-Moreno et al. 2023). 

The impacts generated by harvesting and wildfire leave behind some legacies (e.g. snags and coarsewoody-debris). Deadwood helps to maintain in-situ conservation for many species (e.g. from fungi to birds), offering shelter for natural regeneration, and conserving the natural nutrient cycling through decomposition on time (Bradford et al. 2023; Korhonen et al. 2024; Chang et al. 2024). In our study, the occupancy and volume of snags (BA-D and TV-D) presented few significant differences among natural forested environments. _Nothofagus_ trees presented low tolerance to changes in the natural canopy stand conditions, and crown dieback and mortality usually occur after harvesting (Ruel and Gardiner 2019; Rodríguez-Souilla et al. 2023; Martínez Pastur et al. 2024), increasing snags, e.g. BA-D and TV-D in harvested closed stands (NP-LH, NP-HH, and CNA-H). Wildfires tend to decrease snags, where treatments with wildfires despite harvesting decrease the snag proportion in the stands. The decrease in woody biomass is an expected impact of forest wildfires (Keith et al. 2014; Magerl et al. 2023). Coarse-woody-debris (cover and volume) in the natural stands greatly varied among forested environments (NP > MIX > CAN > ONA), and it is directly related to the tree volume of the stands. Cover and volume of CWD decreased in the impacted stands after 60 years impacts, mainly due to timber and firewood extraction (Table 2). Good-quality _N. pumilio_ forests were largely used for timber in sawmill industry; besides, the low-quality _N. pumilio_ wood and _N. antarctica_ trees were extensively used for firewood until 1970 when natural gas pipes arrived at large cities (Rio Grande and Ushuaia cities in Tierra del Fuego, Argentina) (Gea-Izquierdo et al. 2004). In general, our results showed a decrease in woody legacies (snags and coarsewoody-debris) after 60 years impacts, reducing the recovery capacity of the impacted stands, increasing ecological heterogeneity, and modifying post-disturbance pathways (Seidl et al. 2014; Jönsson et al. 2023; Cerioni et al. 2024). 

Natural regeneration is abundant in Southern Patagonian _Nothofagus_ forests and occurs abundantly after disturbances (Gutiérrez 1994; Gea-Izquierdo et al. 2004;

Page 10 of 18 

Rodríguez-Souilla et al. 2023, 2024), allowing for a tree crown recovery in a few decades (Rebertus et al. 1997; Soler et al. 2022; Chaves et al. 2024). In our study, it is possible to find a clear correlation between crown cover (Table 1) and regeneration cover (Table 2), where _N. pumilio_ is usually greater than _N. antarctica_ (Martínez Pastur et al. 2011; Bahamonde et al. 2018; Ruggirello et al. 2023). The decrease in regeneration cover in _N. pumilio_ harvested stands (NP-LH and NP-HH) was due to overstory cover recovery after cuttings, which decreased in stands impacted by wildfires (NP-LH+F). Failure of natural regeneration was detected in heavily impacted stands with wildfire after 60 years impacts (e.g. NP-HH+F). This lack of adaptation of _N. pumilio_ stands was widely described in the literature and is mainly related to limitations in seeding and recruitment processes, especially at Southern Patagonian forests (Mestre et al. 2022; Urretavizcaya et al. 2022; Ruggirello et al. 2025). Mixed and pure _N. antarctica_ forests presented a similar pattern, where regeneration cover is related to overstory; however, it was lower compared to _N. pumilio_ forests. Harvested closed _N. antarctica_ forests (CNA-H) showed a remarkable decrease in the regeneration cover and can be related to negative synergies with understory development after cuttings and the potential influence of livestock (browsing and trampling) (Martínez Pastur et al. 2022; Huertas Herrera et al. 2023; Bottan et al. 2025; Zamorano-Elgueta and Becerra-Rodas 2025). Despite these differences, the impacted stands presented abundant natural regeneration in their understory, indicating a natural recovery process that can be delayed for the magnitude and type of impacts (Peri et al. 2017; Cafourek et al. 2021), or may generate novel ecosystems if the impacts are maintained in the long-term (Frangi et al. 2015; Martínez Pastur et al. 2021a; Peri et al. 2022; Ramírez et al. 2025). Our study focused on 60-years-after-impacts; however, it may take hundreds of years to recover the full forest structure values (e.g. see Martínez Pastur et al. 2000). 

The different forest types exhibit significant differences in the recovery of forest structure, legacies, and regeneration patterns according to magnitude and impact type after 60 years of disturbance (Fig. 3). Natural forests without impacts occupy an intermediate position between control stands and openlands. Those stands with a lack of legacies and regeneration failure are closer to openlands, while those stands that recover the studied values are closer to natural stands. Chaves et al. (2024) determine that some harvested _N. pumilio_ stands in Southern Patagonia amplify the forest structure differences with natural forests until 7–9 years after harvesting, and 

then the values quickly recover during the next decades. In other study sites, Pérez Flores et al. (2019) found that understory recovers similar characteristics to primary forests after at least 50 years-after-harvesting. However, some _N. pumilio_ stands maintained the differences on time, which can be attributed to a lack of natural regeneration (Cavallero et al. 2015; Martínez Pastur et al. 2021a, 2021b). 

PCA for different forest types and changes due to wildfire and harvesting 60-years-after-impacts (Fig. 4) showed differences across natural gradients (e.g. from closed _N. pumilio_ to open _N. antarctica_ forests and grasslands). In these analyses, it was possible to determine some recovery thresholds between the 60 years-after recovery impacts and some loss of resilience of native forests according to expected natural dynamic models, e.g. heavy-impacted _N. pumilio_ stands with a combination of wildfires and harvesting. Different indicators are proposed to detect these recovery thresholds (a point of no return), however, detecting them in practice remains difficult (van de Leemput et al. 2018). For this, the proposed indices can be a robust identification of resilience indicators with enough resolution and easy taken field records (Drake and Griffen 2010; Dai et al. 2013). 

In _N. pumilio_ stands it is possible to determine whether light harvested stands (with or without wildfire impacts) recover some characteristics associated with forest structure after 60 years impacts, maintaining legacies, and increasing regeneration cover, that allowed them to reach the similar values of non-impacted forests. However, the combination of wildfires and harvesting generated permanent differences in the heavy impacted stands. These changes in the original characteristics were largely referenced in many studies (McRae et al. 2001; Lorente et al 2013; Bousfield et al. 2023) and can be explained due to the maintenance of drivers of change in the modified stands (e.g. understory layer that impedes natural regeneration, livestock impacts, etc.) (Martínez Pastur et al. 2022; Huertas Herrera et al. 2023; Zamorano-Elgueta and Becerra-Rodas 2025). These non-natural forest states can be reversed when these stressors are removed (e.g. closures or planting through active restoration proposals) (Peri et al. 2022; Werden et al. 2022; Cerioni et al. 2024). 

In _N. antarctica_ stands (mixed and pure forests), the impacts generate large gradients but with similarities with non-impacted forests. This is mainly due to this tree species occupying a large range of habitats (e.g. from dry to wet environments, using seeds and sprouts for their regeneration), being more resilient to the natural and human derived impacts (Piper and Zuniga-Feest 2012;

Fajardo et al. 2023). However, the combination of heavy harvesting and wildfires (ONA-H+F) presented greater differences, and are more vulnerable to permanent changes, generating openlands without forest cover in the middle or long-term (Valenzuela et al. 2016; Martínez Pastur et al. 2021a; Ruggirello et al. 2023). Mixed forests decreased the percentage of _N. antarctica_ trees after impacts (from 70–30 to 50–50% basal area of _N. pumilio_ and _N. antarctica_ ). These differences can be due to the harvesting target (e.g. size or quality of trees) or due to differences in the recovery strategies (e.g. seeds vs. root sprouting) (Toro Manríquez et al. 2022; Soler et al. 2022). 

Anthropogenic disturbances and climate change have increased the frequency of wildfires across most terrestrial ecosystems worldwide (Sommerfeld et al. 2018; Bowd et al. 2021; Triviño et al. 2023). Vegetation can change according to climate change and climate events (e.g. ENSO and SAM) (Montaño Bello and Villegas Bolaños 2025; Jia et al. 2026), e.g. changes in seed production, regeneration, growth, and crown dieback (Rodríguez-Souilla et al. 2023, 2024, 2025). Besides, cumulative impacts generated by wildfires and harvesting have expanded the extent of openlands in the landscape and generated more edge environments among the different vegetation types, promoting the formation of new environments, e.g. edges between _N. pumilio_ and openlands, and a gradient of crown closure in _N. pumilio_ forests due to different harvesting intensity (Fig. 1B). These new environments generate new natural dynamic pathways that were not found in natural landscapes, and that can derive in novel ecosystems with new species assemblages (Huertas Herrera et al. 2018; Lencinas et al. 2019; Benítez et al. 2022, 2025), e.g. primary forests significantly differed from secondary forests across the years after the impacts in Southern Patagonia (Alonso et al. 2020; Martínez Pastur et al. 2021a, 2022; Lencinas et al. 2024). 

As presented here, multiple high-severity impacts (e.g. wildfires and harvesting) within short intervals can produce novel conditions that exceed the adaptive forest resilience (Enright et al. 2015; Turner et al. 2019) and can deplete soil-stored seed banks or kill the plant species before seed production and maturation, and/ 

or resprouting failure (Fairman et al. 2019; Bowd et al. 2021). In this context, one major challenge of sustainable forest management and conservation lies in defining recovery thresholds of managed ecosystems (Peri et al. 2017, 2022), e.g. hybrid and novel ecosystems can be created due to lack of recovery of the managed or impacted stands (Frangi et al. 2015; Ramírez et al. 2025). Adaptive forest management and long-term monitoring must be implemented in the managed landscapes, reducing the frequency and magnitude of impacts to maintain the natural resilience of the natural environments. Besides, several studies have reported a decline in ecosystem functioning with latitude, as well as with broad-scale patterns of biodiversity (Tiegs et al. 2019; Qiao et al. 2023), suggesting that it is necessary to consider differences between Northern and Southern forest ecosystems. 

### **Conclusions** 

Natural landscapes present a gradient of stand conditions related to forest structure from closed _Nothofagus pumilio_ to open _N. antarctica_ forests and grasslands. The human derived impacts (e.g. wildfires and harvesting) significantly modified the forest structure, but legacies remain after impacts according to the type and magnitude of wildfires and harvesting. These impacts generate negative synergies, being more severe in _N. pumilio_ than _N. antarctica_ stands (mixed or pure forests). Indexes based on forest structure, legacies and regeneration cover allow us to identify potential recovery thresholds related to human impacts and the generation of hybrid or novel ecosystems linked to ecological and economic degradation. Our analysis focused on 60-years-after-impacts and did not examine the long-term recovery that can require hundreds of years. These findings can be allowed to design more efficient long-term monitoring to implement better adaptive management.
