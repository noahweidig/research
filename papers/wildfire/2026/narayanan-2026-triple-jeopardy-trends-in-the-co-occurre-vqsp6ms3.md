# Triple jeopardy: trends in the co-occurrence of extreme heat, drought, and PM2.5 across the conterminous United States

## Abstract

Abstract Compound climate hazards can produce risks exceeding those captured by single-hazard analyses, yet their joint behavior remains poorly characterized across the United States. Research has focused largely on drought-heat interactions, while growing evidence suggests other hazard combinations may increasingly shape population exposure. This study examines the co-occurrence of extreme heat, drought, and fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅) across the conterminous United States (CONUS) from 1991 to 2023, evaluating pairwise and triple interactions across multiple severity tiers. Rather than uniform intensification, results reveal a shift in the relative prominence of compound exposures. Heat-PM₂.₅ co-occurrence emerges as the most widespread and fastest-growing combination, averaging 22.7 days per summer at the mild tier, nearly three times the frequency of heat-drought, and increasing from 18.8 to 25.7 days per season (+36.8%) between the early (1991–2006) and late (2009–2023) periods. Significant monotonic increases occur across 64% of CONUS, compared with 0.3% showing decreases. Regional variation ranges from 6.8 days per season in the Western North Central region to 54.1 days in the Southeast. Heat-drought co-occurrence, by contrast, shows modest changes across most severity levels. Regional differences are pronounced in the South, exceeding 40 days per season, and the Northwest, where rapid increases coincide with expanding wildfire activity, although source-specific PM₂.₅ was not evaluated. Simultaneous co-occurrence of all three hazards remains rare, increasing from 0.02 to 0.10 days per season at the moderate tier against a 1991–2023 mean of 0.06 days. Despite this low absolute frequency, triple-hazard co-occurrence increases substantially in relative terms (+485% between periods), with significant increases across 18.1% of CONUS pixels. These findings indicate a reconfiguration of compound hazard exposure driven by the growing prominence of specific hazard combinations rather than uniform change, suggesting that frameworks centered on a limited set of hazard pairings may no longer capture dominant regional exposures.
