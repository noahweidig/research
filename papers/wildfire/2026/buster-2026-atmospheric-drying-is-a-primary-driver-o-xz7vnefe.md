# Atmospheric drying is a primary driver of past and future fire weather trends across the Western United States

## Abstract

Abstract There has been evidence of increasing fire activity in the United States since approximately the 1980s, now estimated to cause tens to hundreds of billions of dollars of damage per year. Weather is one of the key drivers of fire activity, with hot, dry, and windy weather commonly associated with high-risk conditions. We are still, however, developing an understanding of how these weather variables have contributed to historical trends in fire weather and whether these trends will continue. In this work, we investigate the impact of declining humidity in arid regions of the United States. We find that these drying trends have been the predominant contributor to historical increases in fire weather in the Western United States based on a common fire weather index, and that this signal is largely missing from downscaled weather projections based on Earth system models. Accordingly, a near-future (2020–2039) fire weather scenario derived from downscaled Earth System Model data produces little to no change in the frequency of extreme fire weather days across the Western United States. However, under a continued drying scenario, many regions in the West experience 20–70% increases in the annual occurrence of extreme fire weather days.

There has been evidence of increasing fire activity in the United States since approximately the 1980s, now estimated to cause tens to hundreds of billions of dollars of damage per year. Weatheris one ofthe key drivers of fire activity, with hot, dry, and windy weather commonly associated with high-risk conditions. Wildfires in the United States have become more common over the last few decades with the Western United States seeing upward trends in burn frequency, burn area, and destroyed buildings<sup>1,2</sup> . In a 2017 report, the direct monetary annualized costs, which include damages, losses, and fire management costs, were estimated to be between $71 billion and $348 billion in 2016 dollars<sup>3</sup> . The United States Congress Joint Economic Committee reported that diminished real estate value and exposure to wildfire smoke are even more costly than property damage alone, estimating total wildfire costs to be between $394 and $893 billion per year<sup>4</sup> . 

The factors driving these increases in fire risk are numerous and complex. To start, wildland fires are fueled by vegetation, and therefore changes in vegetation drive corresponding changesin wildfire regimes, with industrially managed forests being more likely to burn with high severity<sup>5</sup> and invasive grass species increasing fire occurrence by up to 230%<sup>6</sup> . However, without a human component, changes in vegetation would not drive changes in societal risk. Between 1945 and 2015, more than 330,000 buildings were added to fire-prone areas<sup>7</sup> . The addition of new buildings to wildfire hotspots has increased human exposure to fire risk, especially for low- and intermediate-density settlements in the wildland-urban interface<sup>7</sup> . Finally, there is substantial evidence that historical changes in weather and climate, specifically in the warming and drying of the atmosphere over the 

Western United States, have driven increases in fuel aridity, leading to increased burned area<sup>8,9</sup> . 

The phenomenon of atmospheric drying over land in the past several decades is well documented, especially for the arid Western United States with concentrations in certain regions of California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico, and Colorado<sup>9,10</sup> . Evidence of this trend can be seen in increasing vapor pressure deficit and decreasing vapor pressure in the fifth major global reanalysis produced by the European Centre for MediumRange Weather Forecasts (ERA5) and meteorological observation stations fromthe Integrated Surface Database<sup>10,11</sup> .Thereareseveralworkingtheories for the cause of this phenomenon based on atmospheric dynamics and moisture transport<sup>12</sup> and trends in terrestrial evapotranspiration<sup>13</sup> . The relevance of these trends with respect to fire weather has been superficially noted,althoughwithoutquantificationoftheimpactoncommonlyused fire weather indices or future projections of fire weather<sup>10</sup> . 

To estimate future changes in fire weather, many have turned to Earth system models (ESMs)<sup>14–16</sup> . Projections from ESMs such as those from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) are currently the best method to explore possible future fire weather regimes, but have noted limitations in their spatial resolution and physical representation of certain fire weather phenomena<sup>17</sup> . ## Results 

### Components of historical fire weather trends 

The first question motivating the following results is: how has the observed drying trend in the Western UnitedStates contributed tohistorical trends in changing fire weather? To answer this, we calculate the Canadian Fire Weather Index (FWI) using historical reanalysis inputs from ERA5 and Daymet and estimate the isolated contribution from each of the four FWI input variables: temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, and wind speed. Figure 1a shows a clear upward trend in FWI over the 1980–2019 reanalysis period in the Western United States that may have contributed to the increase in wildfires reported by others<sup>2</sup> . 

Perhaps more surprising, however, is the relative contribution of each of the weather variables to this overall trend. As seen in Fig. 1c, the largest contributortothehistoricalincreasesinFWIintheWesternUnitedStatesis the declining trend in relative humidity. Increasing temperatures do drive increases in FWI, as shown in Fig. 1b, but the FWI trends are largely not statistically significant despite the temperature trends themselves being statistically significant<sup>20</sup> . In many regions in the Western United States and especially the Southwest, the effect of declining relative humidity can be two to four times more powerful than the effect of increasing temperatures alone.Othershavepreviouslylinked increasingtemperaturestorealwildfire risk via their influenceon fuel moisture<sup>8</sup> , but this effect is much smaller than the direct drying effect from atmospheric humidity trends as measured by the FWI. Of course, changes in temperature also drive changes in relative humidity, but we see in Supplementary Fig. 1 that 60–97% of the relative humiditytrendsand67–84%oftheFWItrendsarecausedbyrealchangesin specific humidity, not changes in temperature driving down relative humidity. 

The effect from changes in precipitation shown in Fig. 1d is more spatially variable than the effect from temperature or relative humidity, but generally we see decreasing FWI from increasing precipitation in the North Great Plains and increasing FWI from declining precipitation in the Desert Southwest, although in the Desert Southwest this effect is still much smaller in magnitude than the effect from relative humidity. Last, in Fig. 1e, we see that the effect of changes in wind speed is smaller in magnitude than any of the other variables, although we can see a slightly positive increase in FWI from increasing wind speeds along a narrow vertical band in the Great Plains. Note that there is substantial uncertainty in historical wind speed trends<sup>21–23</sup> , and the results in Fig. 1e are of lower confidence than the other variables. 

Seasonal FWI trends based on reanalysis with individual variable contributions are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 through 5 and show similarrelativecontributionsof the fourindependentvariablestotheoverall FWI trend with minor differences depending on the region and season. 

The FWI algorithm is a nonlinear function of the four independent variables,so we might expect the sumof individualFWI trend contributions 

from each variable would not be equivalent to the baseline calculation. However,SupplementaryFig.6comparesthebaselineFWItrendtothesum of the four individual variable trends and shows that in most regions in the contiguous UnitedStates, the sum of individual components is consistent in magnitude and spatial distribution with the baseline nonlinear calculation, giving us confidence that the decomposition in Fig. 1 is meaningful. We do see some regions in Supplementary Fig. 6 (e.g., the Cascade and Sierra mountain ranges) where the sum of contributions results in a much smaller FWI trend than the baseline calculation, indicating that compounding factors are dominant here and the overall trend in FWI cannot be explained by the drying trend alone. 

### Humidity in downscaled Earth system model data 

Giventheimportanceofdecliningrelativehumidityforhistoricalchangesin FWI illustrated in Fig. 1 and the prevalence of fire weather projections derived from downscaled ESMs, we now ask to what extent different downscaling methods reproduce observed historical trends in relative humidity. The ESMs used as input to downscaling methods generally contradict observed humidity trends<sup>11</sup> , and others have noted that downscalingnecessarilyimportstheseerrors<sup>17</sup> .InFig. 2,weconfirmthisistruefor four unique downscaling data products, with methods including generative machine learning, physics-based dynamical downscaling, and biascorrection-based statistical downscaling. 

All four downscaled datasets assessed in Fig. 2 generally reproduce trends found in the source ESM projections to varying degrees, with minor deviations depending on the method. In panels b and h of Fig. 2, we can see the generative machine learning dataset SuperResolution for Renewable Energy Resource Data with Climate Change Impacts (Sup3rCC)<sup>24</sup> and the statistically downscaled dataset NASA Earth eXchange Global Daily Downscaled Projections (NASANEX-GDDP)<sup>25</sup> strongly preserve their input trends with only minor deviation in magnitude and some elevation-dependence in Sup3rCC. This is expected, given that these are primarily statistical methods designed explicitly to preserve input trends. The physical downscaling methods, however, do not have these constraints. 

The physical downscaling methods used in the Western United States Dynamically Downscaled Dataset (WUS-D3)<sup>26</sup> and the Regional Climate Model version 4 (RegCM4)<sup>27</sup> datasets shown in panels d and f of Fig. 2 have greater deviations in both spatial patterning and trend magnitude but still preserve large-scale spatial trends from the source ESM. Interestingly, RegCM4 (and to a lesser extent WUS-D3) increases the relative humidity trend in the WUS when compared to the source ESM trend, which is in contradiction of the observations. Note that WUS-D3 uses a different variant of MPI-ESM1.2-HR (r3i1p1f1 as opposed to r1i1p1f1), meaning that panels d and e of Fig. 2 are based on a unique set of 40 year whereas panels b–c and f–i have 35 years in common from the CMIP6 “historical” simulation. This can lead to spatial discrepancies in trend magnitude driven by interannual variability while comparing against panels d and e. Unfortunately, this is an unavoidable limitation of comparing four downscaled data products produced by different institutions. 

Seasonal patterns in relative humidity show similar results in Supplementary Fig. 7 through 10, with ERA5 showing substantial drying trends in all four seasons. Some of the downscaled data and ESM pairs show substantial drying in the Western United States in certain seasons (for example, in NASA-NEX-GDDP in the Fall; Supplementary Fig. 10), but we see that this effect is only seasonal and averages out to minimal changes in Fig. 2. 

The spatial pattern of relative humidity trends varies substantially based on the individual realization of the source ESM, with each ESM showing varying levels of drying in the Western United States, although all areatleastanorderofmagnitudelessthantheobservedtrends.Althoughwe only show one realization per downscaled dataset here, we focus on an ESM that was available in all downscaled data products with the best historical performance<sup>28</sup> , and others have confirmed that the discrepancy of drying trends in the Western United States is a problem with virtually all ESMs from CMIP6<sup>11</sup> .

### Humidity in projections of fire weather 

We now look to the impacts of the relative humidity trend discrepancy in long-term projections of the FWI. Considering the importance of historical declines in relative humidity for the FWI and the humidity bias in ESM projections, we can reasonably expect future projections to show substantially lower rates of increasing FWI compared to the observed historical trends. Indeed, in Fig. 3, we can see the average 1980–2019 FWI trend calculated from the reanalysis data is frequently three to six times greater in the Western United States than the 2000–2039 trend calculated from the Sup3rCC v0.2.2 five-member ensemble<sup>29</sup> . 

The FWI projections based on the Sup3rCC downscaled data shown in Fig. 3b estimate a relatively spatially uniform increase in FWI across most of the contiguous United States based mostly on projected increases in air temperature. As shown in previous Sup3rCC documentation<sup>29</sup> , there is less spatially coherent agreement on changes in relative humidity, precipitation, and wind speed, leading these variables to have a smaller contribution to average projected change in FWI.

One might question whether further trend correction of additional variables is necessary, but at least in this work, we limit ourselves to only exploring the single continued drying scenario based on the substantial evidence of large-scale discrepancies in the ESM-based atmospheric humidity trends<sup>10,11</sup> . 

Figure 4 shows the results from the five-member Sup3rCC projections with and without the specific humidity trend correction using the metric of increase innumber of daysper year above the historical95thpercentile FWI (this threshold calculated per grid cell from the 2000–2019 time period for each Sup3rCC member). 

Without the trend correction of specific humidity, we see little to no change in extreme fire weather days in the Western United States through the next two decades. Because our 95th percentile threshold sets 18 extreme fire weather days per grid cell per year in the historical period, these regional increases represent a 20–70% increase in the rate of extreme days. Anecdotally, these trend-corrected projections appear to better match how wildfires have changed in the Western United States (especially extreme wildfires) since the 1980s<sup>2</sup> .

## Discussion 

In this work, atmospheric humidity emerges as a dominant driver of both historical and projected trends in fire weather across the Western United States. While historical temperature and precipitation trends contribute to fire weather variability, declines in specific humidity in many regions exert an effect several times larger as measured by the Canadian FWI. This result implies that drying in the actual atmospheric water vapor content (specific humidity) is a key mechanism underlying the observed increase in fire weather risk and that the divergence between observations and ESM simulations reported by Simpson et al.<sup>11</sup> is crucial for understanding long-term changes in fire weather. Simpson et al.<sup>11</sup> suggest that observed aridification may arise from lower land-surface moisture availability or more efficient drying processes than represented in many ESMs. Under warming, evapotranspiration initially increases, but once surface moisture becomes limiting, specific humidity cannot continue to rise and atmospheric drying results. Additional model biases such as excessive evaporation, errors in moisture transport, or unrealistic coupling between atmospheric moisture and precipitation may further contribute to discrepancies between simulated and observed moisture trends. These structural differences suggest that near-term projections derived from ESMs may inherit biases in atmospheric moisture dynamics, particularly in specific and relative humidity, with implications for interpreting projected changes in fire weather risk. 

ESMs are frequently downscaled to more regional climates before being used to estimate fire weather risk; however, we find that four unique methods of downscaling ESM projections all carry forward this discrepancy in humidity trends. As such, fire weather metrics derived from downscaled ESM data products are highly likely to underestimate the upward trend of fire weather in the Western United States over the historical time period. There are several such downscaled data products currently in use for real-world decisionmaking on future fire risk<sup>30–33</sup> . We cannot reliably assess the skill of these downscaled data products for the future time period without future observations, yet based on the inaccurate representation of declining humidity over the historical period, caution may be warranted in using these projections as a foundation for real-world fire weather resilience decision-making. ## Methods 

### Fire weather index 

Throughoutthiswork,weusetheFWIasametricforquantifyingtheoverall fire risk from weather factors<sup>34</sup> . The FWI uses daily atmospheric weather variable inputs to estimate the risk of fire ignition and spread. Notably, the metric does not include vegetation or fuel availability (although it does indirectly model fuel moisture content), sources of ignition, or fire suppression. We use the FWI here instead of other similar fire weather metrics because of its ubiquity in similar studies<sup>15,16,19</sup> . There are a number of similar commonly used fire weather metrics, but they consist primarily of similar combinations of the same weather variables, and the Canadian FWI has previously been recommended as the most skillful<sup>35</sup> . 

The calculation of the Canadian FWI involves deriving daily FWI components, such as the Fine Fuel Moisture Code (FFMC), Duff Moisture Code (DMC), and Drought Code (DC), by applying FWI algorithms to input meteorological variables<sup>36,37</sup> . Typically, calculating the daily Canadian FWI requires daily noon observations of temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, and accumulated precipitation. For our calculations, we use daily maximum temperature, daily minimum relative humidity, daily averagewindspeed,anddailytotalprecipitation.Weusethesedailyextrema in temperature and humidity as they drive more extreme fire weather conditions than daily averages alone. We used the software developed by Quilcaille et al.<sup>38</sup> for calculating daily FWI across all products and introduced two adjustments (“day length” and “drying factor”) compared to the original algorithm by Simard<sup>36</sup> . 

To study the isolated contribution of each of the four weather input variables to the overall FWI trends, we run four calculations based on historical reanalysis data, where three of the four input variables are held constant at their 1980–2019 mean value (per grid cell), whereas the fourth variable is the original time series data. This effectively calculates the partial derivative of the FWI with respect to the historical trends of each input variable. This is in addition to the normal FWI calculation based on the original reanalysis input data with no variables held constant. 

### Data sources 

For historical analysis, we use ERA5 reanalysis data<sup>39</sup> for temperature, humidity, and wind speeds combined with Daymet v4 data<sup>40</sup> for precipitation. ERA5 is used at its native 31-km resolution, and Daymet v4 is aggregated from its native 4-km resolution to the ERA5 grid. 

We use gridded reanalysis data for historical weather, relying on previous validation showing that ERA5 performs adequately for historical extreme heat events<sup>41,42</sup> and humidity trends<sup>10,11</sup> and that Daymet performs adequately for historical precipitation<sup>40</sup> . Wind data is more challenging, and we acknowledge that the spatial resolution of ERA5 is unlikely to properly resolve extreme wind events<sup>43</sup> that can drive extreme wildfire conditions. Given the active debate and associated uncertainty in historical trends and changing wind regimes<sup>21–23</sup> , we accept the ERA5 wind data as adequate for this study given wind’s relatively small contribution to overall trends. 

For future projections, we evaluate a number of downscaling methods and source ESM datasets. We aim to represent a wide range of downscaled data products, each with unique methods that are commonly used throughout the literature. Downscaled datasets represent substantial effort, compute costs, and storage volumes. Accordingly, not all downscaled datasets have the same ESMs, shared socioeconomic pathways (SSPs), or simulation variants. In the near future through 2040, the uncertainty from different SSPsis typically smaller than that from natural variability or model formulation<sup>44,45</sup> , so here we focus on comparisons across downscaling products with the same ESM. 

For a machine learning downscaled product, we evaluate the Sup3rCC v0.2.2 dataset. This downscaling product uses generative adversarial networks, a form of generative machine learning, to downscale 100-km daily ESM data to 4-km hourly data<sup>24,29</sup> . We use five members of the Sup3rCC dataset, each from the SSP2-4.5. 

Forphysics-baseddynamicallydownscaleddata,weusetheWUS-D3<sup>26</sup> and dataproduced with the RegCM4<sup>27</sup> . The WUS-D3 datasetis downscaled using the Weather Research and Forecast model based on the SSP3-7.0 and is available at a 9-km hourly resolution over the Western United States. (All other datasets are available over all of the contiguous United States). The RegCM4 data was produced for SSP5-8.5 at a 25-km resolution before daily outputs were bias-corrected to 4 km using Daymet v4. 

For a purely statistical downscaling method, we use the NASA-NEXGDDP version 1.0<sup>25</sup> . This dataset was downscaled using the bias-correction with spatial disaggregation method to approximately a 25-km resolution based on CMIP6 data. Although multiple SSPs are available for NASANEX-GDDP, we only use the SSP5-8.5 scenario, which compares directly against the RegCM4 dataset.

Another widely used family of statistical downscaling methods is based on the constructed analog approach. Although constructed analog downscaling approaches are not considered here, there have been previous studies demonstrating that fire risk derived from downscaled data using these methods understates historical fire weather trends while highlighting relative humidity model biases as a driving factor<sup>19</sup> . 

For the four downscaled CMIP6 projection datasets, we compare daily average relative humidity trends for a single downscaled ESM based on the higher-resolution version of the Max Planck Institute Earth System Model (MPI-ESM1.2-HR)<sup>46</sup> alongside the source ESM trends without downscaling.AlthoughacomparisonofensemblesofESMdatamayshowslightly different results, Simpson et al.<sup>11</sup> found that all ESMs they considered substantially underestimated the drying trends found in observation-based datasets. Therefore, for the relative humidity trends, we use only a single ESM realization for comparison to illustrate the trends in the source ESM simulation and to directly show how each downscaling method handles these trends. We select MPI-ESM1.2-HR as the best-performing ESM that wasavailable in all downscaled data productsbased on a rigorous evaluation of CMIP6 ESMs over the contiguous United States<sup>28</sup> . ### Trends in humidity and the Canadian fire weather index 

For the calculation of trends in relative humidity and FWI, we fit a linear regression to the daily average time series per grid cell over the period of interest and then report the slope of that line in units of change per year (change in relative humidity or FWI). The period of interest varies depending on source dataset and variable: humidity and FWI trends of historical reanalysis data are calculated over 1980–2019 (all Results sections), ESM-based humidity trends (including trends of downscaled data) are calculated over 1980-2019 (Humidity in Downscaled Earth System Model Data), and FWI trends based on Sup3rCC are calculated over 2000–2039(HumidityinProjectionsof Fire Weather).FWItrendsbased on shorterreanalysistimeperiods(e.g.,30-,20-,and10-yearextents)areshown in Supplementary Fig. 17. Shorter time periods (especially 20- and 10-year extents) result in larger spatial variance in the direction and magnitude of trend, especially in the Western United States, likely due to the increasing influence of interannual variability over shorter time periods.
