# Wildfire synchronicity across European regions is favored by specific weather regimes

## Abstract

Abstract Synchronous wildfires (wildfires occurring in multiple locations within a week) pose challenges to coordinated firefighting efforts between European countries. Despite their potentially severe impacts, their occurrence and relationship with predictable atmospheric circulation patterns are poorly understood. We analyze synchronous wildfires across ten European regions over the past two decades and attribute their seasonal occurrences to seven year-round weather regimes. We find that weather regimes modulate the occurrence and spatial extent (the number of regions involved) of synchronous wildfires primarily by driving warm and dry meteorological anomalies, suggesting that these events may become more frequent in a warming world. Specifically, Atlantic Ridge promotes wildfire synchronicity between multiple pairs of European regions in summer, and European Blocking is the most likely regime co-occurring with synchronous wildfires spanning large spatial domains (or involving more than five regions) in spring and fall. Our findings could support early warning systems for synchronous wildfire emergencies in Europe because these weather regimes are predictable on subseasonal timescales.

Synchronous wildfires (wildfires occurring in multiple locations within a week) pose challenges to coordinated firefighting efforts between European countries. Despite their potentially severe impacts, their occurrence and relationship with predictable atmospheric circulation patterns are poorly understood. We analyze synchronous wildfires across ten European regions over the past two decades and attribute their seasonal occurrences to seven year-round weather regimes. We find that weather regimes modulate the occurrence and spatial extent (the number of regions involved) of synchronous wildfires primarily by driving warm and dry meteorological anomalies, suggesting that these events may become more frequent in a warming world. Specifically, Atlantic Ridge promotes wildfire synchronicity between multiple pairs of European regions in summer, and European Blocking is the most likely regime co-occurring with synchronous wildfires spanning large spatial domains (or involving more than five regions) in spring and fall. Wildfires pose severe threats to both society and the environment. Mitigating their impacts requires timely emergency responses and international cooperation<sup>1</sup> . In Europe, such cooperation is facilitated by the European Union Civil Protection Mechanism<sup>2</sup> , which is activated during wildfire emergencies to provide cross-border firefighting support. An example is the 2018 extreme wildfire season in Scandinavia, where central and southern European countries, including Germany, France, and Italy, deployed firefighting equipment and personnel to support wildfire suppression in Sweden<sup>3</sup> . However, sharing resources becomes increasingly difficult when multiple wildfires occur synchronously across regions<sup>4</sup> . These synchronous events can overwhelm firefighting capacities, potentially leading to more severe impacts than isolated incidents. The increase in extreme wildfire seasons in recent decades has intensified pressure on firefighting capacities<sup>5</sup> , not only affecting traditionally fire-prone Mediterranean countries<sup>6</sup> but also raising concerns in less fire-prone regions, such as central and northern Europe<sup>7–9</sup> . Climate change is projected to substantially increase the frequency and intensity of extreme fire weather conditions across Europe<sup>10</sup> . This shift can lead to more frequent synchronous fire weather across larger spatial areas than under current climate conditions<sup>11–13</sup> . Yet, to the best of our knowledge, observation-based evidence of synchronous wildfire 

occurrences across Europe does not exist, significantly limiting our ability to understand, predict, and prepare for these events. In this work, we address these critical knowledge gaps by linking seasonal synchronous wildfire observations in Europe to so-called weather regimes, which are large-scale, quasi-stationary, recurrent, and persistent atmospheric circulation patterns. These weather regimes are more predictable on subseasonal timescales (several weeks in advance) than local daily weather conditions<sup>14,15</sup> , offering a promising avenue for developing early warning systems and enhancing societal preparedness for synchronous wildfire emergencies in the future. 

The development of synchronous wildfires is shaped by climatic and meteorological processes on different timescales. On annual to multidecadal timescales, wildfire synchronicity is modulated by climate variability<sup>16–18</sup> .ThisrelationshipiswellunderstoodinNorthAmerica,where tree ring-based fire chronologies show that the warm phase of the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation promotes subcontinental synchronicity<sup>17</sup> , while the El Niño Southern Oscillation and the Pacific Decadal Oscillation jointly modulate regional synchronicity<sup>17,19,20</sup> . On seasonal timescales, the overlap in fire weather seasonality can increase the probability of spatially compounding fire weather, as shown between Australia and North America, threatening the feasibility of firefighting cooperation between these two

### Weather regimes modulate the occurrence and spatial extent of synchronous wildfires

From the perspective of early warning, it is thus important to understand under which kind of weather regimes synchronous wildfires and their associated meteorological 

conditionsoccur. We analyze the relationship betweenweather regimes and wildfire synchronicity (Fig. 4) using an ERA5-based daily weather regime classification scheme (Methods). For each region pair with at least 20 seasonal synchronous events, we calculate a relative conditional probability describing the likelihood of synchronous wildfires given a specific weather regime relative to the likelihood of wildfire synchronicity overall (Equation (3)). We assess the significance of this probability measure based on block permutations (p < 0.05, Methods). We find that weather regimes modulate the occurrence and spatial extent (i.e., the number of regions involved) of synchronous wildfires in spring, summer, and fall (Fig. 4). Studyingwildfiresynchronicitybased onsatellite wildfire detectionhas several limitations related to the available fire information. First, satellite fire observations do not provide information on the cause of fires, and it is impossible to eliminate anthropogenic impacts on these observations, such as factors that influence ignition, firefighting efficiency, and other wildfire management policies including prescribed burning<sup>45</sup> . While we tried to 

reduce such anthropogenic influence by excluding cropland fires from our analysis, our dataset may still include intentional biomass burnings if they occurred on natural vegetation. Second, the number of synchronous events may have been underestimated for 2001 and the first half of 2002, because Terra was the only operational satellite providing the required observations during that time<sup>46</sup> . However, our key results show limited sensitivity to this satellite detection inhomogeneity. Most of the significant inter-regional wildfire connections are preserved when these two years are excluded (compare Fig. 1 to Figure S15). Similarly, the effects of EuBL, ZO and GL on the spatial extent of synchronous wildfires in spring and fall remain unchanged when excluding these two years, while a moderate number of significant promoting effects of weather regimes on pairwise synchronicity are lost (compare Fig. 4 to Figure S16). Third, the limited temporal coverage of satellite burnedarea detections (25 years) does not yet allow for analyzing theimpactofclimateregimeshiftsonwildfiresynchronicityina robustway. In addition, several methodological limitations need to be considered in the interpretation of our results. First, synchronous wildfire identification inevitably depends on how the region boundaries are set. Different ways of regionalization can affect the identification of synchronous events. Nevertheless, we believe that the regionalization we use is suitable for the analysis of wildfire synchronicity because it captures the main latitudinal and longitudinal climate gradients of Europe and can well distinguish between different types of fire seasonality. Dominant spatial features of the countrylevel synchronicity analysis are generally represented in the region-level synchronicity analysis (compare Figure S6 to Fig. 1), demonstrating that the spatial pattern of synchronicity only shows moderate sensitivity to different ways of regionalization. Second, the significance of wildfire synchronicity is hard to establish via block permutations between two time series with frequent wildfire events, leading to strong but insignificant connections, such as those observed for IP-EMD during summer and fall (Fig. 1c, d). The following three factors can contribute to this: (a) Counts of synchronous wildfire events between time series with frequent events have less interannual variability than between time series with infrequent events; (b) The condition under which two wildfire dates are identified as synchronous can be stricter for time series with frequent than infrequent events (Methods); (c) Random synchronicity (or synchronicity by coincidence) may occur. Thisissuecouldbemitigatedby,forexample,onlyfocusingondayswith fire numbers or burned area above a certain regional percentile threshold. However, we refrain from doing so because a regional percentile-based threshold does not help to address the highly imbalanced regional wildfire activity, but significantly reduces the number of wildfire observations, making it difficult to stratify the analyses seasonally due to a limited sample size. Third, we have focused on the influence of meteorological conditions relevant to wildfire synchronicity controlled by weather regimes. We acknowledge that wildfires also depend on land surface processes acting on longer timescales, such as fuel load and long-term drought conditions during the fire season<sup>49,50</sup> , which can also be influenced by anomalous weather regime occurrence over extended periods<sup>51</sup> . 

We describe the spatial and seasonal patterns of synchronous wildfires in Europe over the past two decades and link their occurrence as well as spatial extent to weather regimes. We identify high-pressure conditions as a key circulation characteristic promoting synchronous wildfires, and also resolve the underlying meteorological driving forces, that is, warm and dry anomalies, and in specific cases also wind anomalies. The implications of these results are two-fold: (a) The warming and (in some regions) drying of the climate system, as well as potential changes in the frequencies and intensities of weather regimes<sup>44</sup> can lead to extended fire seasons in the future. This indicates that under climate change, synchronous wildfires are likely to occur in previously unaffected seasons and may involve more regions than historical observations, which may challenge the current mode of firefighting cooperation in Europe. (b) Since these analyzed weather regimes tend to be better predictable on subseasonal timescales than local daily weather<sup>14,15</sup> , their relationships with synchronous wildfires illustrated here can contribute to improved operational early warnings and preparedness for these wildfire emergencies. Based on our findings, different European regions and countries can coordinate firefighting resources more effectively, taking into account the likelihood of synchronous wildfires between them in the respective season and under the dominant weather regime.

### Daily weather regime classification 

To link synchronous wildfires to weather regimes, we use a weather regime classification scheme for the Atlantic European sector<sup>31</sup> , which was updated as well as adapted to the ERA5 reanalysis<sup>32</sup> . These seven regimes are a refinement of the more classical but seasonally defined four regimes<sup>60,61</sup> . The underlying classification scheme applies k-means clustering to the leading empirical orthogonal function (EOF) modesof 10-day low-pass filtered and seasonally normalized daily geopotential height anomalies at 500 hPa (Z500) from ERA5 to identify seven weather regimes. These weather regimes thus represent quasi-stationary, persistent, and recurrent largescale circulation patterns in the atmosphere, which characteristically modulate regional surface weather conditions relevant for wildfire occurrence. They canbe grouped into two types: four blocking regimes, European Blocking (EuBL), Scandinavian Blocking (ScBL), Atlantic Ridge (AR) and Greenland Blocking (GL), in which positive geopotential height anomalies associated with high pressure conditions near the surface dominate; three cyclonic regimes, Zonal Regime (ZO), Scandinavian Trough (ScTr) and Atlantic Trough (AT), in which the negative geopotential height anomalies associated with low pressure conditions near the surface dominate. During the 20-year study period, each day is either assigned to one of the seven weather regimes or classified as no regime, representing the climatological mean state of geopotential height anomalies at 500 hPa. In principle, this assignment is done as follows: (a) A specific regime is active on a timestep if its so-called regime index is the highest among all seven regime indices (the regimeindexisascalarquantifyingthesimilaritybetweentheZ500anomaly of any given timestep and the mean Z500 pattern of a specific regime) and equal to or above a minimum threshold of 1.0 for at least 5 days. If these conditions are not fulfilled, no regime is active on that timestep. (b) We use the ERA5 3-hourly weather regime class of the first timestep (at 00:00) for each day, under the assumption that the weather regime at the beginning of the day provides the meteorological preconditions for potential wildfires during the day. In most cases (89% of the days during the study period), weather regimes do not change during any given day and the eight 3-hourly classes are the same.
