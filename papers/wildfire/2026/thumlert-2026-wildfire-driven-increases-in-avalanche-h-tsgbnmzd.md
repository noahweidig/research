# WILDFIRE-DRIVEN INCREASES IN AVALANCHE HAZARD: MECHANISMS, CANADIAN CASE STUDIES, AND A POST-WILDFIRE AVALANCHE RISK ASSESSMENT TOOL

## Abstract

Wildfire-induced forest disturbance in snow-dominated mountain regions can significantly alter snowpack processes and increase avalanche hazard.The removal of forest canopy, wind shelter, and mechanical anchoring changes snow accumulation, distribution, and energy balance, promoting the development of persistent weak layers and increasing both the likelihood and size of snow avalanches.These effects can persist for years to decades, yet post-wildfire avalanche hazard is rarely considered in geohazard assessments, creating a critical gap in risk evaluation.This paper describes the physical mechanisms linking wildfire impacts to avalanche formation and dynamics drawing on recent research, field observations, and numerical modeling.Several post-wildfire avalanche hazard case studies from western Canada are presented, illustrating how burn severity, forest structure loss, and altered snow distribution patterns can increase both avalanche likelihood and magnitude.Building on these examples, we introduce a practical post-wildfire avalanche risk identification tool designed to integrate with existing geohazard assessment frameworks.This tool provides a structured method for identifying burn-related avalanche hazard, supporting more accurate risk increases, and decision-making.Addressing this gap will improve land-use planning, infrastructure protection, and operational safety in an era of increasing wildfire frequency, intensity and severity.

    **Abstract:** Wildfire-induced forest disturbance in snow-dominated mountain regions can significantly alter snowpack processes and increase avalanche hazard. The removal of forest canopy, wind shelter, and mechanical anchoring changes snow accumulation, distribution, and energy balance, promoting the development of persistent weak layers and increasing both the likelihood and size of snow avalanches. This paper describes the physical mechanisms linking wildfire impacts to avalanche formation and dynamics drawing on recent research, field observations, and numerical modeling. Several post-wildfire avalanche hazard case studies from western Canada are presented, illustrating how burn severity, forest structure loss, and altered snow distribution patterns can increase both avalanche likelihood and magnitude. Building on these examples, we introduce a practical post-wildfire avalanche risk identification tool designed to integrate with existing geohazard assessment frameworks. This tool provides a structured method for identifying burn-related avalanche hazard, supporting more accurate risk increases, and decision-making. Addressing this gap will improve land-use planning, infrastructure protection, and operational safety in an era of increasing wildfire frequency, intensity and severity.
