# The nuclear-climate nexus for net-zero pathways: Opportunities, challenges, and policy directions: A review

## Abstract

Rapid decarbonization is now a central sustainability challenge because electricity demand is rising while climate risks are increasingly affecting energy infrastructure. Although solar, wind, storage, efficiency and electrification remain essential for net-zero pathways, many power systems also require firm low-carbon resources, resilient infrastructure and governance arrangements that can sustain reliable energy services under extreme weather, geopolitical disruption and growing industrial demand. This review therefore develops the Nuclear-Climate Nexus as a system-level framework for evaluating nuclear energy not simply as a low-operational-emission technology, but as a conditional contributor to climate mitigation, adaptation, energy security and sustainable development. The review shows that nuclear energy can support net-zero pathways through lifetime extension of safe existing reactors, selective new construction, firm low-carbon electricity, nuclear-renewable integration, industrial heat, reactor hydrogen, desalination, district heating and high-reliability electricity. However, its climate value depends on life-cycle emissions, fossil-fuel displacement, deployment time, cost, financing, licensing, fuel availability, supply-chain maturity, waste governance, safeguards, public legitimacy and climate-resilient siting. To address the need for validation beyond conceptual framing, this review also tests the framework against country-level and regional evidence from the United States, Korea, Canada/Ontario, the European Union and China. Existing reactors may provide near-term mitigation where premature closure would increase fossil generation, whereas new large reactors, small modular reactors and advanced reactors require case-specific evidence before being counted as climate-relevant options. The Nuclear-Climate Nexus is therefore proposed as a technology-neutral decision framework for identifying when nuclear energy complements renewables, storage, efficiency, electrification, demand-side measures and carbon management, and when alternative low-carbon options may be faster, cheaper and/or lower risk.
