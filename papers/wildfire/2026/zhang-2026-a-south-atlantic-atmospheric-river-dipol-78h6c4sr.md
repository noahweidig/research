# A South Atlantic atmospheric river dipole as a key precursor of Amazon wildfires

## Abstract

Wildfires in the Amazon have far-reaching climatic and ecological impacts, yet their predictability remains limited. Here we identify a large-scale atmospheric precursor of August–September Amazon wildfires: a June–July dipole mode of South Atlantic atmospheric rivers (ARs), characterized by suppressed AR activity in the north and enhanced activity in the south. This dipole is strongly linked to interannual variability in Amazon wildfire activity through land–atmosphere feedbacks associated with reduced precipitation and humidity, enhanced surface warming and solar radiation, and increased vegetation stress. We estimate that wildfire-related forest loss associated with this AR dipole mode accounts for approximately 26% of the observed interannual variability in total forest loss. Adding the South Atlantic AR dipole to predictors based on May–June sea surface temperature anomalies and June–July wildfire activity yields substantial predictive skill for August–September burned area (R² = 0.67). Climate model projections further indicate a future intensification of the South Atlantic AR dipole mode, implying increased wildfire-driven ecological degradation in the Amazon under continued warming. This study identifies a South Atlantic atmospheric river dipole as an early warning signal for Amazon wildfires, linking shifts in moisture transport to hotter, drier conditions and increased fire activity in the region.

Under global warming, wildfire activity in the Amazon rainforest releases an estimated 2 billion tons of carbon annually and exerts a substantial influence on the global carbon cycle, with pronounced regional variability<sup>1–3</sup> . Large fires in southern Amazonia, in particular, increasingly threaten long-established primary forests and ecosystems that have developed over centuries. Vegetation loss and soil degradation hinder forest recovery, weaken the region’s carbon sink capacity, and further amplify climate warming<sup>4–6</sup> . The impacts of these wildfires extend beyond carbon emissions: smoke and aerosols can travel thousands of kilometers, degrading air quality and adversely affecting human health across South America<sup>7–9</sup> . Between 2010 and 2019, millions of people were exposed to severe fire-related air pollution for at least one day per year<sup>10,11</sup> , with the record-breaking fire seasons of 2019 and 2020 causing widespread environmental damage<sup>10</sup> . Despite these profound impacts, the drivers and predictability of Amazon wildfires remain less well understood than in other fire-prone regions, such as North America and Australia<sup>11,12</sup> . 

The frequency and intensity of Amazon forest wildfires are strongly influenced by climatic conditions, including high temperatures, reduced precipitation, and prolonged soil drought<sup>13–15</sup> . Interannual climate variability, such as the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and Atlantic sea surface temperature (SST) anomalies, is closely associated with regional droughts and enhanced fire activity<sup>16–18</sup> . Seasonal rainfall deficits increase vegetation stress, reduce fuel moisture, and create conditions conducive to widespread burning<sup>19–21</sup> . Large-scale atmospheric circulation patterns further modulate the spatial distribution of fires across the basin through interactions with local weather, soil moisture, and forest structure<sup>22–24</sup> . Building on these insights, recent studies have explored the predictive potential of climate and hydroclimatic indicators for Amazon wildfires, including seasonal rainfall forecasts<sup>21,25</sup> , SST-based predictors<sup>18,26,27</sup> , ENSO-based indices<sup>26,28,29</sup> , and vegetation greenness metrics<sup>25</sup> . One study showed that the Oceanic Niño Index primarily affects fires in the eastern Amazon, while the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation influences fires in the southern and southwestern Amazon<sup>26</sup> . Building on previous studies linking hydroclimatic variability to Amazon wildfire activity, we propose that atmospheric rivers (ARs)— long, narrow corridors of concentrated moisture transport—represent an important component of the region’s hydroclimate<sup>30–32</sup> . Over continental South America, features detected as ARs form part of the broader Atlantic–Amazon–Andes moisture-transport system<sup>33–35</sup> . Within this system, tropical Atlantic inflow supplies moisture to the Amazon basin, where land–atmosphere recycling further augments atmospheric moisture<sup>33,36,37</sup> . Low-level flow subsequently transports a portion of this moisture southward along the eastern Andes toward the La Plata Basin<sup>36,38–40</sup> , while particular circulation regimes allow some of it to reach the subtropical South Atlantic<sup>34</sup> . Variability in this moisture transport is closely linked to the major regional low-level circulation systems, particularly the Orinoco Low-Level Jet (OLLJ) over northern South America and the South American Low-Level Jet (SALLJ) east of the Andes<sup>38,40–43</sup> . These low-level jets play a central role in organizing and redistributing atmospheric moisture across the Atlantic–Amazon–Andes corridor. As a result, ARs are often dynamically coupled to these jet systems, with their orientation, propagation, and moisture transport strongly constrained by the associated lowlevel flow<sup>34,35,37</sup> . By modulating rainfall, humidity, and surface energy fluxes, ARs can influence vegetation stress, fuel availability, and forest flammability<sup>44,45</sup> . Although ARs are well known for producing extreme precipitation and flooding in other regions<sup>46–48</sup> , whether and to what extent South Atlantic AR variability preconditions Amazon wildfire activity remains unclear. Clarifying this relationship may provide a physically grounded basis for improving seasonal wildfire prediction. 

Here, we demonstrate that variability in South Atlantic ARs during June–July is closely associated with subsequent wildfire activity and forest loss across the Amazon. This finding identifies South Atlantic AR variability as an important component of the climatic system influencing fire activity in the region. Importantly, a June–July South Atlantic AR dipole index provides skillful subseasonal prediction of August–September wildfire occurrence, substantially enhancing forecasting capability. This study advances understanding of the largescale climatic controls on Amazon wildfires and establishes a physically grounded framework for early-warning systems, offering practical guidance for anticipating wildfire risk and supporting ecosystem management under future warming scenarios. 

## Results 

### Lead–lag relationship between atmospheric rivers and Amazon wildfires 

To identify the dominant co-variability between ARs and Amazon wildfire activity, we applied singular value decomposition (SVD) to June–July South Atlantic AR track counts (0°–40° S, 80° W–10° W) and August–September burned area across the Amazon (0°–30° S, 70° W–40° W). These months were selected because both South Atlantic AR occurrence and wildfire activity are relatively frequent during this period, with wildfires peaking in August–September and South 

Atlantic ARs also exhibiting elevated activity in June–July (Supplementary Fig. 1). South Atlantic ARs occur most frequently along the subtropical storm track, whereas burned areas are primarily concentrated in southern Amazonia. This spatial correspondence points to a potentially physically meaningful linkage between South Atlantic AR variability and subsequent Amazon wildfire activity, beyond a purely statistical association. 

The leading SVD mode (Fig. 1) explains 45% of the total covariance between the two fields, indicating a strong and dominant relationship. The associated South Atlantic AR pattern (Fig. 1a) exhibits a clear north–south dipole in AR track counts. The domain in Fig. 1a was selected to encompass the region of highest AR occurrence, where interannual variability is strongest. Sensitivity analyses using alternative domains yield a similar dipole structure, indicating that the results are not sensitive to the choice of analysis domain. When South Atlantic AR activity is suppressed in the north and enhanced in the south, the corresponding burned-area pattern shows widespread increases in wildfire activity across southern Amazonia (Fig. 1b). The two principal component time series (Fig. 1c) are highly correlated (r = 0.65), and the correlation remains significant after detrending (r = 0.52), indicating that the relationship primarily reflects interannual variability rather than long-term trends. Therefore, all subsequent analyses are based on the original data, with the linear trend retained. To assess the robustness of the apparent strengthening of the correlation after 2000, we further examined the relationship using additional wildfire burned-area datasets, including FireCCILT11 and GFED5. The results show a similar temporal evolution and a comparable increase in the AR–wildfire correlation during the post-2000 period across these datasets. This consistency indicates a robust link between South Atlantic ARs and Amazon wildfire activity (Supplementary Fig. 2). In addition, the leading South Atlantic AR mode is closely associated with wildfire frequency across southern Amazonia (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Together, these results demonstrate that June–July South Atlantic AR variability provides a clear leading signal for August–September wildfire activity in the Amazon. 

Given the close connection between ARs and low-level moisture transport over South America, and the potential overlap between intense continental ARs and low-level jets, we further examined whether the relationship between the South Atlantic AR dipole and Amazon wildfire activity can be distinguished from variability associated with the South American Low-Level Jet (SALLJ) and Orinoco Low-Level Jet (OLLJ). Partial-correlation analysis shows that the positive association between the June–July South Atlantic AR index and August–September Amazon burned area remains spatially extensive after controlling separately for the SALLJ and OLLJ indices (Supplementary Fig. 4a, c). By comparison, the correlations between burned area and the SALLJ or OLLJ indices become weaker and more spatially heterogeneous after controlling for the AR index (Supplementary Fig. 4b, d). These results indicate that although ARs and low-level jets are closely related components of regional moisture transport, the AR–wildfire association cannot be fully captured by variability in the selected low-level jet indices. The South Atlantic AR dipole therefore provides complementary information on regional moisture transport associated with subsequent Amazon wildfire activity. 

The relationship between the leading South Atlantic ARs principal component (ARJJ) and the leading August–September wildfire burnedarea principal component (BAAS), together with key land–atmosphere variables, further supports this linkage. Correlation analysis (Fig. 1d) shows that both ARJJ and BAAS are closely associated with August–September meteorological conditions over the southern Amazon (5° S–20° S, 65° W–40° W). ### Impacts of atmospheric rivers on Amazon ecosystems and future projections 

Under future warming scenarios, the South Atlantic AR dipole pattern (see the subsection AR detection and analysis in the Methods) is projected to strengthen markedly, with AR track counts decreasing in 

northern regions and increasing in southern regions. The magnitude of this intensification increases with emission scenario severity: the projected trend under Shared Socioeconomic Pathway 2–4.5 (SSP2–4.5) is 0.02 per decade, while under Shared Socioeconomic Pathway 5–8.5 (SSP5–8.5) it approximately doubles to 0.04 per decade (Fig. 3a). Forest fragmentation is projected to increase by 11.0%, substantially reducing ecological connectivity across the basin and compromising ecosystem functioning (Fig. 3d). Taken together, these results indicate that anthropogenic warming is likely to intensify South Atlantic AR variability, exacerbate wildfire activity, and accelerate forest degradation and carbon losses in the Amazon. The pronounced divergence among emission scenarios underscores the importance of mitigation efforts to limit future ecological damage and associated climate feedbacks. 

### Predicting Amazon wildfires based on atmospheric rivers 

The predictive potential of South Atlantic ARs for Amazon wildfire activity was assessed using a multiple linear regression model that incorporates the June–July South Atlantic AR index (ARJJ), May–June SST, and June–July southern Amazon burned area, with the latter included to account for the persistence and seasonal memory of wildfire activity. The SST predictor was defined as the meridional SST gradient between two regions, R1 (5° N–25° N, 60° W–20° W) and R2 (45° S–60° S, 60° W–20° W), computed as the difference in mean SST anomalies between R1 and R2, following previous studies that demonstrated the relevance of this gradient for predicting Amazon wildfire activity<sup>18</sup> . Model performance was evaluated using a leave-oneout cross-validation approach. 

The predicted August–September burned area closely reproduces the observed interannual variability (R² = 0.67; Fig. 4a). In comparison, a baseline model including only SST and antecedent wildfire activity explains less variance (R² = 0.58; Supplementary Fig. 12a). The results demonstrate that AR variability provides additional predictive skill, corresponding to a 15.5% relative increase in explained variance compared with the baseline model, beyond that associated with fire persistence and oceanic precursors alone. This indicates that ARs serve as an independent, physically meaningful predictor rather than a proxy for autocorrelated fire activity. 

We further examined commonly used climate indices, including ENSO and the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation, which have been reported to influence Amazon wildfire variability. When these indices are considered together with ARs and SST anomalies in a multivariate linear regression framework with stepwise selection<sup>52</sup> , they do not yield a measurable improvement in predictive skill. This result likely reflects differences in their dominant spatial influence and characteristic time scales relative to the more regionally focused and seasonally specific signals captured by South Atlantic ARs and SST anomalies. 

Spatially, the model successfully reproduces the observed distribution of burned area, including major fire hotspots, and captures the spatial patterns of severe fire years such as 2017 and 2019, with spatial correlation coefficients exceeding 0.9 (Fig. 4b–e). Importantly, consistent performance is found across the two terrestrial ecoregion types, indicating no marked dependence of predictive skill on ecological classification. In particular, quantitative evaluation shows that the spatial correlation between predicted and observed burned area 

reaches 0.80 (0.80) in 2017 (2019) for Tropical and Subtropical Moist Broadleaf Forests, and 0.91 (0.87) for Tropical and Subtropical Grasslands, Savannas, and Shrublands, respectively. 

Importantly, using June–July predictors to forecast August–September wildfire activity avoids temporal overlap with the predictand, providing a 1–2-month lead time. Considering that AR events are typical synoptic-scale processes whose direct impacts may not persist throughout an entire season, we also examined the use of July–August ARs to predict August–September Amazon wildfire activity (Supplementary Fig. 12b). This approach still demonstrates considerable predictive skill, indicating that ARs not only exert significant influences on the synoptic scale, but can also generate more persistent climatic effects through the accumulation of events at the seasonal scale. 

## Discussion 

This study identifies a pathway linking variability in South Atlantic ARs to subsequent Amazon forest degradation and wildfire activity. The June–July north–south AR dipole pattern over the South Atlantic plays a pivotal role in shaping land–atmosphere interactions that favor extensive burning in the following months. This AR mode is associated with a cascade of hydroclimatic responses across the Amazon, including reduced rainfall and humidity, suppressed vegetation growth, enhanced surface heating, and strengthened upper-level anticyclonic circulation, which together precondition forest environments for fire ignition and spread. Importantly, the June–July AR dipole pattern explains approximately 26% of the spatial variability in forest loss across the Amazon. By combining June–July AR indices with associated sea surface temperature gradients and the June–July southern Amazon wildfire index, our statistical framework skillfully predicts August–September wildfire activity over the Amazon, achieving an R² of 0.67. These findings highlight the important role of AR dynamics in Amazon wildfire predictability and provide a physically grounded pathway for early-warning systems linking atmospheric circulation to ecosystem vulnerability under future warming. Projections of future climate further indicate an intensification of the South Atlantic AR dipole mode and its associated amplification of Amazon wildfire risk, underscoring the urgency of mitigation efforts to limit ecological degradation in the Amazon<sup>53,54</sup> . 

Previous studies have primarily emphasized local climate anomalies, ENSO-related teleconnections, or land-use change as dominant drivers of Amazon wildfires<sup>14,28,29</sup> . While these factors capture important aspects of fire variability, extratropical moisture transport and large-scale atmospheric circulation provide an additional perspective on the climatic processes that precondition forest environments. By identifying the South Atlantic AR dipole as a physically grounded precursor, our study complements and extends earlier work, offering predictive skill for both the magnitude and spatial distribution of burned area. In contrast to ENSO-based or local precipitation indices<sup>25,28</sup> , the AR dipole integrates both dynamical and thermodynamic signals, providing mechanistic insight into how subtropical atmospheric processes influence tropical fire regimes. Quantifying the fraction of Amazon forest loss attributable to AR variability provides an explicit link between large-scale atmospheric drivers and ecosystem degradation. 

Several limitations should be noted. The predictive skill of the ARbased framework is inherently constrained by the spatial extent of historical wildfire activity. As deforested and fire-prone edges of the Amazon continue to migrate inward, future wildfires are likely to encroach into forested core regions that have experienced limited burning in the historical record<sup>12,55,56</sup> . The lack of historical fire occurrence in these regions limits the ability to establish robust statistical relationships between AR variability and wildfire activity. ## Methods 

### Data 

This study employs the fifth generation of the ECMWF Reanalysis (ERA5) at its native 0.25° × 0.25° spatial resolution for the period 1979–2020<sup>64</sup> . Key variables include six-hourly horizontal winds and specific humidity at 37 pressure levels between 1000 and 300 hPa, which were used for AR detection. Daily geopotential height, horizontal wind, near-surface air temperature, precipitation, and radiation fluxes were derived from hourly ERA5 data, while monthly variables were obtained directly. Sea surface temperature data were taken from the HadISST1 dataset provided by the Met Office Hadley Centre<sup>65</sup> at a spatial resolution of 1° × 1°. 

Monthly burned-area data were obtained from the Reconstructed Global Monthly Burned Area Maps from 1901 to 2020 dataset<sup>66</sup> . This product provides global burned-area fractions at 0.5° × 0.5° resolution, reconstructed using machine-learning models trained on present-day satellite observations (FireCCI51) for the period 2003–2020, together with climate, vegetation, population, and land-use variables. Following the Global Fire Emissions Database (GFED) regional framework, the globe was divided into 14 regions, with separate models developed for each. To better represent extreme events, grid cells exceeding the 90th percentile of burned area within each region were classified as extreme-fire areas and modeled separately. The reconstructed dataset spans 1901–2020 and has been validated against independent satellite observations and palaeo-fire records, providing a spatially and temporally consistent long-term record of wildfire activity. In this study, analyses were conducted primarily for the period 1979–2020. In addition, we further used the FireCCI Global Long-Term (LT11) Burned Area Product (FireCCILT11)<sup>67</sup> and Global Fire Emissions Database version 5 (GFED5) burned-area<sup>68</sup> datasets for cross-validation, which confirmed the robustness and stability of the results. 

Future changes in the South Atlantic AR dipole pattern were assessed using output from 16 models participating in Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6; Supplementary Table 1) under the SSP2–4.5 and SSP5–8.5 scenarios. Based on the established AR database spanning 1979–2100, with 2014 marking the transition between historical simulations and future projections, three 30-year periods were analyzed: the near future (2021–2050), mid-century (2051–2080), and late century (2081–2100). The ensemble mean of historical simulations from the 16 CMIP6 models reproduces the climatological distribution of ARs over the South Atlantic, including the locations and magnitudes of the principal maxima. 

Fire-induced carbon emissions were derived from the burnedarea component of the Global Fire Emissions Database version 5 (GFED5)<sup>69</sup> . GFED5 provides monthly burned-area estimates at 0.25° × 0.25° resolution for five fire types—natural burning, cropland burning, deforestation burning, peatland burning, and total burned area—covering the period 2001–2020. For the pre-MODIS period (1997–2000), only the total burned area is available at 1° × 1° resolution. Combined 

with emission factors, GFED5 enables consistent estimation of wildfirerelated carbon emissions across fire types and regions. 

Drought conditions were characterized using the global Standardized Precipitation–Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) dataset (SPEIbase), which provides long-term, high-quality monthly drought indices at a spatial resolution of 0.5°<sup>70</sup> . SPEIbase offers multi-timescale drought metrics ranging from 1 to 48 months, derived from monthly precipitation and potential evapotranspiration data from the Climatic Research Unit (CRU) at the University of East Anglia, beginning in January 1901 and updated continuously. 

Vegetation conditions were characterized using the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), a widely used indicator of vegetation greenness<sup>71</sup> . Global NDVI estimates are available at approximately 1 km spatial resolution with a 10-day temporal frequency, derived from SPOT/VEGETATION observations for 1999–2013 and from PROBA-V observations from 2014 through June 2020. 

Forest loss data were obtained from a global Landsat-based dataset with a native spatial resolution of 30 m, covering the period 2000–2020<sup>72</sup> . Within the Essential Biodiversity Variable (EBV) framework, forest cover loss is defined as a stand-replacement disturbance that results in the transition from forest to non-forest land cover. The original data were aggregated to a spatial resolution of 450 × 450 m using a scaling factor of 15 and formatted according to the multidimensional structure and standards established by the GEO BON community for EBVs. 

### Fire weather index calculation 

The Fire Weather Index (FWI) used in this study is based on the Canadian FWI system, which is widely applied in global fire-weather and fire-risk research. The index was calculated using ERA5 reanalysis data, including daily total precipitation, near-surface air temperature, wind speed, and relative humidity. FWI integrates the Initial Spread Index and the Build-Up Index to provide a quantitative measure of potential fire intensity. The computation of both observed and modeled FWI follows the methodology implemented in a publicly available code repository<sup>73</sup> . 

### AR detection and analysis 

ARs were identified using version 1.0.0 of the open-source Python algorithm developed by Kennett (2021)<sup>74</sup> , which is based on the Guan and Waliser (2015) formulation<sup>75</sup> . This implementation reproduces the principal characteristics of the original Guan and Waliser framework while providing a computationally consistent approach that can be applied uniformly to both ERA5 reanalysis and all CMIP6 simulations. Using a single detection algorithm throughout avoids methodological inconsistencies among different AR datasets and enables direct comparison of historical and future AR activity across multiple climate models and emission scenarios. This approach ensures that detected ARs satisfy established criteria for length, narrowness, and flow direction, enabling robust identification of AR pathways and landfall locations. grid cells exceeding both thresholds were identified as enhanced-IVT objects. 

For each object intersecting the land mask, the land grid cell with the maximum IVT was defined as the landfall location. Starting from this cell, an upstream AR axis was constructed following Brands et al. (2017)<sup>76</sup> , as implemented by Kennett (2021)<sup>74</sup> . At each step, the local IVT direction was discretized into eight cardinal directions, and among the upstream neighboring grid cell and its two adjacent cells, the cell with the highest IVT was appended to the axis provided that its IVT exceeded the applicable threshold. Axis construction terminated when no candidate cell exceeded the threshold or a previously selected cell was encountered. 

An object was classified as an AR only if it satisfied all of the following criteria: 

1. Axis length > 2000 km; 

2. Length-to-width ratio > 2 (width defined as footprint area divided by axis length); 

3. Poleward component of the object-mean IVT > 50 kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹; 

4. At least 50% of footprint grid cells had IVT directions within 45° of the object-mean IVT direction; 

5. The orientation defined by the first and last axis cells differed from the object-mean IVT direction by no more than 45°; 

6. The distance between the axis endpoints exceeded 1000 km. 

All subsequent spatial analyses were based on the extracted AR axes rather than AR footprints. The gridded AR-axis occurrence count was defined as the number of six-hourly AR axes intersecting each grid cell. ARs were identified independently at each six-hourly time step without explicitly linking consecutive detections into lifecycle trajectories. Although AR lifecycles were not tracked, persistent ARs contribute multiple consecutive detections, allowing the occurrence count to reflect their temporal persistence. Comparison with multiple independent AR detection methods further demonstrates that the choice of using AR axes rather than AR footprints does not alter the principal conclusions of this study (Supplementary Fig. 13). 

The method for identifying ARs in the CMIP6 data follows the same approach as applied to the reanalysis data, based on IVT and the criteria outlined above. By linear projection, we derived the time series of June–July South Atlantic AR dipoles for each model, consistent with the observed SVD1 mode. 

To evaluate the sensitivity of the inferred AR–wildfire relationship to AR detection, we repeated the SVD analysis using three AR definitions from the Atmospheric River Tracking Method Intercomparison Project (ARTMIP): Guan and Waliser v4 (2024)<sup>77</sup> , Lora v2<sup>78</sup> and Pan–Lu<sup>79</sup> (Supplementary Fig. 13). The Lora v2 and Pan–Lu methods broadly reproduce the leading coupled mode obtained in our original analysis (Supplementary Fig. 13g–l). Although the Guan and Waliser v4 method<sup>77</sup> captures the dominant spatial mode of South Atlantic AR activity, its associated wildfire pattern shows some differences (Supplementary Fig. 13a–c), likely owing to the inclusion of relatively weak ARs resulting from the segment-based poleward criterion. Applying a strong-AR criterion (object-mean IVT ≥ 500 kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹) produces a wildfire pattern that is much more consistent with those obtained using the other detection methods (Supplementary Fig. 13d–f). These results support the robustness of the AR–wildfire relationship while identifying AR intensity as an important source of uncertainty among detection methods. 

To disentangle the thermodynamic and dynamic contributions to the AR frequency trend, we decomposed the total AR variability into dynamically and thermodynamically driven components using a scaling approach<sup>80,81</sup> . In this framework, a synthetic field of daily IVT representing purely dynamical variability was generated by rescaling the specific humidity field. The scaling coefficient, defined as the ratio qc=qs, adjusts the specific humidity at each grid point and pressure level, where qc is the climatological mean and qs is the seasonal mean 

for the given June–July period. Because moisture changes are expected to follow the Clausius–Clapeyron relationship, this scaling effectively removes year-to-year variations in atmospheric moisture. The IVT derived from this scaled humidity field was calculated using the same detection threshold and then applied to the AR identification algorithm, thereby isolating the variability in ARs that arises purely from dynamical processes. The thermodynamic component was subsequently obtained as the residual between the total and dynamical effects, consistent with evidence that the two contributions combine approximately linearly. 

### Low-level jet indices 

Following Montini et al. (2019)<sup>38</sup> , the South American Low-Level Jet (SALLJ) was identified at Santa Cruz de la Sierra, Bolivia (SC; 17.8° S, 63.2° W), and Mariscal Estigarribia, Paraguay (MA; 22.0° S, 60.6° W), using six-hourly winds at 850 and 700 hPa. The zonal and meridional wind components were averaged over the four grid cells nearest to each location. A SALLJ event was identified at each site when all three criteria were satisfied: (1) the 850 hPa wind speed exceeded its site- and season-specific 75th percentile; (2) the wind-speed difference between 850 and 700 hPa exceeded its corresponding 75th percentile; and (3) the 850 hPa wind direction was between 292.5° and 45°, representing flow from northwest through north to northeast. A day was classified as a SALLJ day when these criteria were simultaneously satisfied during at least one of the four six-hourly time steps (00, 06, 12, or 18 UTC). The SALLJ indices were defined as the number of SALLJ days during June–July and August–September of each year. 

Following Builes-Jaramillo et al. (2022)<sup>41</sup> , the Orinoco Low-Level Jet (OLLJ) intensity index was defined using the area-averaged 925 hPa zonal wind over the OLLJ core region (6°–8° N, 67°–69° W). Because the OLLJ is characterized by easterly flow, the sign of the zonal wind was reversed so that positive index values indicate a stronger OLLJ. 

### Liang–Kleeman information flow 

To identify the dominant pathways through which June–July ARs influence August–September wildfires, we quantified directional interactions using the Liang–Kleeman information-flow framework<sup>82,83</sup> , which provides a physically consistent and rigorous measure of information transfer in complex dynamical systems. Unlike correlation-based metrics, this method distinguishes directional influence from statistical association by quantifying the contribution of one variable to the rate of change of uncertainty (entropy) in another. 

Consider a two-variable stochastic dynamical system with X 1 (target) and X 2 (source), whose evolution is assumed to follow linear stochastic dynamics driven by Gaussian noise. In this framework, information flow from X 2 to X 1 is defined as the rate of change of the marginal entropy of X 1 induced by X 2, providing a directional and asymmetric measure of causality. The maximum likelihood estimator can be expressed analytically in terms of sample covariances and temporal derivatives of the observed time series.

activity; wildfire-induced CO₂ emissions, which quantify carbon released to the atmosphere; wildfire-driven forest fragmentation, which characterizes degradation of forest structural integrity; and wildfire-related forest loss, which reflects reductions in forest cover. Among these indicators, burned area, wildfire-induced CO₂ emissions, and forest loss are derived directly from observational datasets, whereas forest fragmentation is quantified using the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI). 

### Forest fragmentation index 

For forest fragmentation, a binary forest mask was constructed for each time slice, and all contiguous forest patches were delineated using a four-neighbor connectivity scheme. Three patch-scale structural metrics were then computed for each forest pixel: edge density (ED), patch density (PD), and mean patch area (MPA). Edge density was calculated by convolving each patch with a 3 × 3 neighborhood kernel to identify boundary pixels and normalizing the resulting edge length by the total forested area. Patch density was defined as the number of discrete forest patches per unit area. Mean patch area was obtained by summing the area of each patch—converted from pixel count using a 30-m pixel size—and assigning this value to all pixels within the corresponding patch. To ensure comparability among metrics, ED, PD, and MPA were min–max normalized across space and time. The pixelwise Forest Fragmentation Index (FFI) was then defined as the average of the three normalized components, with MPA entering as 1−normalized MPA to represent increasing fragmentation with decreasing patch size.

### Wildfire prediction and Leave-one-out cross-validation 

A multiple linear regression model was developed to predict August–September grid-level wildfire burned area across the Amazon (BAAS) using the June–July South Atlantic AR index (ARJJ), June–July Amazon burned area (BAJJ), and May–June sea surface temperature (SSTMJ). The prediction model is expressed as:

where a0, a1, a2, and a3 denote the coefficients determined through the multivariable regression procedure for BAAS. ARJJ, BAJJ, and SSTMJ are indices of the three predictors. 

The BAAS prediction model was evaluated using a leave-one-out cross-validation approach. For each year, the regression coefficients (a0–a3) were estimated using data from all remaining years at each grid point, and the trained model was then used to predict the excluded year. This procedure was repeated for all years from 1979 to 2020, yielding a cross-validated time series of annual predictions and demonstrating the model’s capability to robustly forecast interannual variability in wildfire activity.
