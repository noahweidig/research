# Increasing global human exposure to wildland fires despite declining burned area

## Abstract

Although half of Earth’s population resides in the wildland-urban interface, human exposure to wildland fires remains unquantified. We show that the population directly exposed to wildland fires increased 40% globally from 2002 to 2021 despite a 26% decline in burned area. Increased exposure was mainly driven by enhanced colocation of wildland fires and human settlements, doubling the exposure per unit burned area. We show that population dynamics accounted for 25% of the 440 million human exposures to wildland fires. Although wildfire disasters in North America, Europe, and Oceania have garnered the most attention, 85% of global exposures occurred in Africa. The top 0.01% of fires by intensity accounted for 0.6 and 5% of global exposures and burned area, respectively, warranting enhanced efforts to increase fire resilience in disaster-prone regions.

37 Wildland fires – here defined as fires in vegetated land excluding commercial agriculture 38 areas – have increasingly impacted social and environmental systems in various 39 regions across the globe in recent years ( _1_ , _2_ ), directly accounting for at least 2,500 40 human deaths and 10,500 injuries from 1990-2021 ( _3_ ). Indirect impacts of wildland fires 41 are several-fold larger and extend for years after the burn ( _4_ ). For example, an annual 42 1.53 million deaths around the globe are attributable to landscape-fire-induced air 43 pollution ( _5_ ). Fire activity is directly linked with climate and weather ( _6_ ), and climate 44 change has increased the number of days conducive to extreme fire behavior over 45 many fire-prone regions, culminating in a >54% increase in extreme fire weather from 46 1979-2022 globally ( _3_ ), longer fire-weather seasons ( _7_ ), and increased nighttime 47 flammability ( _8_ ).

Human activities can compound or confound the climate change impacts on fire activity ( _3_ , _9_ , _10_ ). Human ignitions, both intentional and accidental, account for 84% of all wildfires in the contiguous United States ( _11_ ) and >90% in Mediterranean Europe ( _12_ ). Although lightning ignitions can dominate in more remote regions ( _13_ , _14_ ), people strongly modify the timing and locations of fires ( _11_ , _15_ ). People also modify vegetation type, structure, density and continuity, which, in turn, change regional fire regimes ( _16_ ). For example, the introduction of invasive species in deserts of North America resulted in more frequent and larger wildfires ( _17_ ), whereas agriculturally-induced land fragmentation in African savannas reduced burned areas ( _18_ ). Outside of the tropics, humans commonly seek to limit burned area by suppressing fire, but as settlement expands into previously wildland areas, causing the Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) to grow ( _19_ ), human exposure to fire also grows ( _20_ ). While the WUI covers only 4.7% of the global land surface, nearly half of Earth’s population reside in WUI areas ( _21_ ) and regional and global WUIs have been expanding ( _22_ , _23_ ). 

The confluence of changing social and environmental controls of fires resulted in 26% decline in global burned area in the past two decades, driven by trends in African savannas ( _24_ ), despite increased fire extent in temperate and boreal forests ( _25_ ) and increased occurrence of intense fire events ( _26_ ). Here we examine the global and regional patterns and trends of human exposure to wildland fires. We further probe the characteristics of wildland fires that impact humans, and investigate the role of population dynamics in the overall exposure patterns and trends. Additionally, we investigate patterns, trends, and drivers of population exposure to intense fire events, as exposure to such fires has been tied to fire disasters ( _2_ ). We use 18.6 million individual fire records from 2002-2021 from the MODIS-based Global Fire Atlas ( _18_ , _27_ ) and gridded population data from WorldPop ( _28_ ). We also use MODIS-based land cover and use ( _29_ ), active fire records ( _30_ ), and vegetation indices ( _31_ ) to exclude nonwildland fires in the Global Fire Atlas ( _27_ ). Our approach does not exclude deforestation

Exposure to wildland fires can be 84 harmful and devastating, resulting in loss of lives and damage to infrastructure, noting 85 that not all instances of exposure lead to negative outcomes.

## **Trends of global exposure to wildland fire** 

Global annual exposure to wildland fire increased by 7.7 million people (+39.6%) from 2002-2021 (382,700 persons/year; p<10<sup>-5</sup> ; Fig. S1A). Africa accounted for nearly all of the increase (373,000 persons/year; p<10<sup>-7</sup> ; +46.3%). South America (+33.8%), North America (+15.7%) and Asia (+2.8%) experienced non-significant increases in exposure to wildland fire from 2002-2021, whereas Oceania (-47.3%) and Europe (-17.3%) observed non-significant declines (Fig. S1A). Interannual variability of exposure to wildland fire and age distribution of the exposed populations are discussed in Sections S2 and S3 (Fig. S10), respectively. 

Exposure density also increased globally, exposing an additional 6.2 (+93.4%) persons per km<sup>2</sup> of burned area in two decades (p<10<sup>-6</sup> ; Fig. S1B). All continents experienced rising exposure density, with Africa and Europe showing the largest increases, exposing an additional 7.9 (+90.4%) and 6.8 (+42.3%) persons per km<sup>2</sup> of burned area, respectively, in two decades (p<10<sup>-7</sup> and p=0.2, respectively; Fig. S1B).

## **Role of population dynamics in exposure to wildland fire** 

135 By applying fixed 2002 population patterns, we developed a counterfactual model to 136 account for the role of population dynamics versus fire dynamics in driving the observed 137 exposure trends. Population dynamics – i.e., population growth and migration during 138 2002-2021 – accounted for 111.3 million exposures to wildland fire in the past two 139 decades globally (25.3% of all global exposures; Fig. 2A). Without the effects of 140 population dynamics, cumulative exposure to wildland fire from 2002-2021 would have 141 been between 10.0% to 27.9% lower in Africa, Asia, North America, and South America, 142 but 5.0% and 2.3% higher in Europe and Oceania, respectively (Fig. 2). Counterfactual 143 trends in Europe and Oceania are driven by the marginal shrinking of the fire and 144 human settlement overlap from 2002-2021 due a variety of factors including migration 145 from rural to urban areas. 146 147 In the absence of population dynamics, the global exposure to wildland fire would have 148 decreased at a rate of 278,000 people/year (p<10<sup>-4</sup> ; 27.7% decline in two decades) 149 commensurate with the decline in burned area (Fig. 2A) – with counterfactual trends 150 primarily driven by declines in Africa (-250,000 people/year, p<10<sup>-4</sup> ; -29.8%; Fig. 2E). 151 152 Without the population changes since 2002, exposure per unit burned area would be 153 rather stable globally (1.8% decline in two decades; -0.006 persons/km<sup>2</sup> /year; p=0.83), 154 indicating that human influences, including population increase in and migration to fire-

## **Population exposure to intense wildland fires** 

Following Cunningham et al. ( _26_ ), we define “intense” fires as events with cumulative fire radiative power exceeding the 99.99<sup>th</sup> percentile of all wildland fires globally (Fig. 3A). Intense fires had a disproportionately large impact, representing just 0.01% of all wildland fires yet accounting for 0.6% of total population exposures (2.7 million people) and 5.0% of the cumulative burned area (2.4 million km²) globally. Similar to all wildland fires, 84.7% of global exposures to intense wildland fires occurred in Africa, while a significantly higher proportion was observed in North America (6.0% for intense fires compared to 1.2% for all wildland fires). Regional impacts of intense fires were pronounced in North America, Oceania and Europe, accounting for 3.1% (14.1%), 2.7% (15.2%), and 2.0% (4.1%) of all population exposures to wildland fire (burned area) in each continent (Fig. S11). See Fig. S12 for the contribution of population dynamics to exposure to intense fires. 

Population exposure to intense fires remained stable globally from 2002-2021 (Fig. 3B), with mixed non-significant trends in different continents (Fig. S11). Global burned area declined both for intense fires (-47.2%; p=0.12) and other fires (-24.9%, p < 0.01) (Figs. 3B,C) in the past two decades. Regional trends, however, showed nuanced differences; for example, burned area by intense fires increased significantly in North America and South America from 2002-2021 (Fig. S11).

In wildfire disaster-prone regions ( _2_ ) – Western North America, the Mediterranean, and Southern Australia, where loss of life and property to wildfires has occurred several times – 253,400 people were exposed to intense fires from 2002-2021, accounting for 32.0%, 4.1%, and 2.8%, of all exposures to wildland fire in each region, respectively (Fig. S13), far in excess of the 0.6% of global exposures to intense fires. Population exposure to intense fires showed non-significant increases in Western North America and Southern Australia from 2002-2021 (Fig. S13).

## **Discussion** 

190 Fires are complex social-ecological phenomena, and using burned area alone – often a 191 common indicator of fire activity – fails to capture their full impact ( _20_ ). The contrasting 192 trends of burned area and exposure to wildland fire indicate multidimensional 193 relationships between fire, humans, and landscapes ( _9_ , _10_ , _25_ , _32_ , _33_ ). Specifically, 

5 

194 land fragmentation ( _24_ ) induced by increasing human presence and expanding 195 commercial agriculture in Africa reduced regional burned area, but co-located more 196 humans with wildland fires – including fire use – and increased their exposure ( _3_ ). 197 Increasing human exposure to wildland fire in North America is due to both expansion of 198 the WUI ( _19_ ) and increasing burned area ( _14_ ), and declining exposures in Europe is 199 due to depopulation of rural areas ( _34_ ), although climatic trends promoted more 200 extreme potential fire behavior in both regions ( _35_ – _37_ ). In South America and Asia, 201 population dynamics increased human exposure to wildland fire, despite a decline in 202 burned areas from 2002-2021. 203 204 We found an increasing co-location of wildland fire and human settlements globally and 205 in every continent. Global exposure density roughly doubled in the past two decades, 206 mostly driven by trends in Africa. These trends are in accord with the expansion of the 207 global WUI ( _22_ , _23_ ). WUI expansion not only places humans at a higher risk to the 208 negative effects of fire ( _19_ ), but also increases the number of anthropogenic ignitions of 209 fire ( _38_ ). 210

Effects of intense fires were particularly significant in North America, Oceania, and Europe coinciding with global hotspots in fire disasters ( _2_ ). Various wildfire mitigation strategies can be implemented in disaster-prone regions to reduce human impacts from fire exposure ( _39_ ), as well as to limit human-caused fires ( _40_ ). In wildfire-prone WUI areas, home hardening—incorporating structural and landscaping modifications to enhance fire resistance—is essential. There is also a need for increased intentional fire use as a vegetation management tool to mitigate wildfire disasters ( _41_ – _43_ ). 

218 

219 Our wildland fire exposure estimates focus solely on direct human exposure and do not 220 account for indirect impacts such as exposure to smoke and postfire debris flow and 221 flood ( _44_ ). As a result, these figures are conservative, and the total number of people 222 affected by wildland fires—whether directly or indirectly—is likely much higher. We note 223 that extreme fire weather and fire behavior are expected to intensify in a warming 224 climate ( _45_ ), and fires are projected to increasingly impact humans especially given the 225 population increases and WUI expansions ( _23_ ). 226 

227 Finally, our analysis indicates that the geography of wildfire disasters is distinctly 228 different from the geography of major human exposures to wildland fires globally ( _2_ ). 229 Most wildfire disasters with the highest fatalities and societal losses has occurred in 230 areas with Mediterranean climates in Australia, Southern Europe, South Africa, Western 231 North America, and the west coast of South America ( _2_ , _26_ , _46_ ). These wildfire disaster 232 regions are not global hotspots in human exposure to wildland fire. By contrast, our 233 analysis found the highest cumulative area burned and the highest human exposure to 

6 

- 234 wildland fire (both cumulative exposure and exposure density) in regions that have had 235 relatively few wildfire disasters, such as in tropical Africa ( _2_ ).
