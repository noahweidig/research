# Mitigation of US wildfires through prescribed burning revealed by four decades of satellite data

## Abstract

Abstract
          In the United States, forest and fire management communities ignite and manage fires under controlled conditions, known as prescribed burning, to prevent the occurrence of large wildfires. Studies on prescribed fires often lack long-term data to validate their effectiveness in reducing wildfires. Using 38-years of satellite observations of burned area, we assess the effect of prescribed fires on wildfires, and the impact on air quality using satellite observations of carbon monoxide (CO) as proxy. We demonstrate evidence of a clear, location-specific causality between prescribed burning and wildfire behavior: 1) areas have fewer wildfires following months of prescribed burning, and 2) prescribed burning helps stop the spread of new wildfires for at least five years, as burned areas act as barriers to fire propagation. Across the western US, we observe minor impact on air quality degradation for areas where both prescribed and wildfires occur. While areas with only wildfires and no prescribed conducted, experience significant air quality degradation. More prescribed burning in the eastern US leads to more significant air quality degradation than those from wildfires, however the overall air pollution levels remain much lower than in the western US. As wildfires are projected to increase in the coming decades, prescribed burning will be crucial in mitigating and reducing the risk of large wildfires and their adverse impact on air quality.

25 Over the recent decades, wildfires are becoming more frequent and intense due to 26 climate change<sup>1,2,3,4,12</sup> and past forest management<sup>62,63,64</sup> , directly impacting human life 27 and vegetation, while indirectly harming public health through smoke-induced air quality 28 degradation<sup>5</sup> . Despite studies highlighting the benefits of using prescribed fires for 29 wildfire reduction, their effectiveness has primarily been evaluated at small spatial and 30 temporal scales<sup>8</sup> . Moreover, many studies rely on permit record datasets rather than 31 observed data<sup>6,7,9,10,11</sup> . Reporting systems can introduce uncertainties, with unexecuted 32 prescribed burnings sometimes inflating annual burned area estimates<sup>13,21,67</sup> . To cross33 validate and improve the accuracy of these reporting systems, satellite observations can 34 play an important role. Satellite measurements not only provide an estimate of the area 35 burned, but also offer comprehensive data coverage, including over areas that are 36 sparsely monitored from the ground.

Fires release greenhouse gases, aerosols, and pollutants such as CO during incomplete combustion<sup>14,15</sup> . CO can persist in the atmosphere for weeks to months, making it a valuable tracer for tracking the transport of pollution and its impact on air quality<sup>16</sup> . Additionally, CO is a precursor of tropospheric ozone, a toxic gas harmful for both public health and vegetation<sup>17,18</sup> . Although CO emissions have decreased due to improvements in combustion efficiency of anthropogenic sources, these decreasing trends are slowing down mainly due to increases in wildfire emissions<sup>19,20</sup> . Therefore, understanding the local impacts on air quality from both wildfire and prescribed fire emissions is essential, especially as wildfire frequency and intensity are expected to rise<sup>59</sup> . 

This study is the first to analyze almost 40-years of wildfire and prescribed burned area data across the Contiguous US (CONUS). The objective is to analyze the effect of prescribed fires on wildfires and to use CO data (for the period 2018-2021) to assess how prescribed burning affects regional air quality. 

Using the 38-year record of burned area from the Monitoring Trends of Burned Severity (MTBS) dataset from 1984 through 2021, we analyze the effect of prescribed fires on wildfires across CONUS (Fig. 1, and SI). We focus our analysis on individual areas (0.25x0.25) in five regions (Fig. 1a), each characterized by different ecosystems and hydro-meteorological regimes. Across CONUS, 1878 areas experienced either only wildfires, prescribed fires, or both over the 38-years. Out of these areas: 1036 only experienced wildfires, 436 only prescribed fires (green in Fig. 1a), and 406 show both, with 297 showing statistically significant correlation (p<0.05). At individual grid cells with only one fire type, no direct effect and interaction can be inferred. Out of the 297 areas, 296 show a negative Spearman’s correlation between monthly wildfires and the 12-month running average of prescribed fires (blue in Fig. 1a) indicating more prescribed burning leads to fewer or no wildfires. Overall, the observed causal relation between prescribed and wildfire burning suggests that increasing prescribed fire use can be an effective strategy for wildfire suppression and reduction across the entire CONUS. Regional examples (Figs 1b-f) show a distinct pattern emerging for all these areas: wildfires tend to occur more frequently when no prescribed burning has been conducted in the 12 months preceding them, while little to no wildfire activity is observed during periods after prescribed burning. In some areas (Fig. 1e), wildfire decline is gradual with increasing prescribed burning. Additional examples can be found in Fig. S5.

The number of people threatened by factors like wildfires is increasing<sup>49,58</sup> , making this relevant. The intensity and severity of prescribed fires are lower than wildfires because they are conducted under moderate environmental conditions, such as high fuel moisture and calm winds, resulting in smaller severity burned area. However, finding these ideal burning conditions has historically been challenging, especially for the western regions with their complex topography and shorter burn windows<sup>31,32,33</sup> . Wildfire CO emissions in the Pacific region equate to 16% of anthropogenic CO emissions. Despite the challenges and negative perceptions associated with prescribed fire in this region, our findings suggest that increasing prescribed burning could be beneficial in multiple ways: (1) reduction in wildfire risk in the succeeding years after burning, (2) reduction of the total area affected by wildfires as prescribed fires create protective barriers and (3) improvement of the overall air quality. Prescribed fires can also enhance forest resilience and sustainable carbon sequestration<sup>68</sup> . This study did not assess the role of prescribed burning on the carbon storage capacity of an ecosystem or its biodiversity, but these aspects could be as important as its impact on reducing wildfire risks and reducing the total area affected by wildfire. We note that strategically placing fuel treatment in landscapes will maximize their effectiveness<sup>71,72</sup> and this work does not take this into account. However, our assessment of prescribed fire effectiveness holds across very large areas and over several decades, thereby providing important results.
