# What goes with the flow: exploring climate impacts on low-flows, their influence on water quality, and instream flow management responses across the United States

## Abstract

Across the United States (US), changes to low-flows in rivers are being driven by interactions between rising temperatures, changing precipitation patterns, and freshwater demand. Increasing frequency, duration, and extremity of low-flows profoundly impact water quality, and consequently aquatic ecosystem health and water supply. However, our understanding of how water quality constituents respond to low-flows regionally is limited by disparate knowledge across disciplines and space. This study leverages literature and expert knowledge to synthesize an interdisciplinary understanding of low-flows’ impact on water quality and aquatic ecosystems, and subsequent implications for national and regional-scale instream flow management. While regional hydrologic differences occur in drivers of low-flows across the US, consistent themes exist in water quality’s response. Low-flows generally result in higher water temperatures, elevated salinity, and lower dissolved oxygen in most studied rivers, with changes in turbidity and nutrients being inconsistent. Low-flow extremes are associated with declining aquatic ecosystem health, including reduced primary production and macro-invertebrate mortality, which can be exacerbated by climate change and other anthropogenic (stream hardening) and natural (high-biota density) factors. Some US regions are underrepresented in the literature regarding links between low-flows and water quality, particularly Hawai’i and US-affiliated Islands (regions with unique water availability and socio-ecological system challenges). Instream flow management prioritizes addressing such challenges, though flow management frameworks vary in both their scale and explicit representation of water quality constituents. Our review provides context about the US’ instream flow management challenges and the importance of water quality’s consideration in managing low-flow regimes.

# 1 Introduction 

Impaired water quality associated with low-flow events (streamflow regimes characterized by limited water supply) presents challenges to both aquatic ecosystem health and the maintenance of water-intensive sectors (e.g., agriculture, municipal, energy). Understanding the ecological degradation of river water quality during low-flows requires knowledge of regional climate change impacts, effects of human driven land use/cover change, and the role of river management decisions and frameworks. Climate change continues to drive shifts in average temperature and rainfall patterns globally (e.g., Konapala et al., 2020), which strongly influence low-flow timing and duration (Simeone et al., 2024) and result in more frequent and extreme severe weather (Kunkel et al., 2014; Armal et al., 2018) around the world (e.g., Clarke et al., 2022). Observed and projected changes in low-flow regimes are thus a partial consequence of climate change; such changes including more frequent flow intermittency (Reynolds et al., 2015; Sauquet et al., 2021) and altered lowflow seasonality (Floriancic et al., 2021). Such changes have multiple associated ecological impacts, such as stream drying events reducing habitat connectivity and interfering with spawning periods (Jaeger et al., 2014; Zipper et al., 2021), and the concentration versus dispersal of river effluent and other pollutants that affect primary production (Saunders et al., 2004; Schoen et al., 2007). 

Within the United States (US), low-flow duration and frequency have historically responded greatly to human influence, with Dudley et al. (2020) noting significant changes in annual 7- day urban low-flows in the last 100 years. Human disturbances to river basins are precisely why Milly et al. (2008) argued that stationarity (natural system behavior within a consistent range of variability) is now an inappropriate assumption for water body assessment, which multiple studies of the nonstationarity of low-flows have since verified (Sadri et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2019; Goswami et al., 2022).This non-stationarity is evident from low-flow trends over the last 85 years. Long-term streamflow metrics in the US show regional trends of increasingly frequent and drier low-flows in the West and Southeast and less frequent and wetter low-flows in the Central Plains, Midwest, and Northeast (US Environmental Protection Agency, 2024; Supplementary Figure SA1; Hammond et al., 2022). Moreover, climate change has been linked to streamflow trends at over twothirds of US monitoring stations (Carlisle et al., 2019), further demonstrating climate change’s role as an important driver of low-flow non-stationarity. 

Climate change has intensified low-flow extremes arising from deforestation, urban sprawl, and rising domestic and industrial water demand (Ledford et al., 2020; Zhang and Wei, 2021; Padowski and Jawitz, 2012). Spatial differences in factors such 

as evapotranspiration, precipitation, and soil moisture lead to distinct regional regimes (Pournasiri Poshtiri et al., 2019). For example, Western US snow-fed river basins have experienced declining snowpack (Siirila-Woodburn et al., 2021), which is associated with more extreme summertime low-flows (Kapnick and Hall, 2012; Kinnard et al., 2022) with implications for streamflow prediction, water supply management, and ecosystem monitoring (Wood et al., 2016; Livneh and Badger, 2020). Knowledge of how regional climate drivers impact low-flows is critical for preserving socio-ecological services. Managing river water quality response to low-flow trends requires interdisciplinary, place-specific strategies that equally value climate change, ecosystem health, and socioeconomic demand (Crausbay et al., 2017). 

Climate-driven water quality degradation during low-flows is largely caused by altered surface runoff and increased concentration of effluent and other suspended or dissolved constituents (Ryberg and Chanat, 2022). These impacts on river water quality may be direct or indirect. For example, a direct impact of drought events is to increase surface water temperatures and consequently decrease dissolved oxygen (DO) content (Whitehead et al., 2009), and an indirect impact is the co-occurrence of drought conditions with the diversion of river water for agricultural and industrial application in (semi-) arid regions, which results in heightened instream effluent concentrations (Sabater et al., 2018). This association between low-flows and reduced river water quality means that common aquatic ecosystem health concerns, such as nutrient loading triggering harmful algal blooms and hypoxia (Mallin et al., 2006; Jarvie et al., 2013), can pose a greater threat during drought. Additionally, local water quality responses to changing low-flow regimes can produce unique river management challenges. For instance, the Middle Rio Grande Basin in central New Mexico can experience increased turbidity when stormwater runoff during times of drought carries excess sediment and forest fire debris into local rivers (Dahm et al., 2015). In contrast, the primary water management challenge to Washington’s Nooksack River is the stress that stream temperature places on salmonid populations, worsened by both rising air temperatures and declining snowpack exacerbating low-flows (Klein et al., 2017; Pelto et al., 2022). These examples demonstrate the region-specificity of management challenges that US rivers face. 

Determining how to manage river water quality in response to climate change and other anthropogenic processes is complicated by the multitude of river water quality constituents (e.g., stream temperature, DO, sediment, salinity, nutrients) relevant to aquatic ecosystem health, as well as the breadth of scales and mechanisms that produce river management challenges. Various river management frameworks and approaches have therefore been created for prescribing river-specific environmental flows or supporting distinct river functions

However, such frameworks’ application for bringing river water quality management into laws and policies can be “piecemeal and uncoordinated” in the US context (Stein et al., 2021), suggesting an opportunity to refine and expand water quality’s incorporation in these frameworks. 

In this paper, we use a purposive, nonsystematic review to describe current knowledge of how climate-influenced lowflows in rivers change water quality across the US, how in turn those water quality changes influence ecosystem health, and how instream flow management has been employed around the US to tackle these challenges. Here we start from the current understanding of climate change’s impacts on low-flow hydrology, and follow with the subsequent effects of low-flows on water quality constituents through their own influence on riverine ecology, and finally to emerging management strategies and frameworks currently used to mitigate those effects (Figure 1). This framework is presented with two caveats. Firstly, this framework serves to illustrate this paper’s progression of addressed topics, and does not suggest a unidirectional relationship of climate change through to management needs. Secondly, low-flows are only one component of instream flow regimes; however, several studies have already explored connections between water quality and high-flows/flow extremes in the US (Towler et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2021; Ryberg and Chanat, 2022). Further, a 2023 solicitation of the National Climate Adaptation Science Center to the Instream Flow Council (IFC) identified the need to “improve the understanding of the connection of flow and water level to water quality” as a pressing instream flow issue; this entails collating place-based examples that convey the relationship between low-flows and river water quality constituent response as we do here. 

This work does not seek to offer generalizable, US-wide relationships between climate change, low-flows, water quality, and instream flow management. This work’s contribution is rather in its identification of regional, basin-scale and river-scale relationships between these processes through examination of place-based studies. Comparing published studies within and across regions illuminates both unique and common management challenges and approaches associated with low-flow regimes, which may be relevant and transferable to river management in less-studied, hydrologically similar regions. As such, we also include examples in this review from data-poor regions that are frequently excluded from national-scale studies, such as Alaska, Hawai’i, and USaffiliated Islands (Kiang et al., 2013), since not doing so would bias our understanding of low flow impacts on ecosystem health to reflect a few well-studied regions. We provide this summary for climatologists, ecologists, hydrologists, and biogeochemists seeking to understand the management challenges and approaches adopted in response to water quality degradation due to changing low-flows.

We chose this type of review to fit the scope of our interdisciplinary research question, the current state of the literature, and the need to incorporate complexity of both ecological mechanisms and disparate terms and metrics used in the literature, as has been recommended (Cook, 2019). We searched Google Scholar and Web of Science using terms related to selected water quality constituents, river features, and terms relating to the research topic (e.g., “climate change”, “dissolved oxygen”, “headwaters”, “river management”, “stream temperature”, “United States”). As in a scoping review (Cook, 2019), we then performed secondary searches as our understanding of the existing literature evolved to follow up on data-poor regions or rivers (e.g., Alaska, Pacific Islands, Colorado River); see Supplementary Table SA1 (for a complete list of all search terms used (also published in Lee et al., 2025)). We selected the following constituents to represent water quality: stream temperature, DO, turbidity, salinity, and nutrients (specifically nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P)). These physical, optical, and chemical properties of water are regularly included in nationallevel programs and products developed by the United States Geological Survey (USGS), such as its ‘National Water Quality Program’ (Erickson et al., 2025) and the ‘National Water Dashboard’ data portal (https://www.usgs.gov/tools/national-water-dashboardnwd). We initially screened additional constituents’ response to low-flows, such as heavy metals, pH, fecal bacteria, chlorophyllɑ, dissolved/particulate organic carbon, and dissolved greenhouse gases, all of which can be important to ecosystems and management decisions and can have low-flow responses; however, we chose to focus on constituents with more robust monitoring and reporting in the literature. While there exist multiple terms, measures, and proxies used for our five chosen constituents, the general terms we have used in this review are defined in Supplementary Table SA2. 

Given our aim to present an overview of the response of water quality constituents to low-flows and to identify national and regional patterns, we include studies regardless of low-flow event driver (e.g., climatic, anthropogenic) and low-flow definition. Furthermore, many articles relevant to our research topic did not use an explicit low-flow definition, and Smakhtin (2001) noted that “low-flow” can be used to describe everything from discharge reduction in all flow regimes to the time and conditions between river flood events. We therefore chose to maintain a wide scope and documented common “low-flow” definitions that were used and encountered in our article search (see Lee et al. (2025) for the lowflow definitions that formed our published catalog of literature). This lack of consistent terminology for what constitutes a low-flow event further justifies our decision to conduct a purposive, nonsystematic review (as opposed to using systematic “Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)” guidelines). Homogenization of literature that does not meet discrete screening criteria, such as a quantitative definition of “low-flow” in an article’s main text, risks removal of relevant literature from disciplines and researchers that analyze low-flows qualitatively (da Silva and Daly, 2024). This allowance for flexible “low-flow” definitions (and our primary and secondary keywords) returned an initial 208 articles, found from March to October 2024. To maintain this review’s focus on contemporary, highly-cited, US-centric literature, these 208 studies were screened for inclusion in this review 

based on the following criteria (this approach is similar to the methodology of Seybold et al. (2022)): 

- Included studies must have been published in peer-reviewed scientific journals, whether research articles or review papers, or have been published as a USGS report. 

- For studies that incorporated climate model-based projections, only those that enlisted Phase 3 or later of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project’s General Circulation Models (GCMs) were included. 

- For process-based studies, those published after the year 2,000 were given preference for inclusion in this review. Articles published in the 20th Century were included if they were either highly-cited (100 citations or more) or if they were deemed foundational to their field. 

- For theoretical works, we included articles with a study domain outside and/or beyond the US, though data-based studies were restricted to US-only to ensure accurate description of low-flow behavior within our selected domain. 

- We excluded works focused solely on lakes (lentic systems), though studies examining flowing (lotic) systems that drain such features were included. 

The studies identified by these criteria were eligible for full review and details from them were input into a database (Lee et al., 2025)) that details each study’s location, spatial scale, river network position, low-flow metric, constituent(s) studied, trend of constituent(s) during low-flow conditions, ecological impacts, and management implications. Our criteria yielded a collection of studies that encompass the geographic range of US water quality management challenges during low-flow events. ### 3.1.1 Stream temperature 

Stream temperatures in the US have risen in recent decades largely due to air temperatures increasing at rates ranging from 0.009 °C to 0.077 °C per year (Kaushal et al., 2010; 

Isaak et al., 2012), comparable to warming trends observed globally (van Vliet et al., 2011). However, cooling stream temperature trends in some parts of the US, such as the Pacific Northwest and Alaska, can be attributed to greater riparian vegetation coverage (Arismendi et al., 2012) or permafrost thaw (Sjöberg et al., 2021). Many studies have concluded that river water temperature tends to be higher during low-flow regimes compared to other instream flow states (van Vliet et al., 2011; Mosley, 2015; Folegot et al., 2018; Stewart et al., 2020; Seo et al., 2022; White et al., 2023). King et al.’s (2016) study of river temperature modeling of Alaska’s Kuparuk River observed that, under low-flow conditions, increased surface area relative to water volume can elevate water temperatures due to increased solar radiation exposure and reduced mixing with cooler water. Furthermore, the low water velocity often associated with low-flow regimes further increases radiation exposure and decreases mixing (Sinokrot and Gulliver, 2000; Asarian et al., 2023), while also trapping more heat near the water surface due to greater thermal stratification (Buxton et al., 2022).

Infrastructural projects such as land development and river impounding can, however, worsen the effects of climate warming on stream temperature, particularly in urban areas during summer low-flows (Stewart et al., 2020; Kelleher et al., 2021; Dittbrenner et al., 2022). Reservoirs can have contrasting effects on stream temperature in the US depending on their location and depth of water release (i.e., surface versus hypolimnion; Rice and Jastram, 2015). Zaidel et al. (2021) showed that small surface release dams across Massachusetts can produce quite large stream temperature increases below the dam level (up to 

5.4 °C), with these effects compounded by impoundment size, solar radiation exposure, lack of riparian shading, and longer water residence times in lentic environments. Conversely, in other systems with regulated flows, such as California’s Sacramento River, releases of cooler water from upstream reservoirs can mitigate extreme temperature changes, illustrating the potential for discharge management strategies to stabilize stream temperature dynamics (Michel et al., 2023). Li et al. (2015) go further by isolating the effect of reservoir operations during low-flows irrespective of their location or depth of release. Their largescale stream temperature model within the Community Earth System Model framework demonstrated that water released from reservoirs during summertime low-flows (August-October) could reduce downstream temperatures by 1 °C–2 °C in rivers across the US. These opposing thermal impacts of reservoirs highlight the complex relationship between river management and stream temperature patterns. 

### 3.1.2 Dissolved oxygen 

Studies both outside (Rajwa-Kuligiewicz et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2021) and within the US (Huggins and Anderson, 2005; Sprague, 2007; Zhi et al., 2023) have documented declining DO concentrations in response to higher temperatures and more extreme low-flows. The use of the SWAT (Soil and Water Assessment Tool) hydrological model driven by GCMs further attests to a link between these DO declines and climate change.

Ficklin et al. (2013) projected DO declines across Sierra Nevada sub-basins in response to rising stream temperature and changing snowmelt timing/volume. Shrestha et al. (2019) also utilized SWAT simulations driven by GCM climate projections to link lower DO levels to reduced summer discharge in Ohio’s Great Miami River. This mechanistic link between DO and stream temperature is a consequence of warmer water having a lower oxygen saturation limit (Cox, 2003; Chapra et al., 2021). However, DO responds to more than just thermal regimes; it is also linked to changes in biogeochemical indicators (e.g., pH, nutrients, salinity) in rivers across the US (Abdul-Aziz and Gebreslase, 2023). This is because excess organic matter and sediment accumulation within rivers raises oxygen demand, so that drivers such as eutrophication triggered by nutrient loading can cause local DO depletion (Radwan et al., 2003). DO decreases when biological demand increases; this is observable at local scales, particularly during low-flow conditions (Mason et al., 2007) as well as on global scales (Blaszczak et al., 2023). 

Place-based studies of the impacts of thermal regimes and/or biological demand on DO saturation during low-flows have been performed in multiple regions. For instance, groundwater-fed creeks in Texas’s Edwards Aquifer have a natural seasonal DO fluctuation that is disrupted by groundwater withdrawal, which is compounded by rising stream temperatures causing further changes in oxygen saturation limit (Mahler and Bourgeais, 2013). Declining discharge volumes due to withdrawal (along with climate change’s projected effects on stream temperature and groundwater recharge) threaten the Aquifer’s endemic Barton Springs salamander. Salmon spawning is also threatened by DO declines, with several creeks in Southeast Alaska exhibiting a sharp summertime DO decline when low-flows coincide with greater spawning density (Fellman et al., 2019). Another example of biological demand’s interplay with DO declines occurs in Oregon’s Klamath River, in which suspended blue-green algae and dissolved organic carbon carry biological oxygen demand downriver and thus accelerate DO depletion in urban areas (Sullivan et al., 2010; McCabe et al., 2021). Organic-rich riverbeds tend to have high sediment oxygen demand due to internal biological and chemical oxidation, a process that causes DO depletion and consequent fish kills particularly during low-flow events, as shown in Texas’ Arroyo Colorado River (Matlock et al., 2003). These studies collectively represent the range of instream flow scales (e.g., creek, river), processes (e.g., biological phenomena, climate change) and locations that can experience DO declines at least partially in response to extreme low-flows. 

Changing DO levels in response to low-flows are, however, driven by more than just climate change and associated biological demand dynamics (Gibson et al., 2005). Elevated DO concentrations over hardened stream sections in Hawai’i (O’ahu) result from consequent disruptions to both sediment deposition and natural low-flow occurrence (Laws and Roth, 2004). Additionally, hypoxic conditions in rivers along the Interstate-85 corridor are common due to harmful algal blooms produced by municipal pollutant runoff from the Southeast US’s rapid urbanization, a problem known to worsen during low-flows (Jackson et al., 2023). DO levels also respond considerably to management during low-flow events. Wehmeyer and Wagner (2011) showed that decisions to flood or drain land behind the Roanoke Rapids Hydroelectric 

Dam (North Carolina) impacts upstream DO concentrations on several-day timescales, creating a risk of violating minimum DO concentration requirements if not accounting for low-flow events (Rakib et al., 2015). Environmental water purchases have also been made as an attempt to mitigate low-DO extremes during the dry season in California’s Walker Basin, in an effort to protect the local aquatic ecosystem (Null et al., 2017). The range of anthropogenic processes that can exacerbate DO extremes during low-flows necessitates management that considers DO impacts. 

### 3.1.3 Salinity 

Increasing salinity, or salinization, of freshwater systems has complex ecological consequences, the management of which still faces multiple knowledge gaps (Kaushal et al., 2021). Three major natural phenomena associated with increased salinization in rivers include: 1) concentration of dissolved solid mass via evapotranspiration and flow volume reduction; 2) enhanced contribution from saline enriched sources such as increased flow contribution from groundwater and salt-secreting riparian vegetation (Glenn et al., 2012); and 3) low-flows enabling upstream movement of salt wedges and saltwater intrusion (Mosley, 2015). Human-driven processes also contribute to local secondary stream salinization, such as irrigating agricultural lands with extracted groundwater (Olson, 2019), application of road salts for deicing and dust suppression (Evans et al., 2018; Hintz and Relyea, 2019), and leakage from mining spoils (Timpano et al., 2018). Climate change amplifies these natural and human drivers of increasing salinity due to rising stream temperatures promoting a greater evaporation rate (Cañedo-Argüelles et al., 2013). 

Globally, freshwater systems typically increase in salinity during low-flows, as often reported in localized assessments of water quality response to drought (Mosley, 2015; van Vliet et al., 2023). In the United States, seasonal salinity patterns vary regionally, generally peaking in summer-fall in Pacific and Gulf Coast rivers and declining in the spring in snowmelt-dominated systems such as the Sierra Nevada and Rocky Mountains (Bolotin et al., 2023). Annual salinity peaks typically coincide with low precipitation and elevated temperatures that limit surface runoff and discharge, though anthropogenic inputs (such as the application of deicers) can produce winter/spring salinity peaks in colder regions (Kaushal et al., 2005). Drought conditions can substantially amplify these effects. For example, in Southern US rivers, median salinity increases by 21% during drought conditions, with the majority of sites having statistically significantly (p < 0.05) higher salinity during drought compared to non-drought periods (Jones and van Vliet, 2018). In coastal systems, lowflows can drive salinity increases by enabling upstream migration of saltwater and altering estuarine gradients, with additional influence from large-scale atmospheric circulation patterns (Schulte et al., 2017; Petkewich et al., 2019). The US Army Corps of Engineers has responded by installing sills that slow salt wedge migration in the Mississippi River Basin, thereby protecting freshwater supply (McAnally and Pritchard, 1997). 

The need for river management posed by elevated salinity during low-flows is frequently spurred by the subsequent threat to socio-ecological services. Extended periods and/or fluctuations of high and low salinity can cause stress and mortality to even the

Acute or chronic salinization can create uninhabitable conditions for salinity-sensitive species like freshwater mussels (Johnson et al., 2018) and turtles (Mahan et al., 2022), as well as more tolerant species like oysters and seagrass (Lee et al., 2026). Semi-arid estuaries have also experienced decreases in white shrimp and blue crab populations as flow reductions prompt invasion of salinity-tolerant species (Palmer and Montagna, 2015). 

Rising sea levels also exacerbate low-flows by limiting municipal freshwater intake in estuaries, a threat posed to drinking water supply in the Chesapeake Bay (Rice et al., 2012) and to oyster populations and commercial catches in Louisiana’s Breton Sound (La Peyre et al., 2009; La Peyre et al., 2013). The most common management approach for high salinity is freshwater release from upstream to dilute instream water and push back salt fronts. Such strategies to reduce instream salinity can increase groundwater salinity; basin transfer of saline drainage waters away from agricultural soils is thus sometimes practiced in closed basins to protect soils and groundwater from salinization (Suarez, 1989). In coastal regions, the Coastal Salinity Index has been enlisted to monitor long-term dynamics of the fresh/saltwater interface and thus alert managers of the early onset of drought (Nolan et al., 2016; Conrads and Darby, 2017; Petkewich et al., 2019). This index is useful especially when low-flows affect coastal instream flows due to drought focused further upstream. The management of aquatic ecosystems vulnerable to high salinity can also stress resources due to the fact that elevated salinity also challenges municipal and agricultural water usage (Jones and van Vliet, 2018), which have historically taken management priority. 

### 3.1.4 Turbidity 

Turbidity is an optical characteristic of water that measures water clarity, by which the presence of suspended and dissolved materials (e.g., clay, silt, plankton, microscopic organisms, organic and inorganic matter) causes scattering of incident light (Bash et al., 2001). Globally, low turbidity is observed in rivers and streams during droughts due to insufficient catchment runoff, which gives sediments enough time to settle due to low water velocity (Tunnicliff and Brickler, 1984; Caruso, 2001; Caruso, 2002; Golladay and Battle, 2002; van Vliet and Zwolsman, 2008; Hrdinka et al., 2012; Mosley et al., 2012). However, increased turbidity can also be observed during droughts if a point source of suspended load into a river or stream drives the subsequent decline in water clarity (Mosley, 2015), such as a high input of sediment and organic matter from connected lakes and tributaries (e.g., algal accumulation in the Minnesota River Basin’s Elm Creek; Lenhart et al., 2010). Mountainous regions with dry climates show high fluctuations in turbidity during low-flows, such as the Lower Colorado and Great Basins, whereas the Southeast US and East Coast have displayed less fluctuation, due to humid regions often having less sediment available to transport than arid sites due to vegetative cover (Ahmadi et al., 2019; Murphy, 2020). These studies suggest that the magnitude and direction of changing turbidity during lowflows depends on point versus non-point sources of organic material release into rivers, and on regional US geographies. 

The regional geographies that influence turbidity’s relationship with low-flows depend on more than just topography and material 

source type. For instance, downstream river reaches tend to experience higher turbidity due to the typical increase in flow volume and drainage area (Lenhart, 2008; Lenhart et al., 2010). Water depth is another important local driver of turbidity, with shallower water (due to low-flows or otherwise) frequently associated with sediment (re)suspension and thus increased turbidity in both lentic and lotic ecosystems (Lathrop, 2007; Ludovisi and Gaino, 2010; Olds et al., 2011; Mosley et al., 2012; Mosley, 2015). Shallower water during low-flows is also more susceptible to sediment (re)suspension due to wind at the water’s surface mixing benthic sediments (Carper and Bachmann, 1984; Luettich et al., 1990; Olds et al., 2011). Landscape type also influences local turbidity responses. For example, wetlands and lakes supply organic matter that raises the turbidity of adjacent streams in the Minnesota River Basin’s prairie pothole landscape, contributing to adverse conditions for aquatic organisms during summer low-flow conditions (Lenhart et al., 2010). 

The mechanisms that impact turbidity during low-flows can be highly localized and are thus challenging to extrapolate even within US regions. In the Midwest, some low-flow events have been accompanied by increased turbidity (e.g., Nebraska’s Harlan County Reservoir experiences tripled turbidity levels during July-August in drought years compared to normal years; Olds et al., 2011), while others are accompanied by decreased turbidity (e.g., Illinois’ water supply system experienced reduced turbidity during a 1930s drought that induced low-flows across the state; Armstrong, 1931; Mosley, 2015). The localized drivers and inconsistent direction of change pose management challenges for turbidity. It has been proposed that a holistic approach can manage turbidityimpaired streams by enabling region-specific strategies that mitigate the deposition and suspension of sediment and nutrient-rich agricultural runoff (Lenhart et al., 2010). 

### 3.1.5 Nutrients 

Nutrients like nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) predominantly demonstrate small or inconsistent responses during droughts and heatwaves at a global scale (van Vliet et al., 2023); these complex patterns are similarly reflected in US low-flow literature. Point sources of nutrients (such as P from septic systems) tend to produce increased nutrient concentrations during low-flows due to lack of dilution and constant inputs (Macintosh et al., 2011). Conversely, nonpoint or diffuse nutrient sources (such as ammonia and soluble reactive P from agriculture) tend to decrease in concentration during low-flows because of their reduced runoff from reduced land-water connectivity (Golladay and Battle, 2002). The processes that impact nutrient concentration during low-flows are often heterogeneous, even within the same basin. Smith and Jarvie’s (2018) study of the tile-drained landscape of Indiana’s Western Lake Erie Basin observed extreme low-flows (lowest 5<sup>th</sup> percentile) leading to N depletion relative to P and carbon (C) in two sites, and P depletion relative to N and C in another. These relationships between relative nutrient concentrations during low-flow events can place pressure on aquatic ecosystems. 

The drivers of the heterogeneous responses of nutrients to low-flows are numerous and exist on multiple spatial scales. Graham et al.’s (2024) global review of 314,046 water quality stations from 1980 to 2021 showed that, under drought conditions, median

Such interactions between hydrology and biogeochemistry may also control nutrient processing. Similar to turbidity, the concentration, distribution, and characteristics of nutrients during low-flows lack overarching trends, with changes in both being sensitive to point versus non-point source origin, rate of runoff, regional land topography, and urban versus rural land use. 

The inconsistent response of nutrient concentrations during low-flows further highlights the need for targeted nutrient management to protect ecosystem health. For instance, the prevalence of Cyanobacterial Harmful Algal Blooms (CyanoHABs) in rivers is often favored by prolonged drought (and by extension low-flows) following intense rainfall events, a problem made worse by overapplication of fertilizer (Paerl et al., 2016). However, simply reducing fertilizer application to manage CyanoHABs is insufficient due to the interconnectedness of riverine systems and their associated water bodies, such is the case for Oregon’s Klamath River, parts of which are naturally eutrophic (Paerl et al., 2018). As such, assessing nutrient management strategies requires more than point measurements of concentration and flux; spatially integrated metrics can better capture basin-scale risk. One such example is the Water Quality Risk Index (WQRI) developed by SchafferSmith et al. (2023) for North Carolina’s Cape Fear River Basin. This index combined nutrient variability and intensity calculated over multiple sub-basins to construct spatially continuous WQRI scores for the entire basin, which revealed the importance of managing the Cape Fear Basin’s point nutrient sources during low-flows. Jackson et al. (2023) provide multiple examples of how nutrient loading in streams is being managed across the Southeast US, such as aerator installation and city-level “Low Inflow Protocols” that recommend specific management approaches (e.g., voluntary 

conservation, reservoir release) in response to low-flows. Given strong basin-specific controls on nutrient dynamics, effective management must be similarly tailored and coordinated at the watershed scale, targeting both N and P and accounting for upstream inputs and reservoir operations that directly affect downstream water quality (Paerl et al., 2018; Summers and Ryder, 2023).

# 4 An opportunity for better managing low-flow regimes in the United States 

As shown by our review, the increased frequency and magnitude of low-flows generally impairs water quality. Certain regions, particularly the Western US (Siirila-Woodburn et al., 2021), the Southeast US (Stephens and Bledsoe, 2020), and Hawai’i (Clilverd et al., 2018), have experienced negative water quality impacts due to the increasing prevalence of low-flow extremes. Such worsening extremes and knowledge of their association with water quality constituents raises the need for place-based management approaches that mitigate and adapt to these changes in aquatic ecosystems. In this section of the paper, we contextualize the opportunity for improving management of water quality during low-flow regimes by discussing each of our studied constituent’s impacts on aquatic ecosystem health (Section 4.1) and the need for flow management frameworks that explicitly incorporate river water quality under a changing climate (Section 4.2).

The effects of turbidity and nutrient concentrations on aquatic ecosystem health are closely related due to their effects on aquatic primary production. The associated effects of these two constituents on aquatic primary production provide a focal point for localized river management strategy. 

## 4.2 Incorporating water quality into instream flow management frameworks in a changing climate 

Despite the various regional mechanisms that influence water quality during low-flows, climate change poses some consistent flow management challenges across the US. The regional timing and duration of annual low-flows vary climatologically, but the expected future trend across most of the US’ river basins is decreased lowflow magnitude; this is particularly true of basins fed by snowmelt (Milly and Dunne, 2020). In multiple studies, GCMs have projected decreases in low-flow river volumes (Demaria et al., 2016; Maurer and Duffy, 2005), and climate change appears likely to exacerbate low-flow management issues presented in this review. If decreased low-flow magnitude and increased duration (Demaria et al., 2016) is the future of US rivers, our review suggests that increasing stream temperature and salinity and decreasing DO could occur in most river basins (Figure 3; Table 2). However, the regional interplay of climatic, hydrological, ecological, and anthropogenic factors provides nuance for individual river basins, highlighting the pressing need for localized studies that recommend optimal water quality and quantity management strategies (see Figure 4 for examples of regional management challenges). Such studies (e.g., Cravens et al., 2024) are an opportunity to share stories 

about management successes across basins that face similar lowflow and water quality challenges; a selection of these challenges is illustrated in Supplementary Figure SA2. 

Flow management strategies (e.g., scheduled discharges to mitigate thermal stratification, limits on deicer applications to limit seasonal peaks in salinity) are widely used across the US to address water quality challenges during low-flows. A key tool for evaluating these strategies is the use of flow management frameworks. Most flow management frameworks predominantly focus on representing water quantity, whereas water quality is represented to varying degrees. Here, we examine three commonly used flow management frameworks (Ecological Limits of Hydrologic Alteration (ELOHA), Functional Flows (FF), and Instream Flow Incremental Methodology (IFIM)) to assess how water quality is represented. These examples represent only a few of the potential approaches to flow management frameworks compiled by the IFC (Annear et al., 2004), but allow an examination of different strengths and uses of such frameworks (see Supplementary Table SA4 for a comparative assessment of the three frameworks; see Annear et al., 2004 for detailed information about each framework in a management context). 

The ELOHA framework conceptually integrates environmental flows into regional water planning and management in four steps: building a hydrologic foundation of streamflow data, classifying natural river types, determining flow-ecology relationships for each river type, and implementing policy to achieve river condition goals (Poff et al., 2010). These goals can be used to form the basis of river management priorities (e.g., minimum river discharge volume, maintaining fisheries, supporting riparian vegetation) by prescribing environmental flow standards, which Acreman (2016) defines as “the quantity, quality and timing of water flows required to sustain freshwater and estuarine ecosystems and the human livelihoods and wellbeing that depend on these ecosystems.” Multiple studies have demonstrated ELOHA’s value for prescribing environmental flow standards, including low-flow thresholds in the Potomac (Buchanan et al., 2013) and Susquehanna River Basins (Zhang et al., 2016). There is a growing call to incorporate water quality in ELOHA-based approaches (e.g., Troy et al., 2023). While some studies have demonstrated the feasibility of integrating nonstationarity and climate scenarios (St-Hilaire et al., 2021) and metrics such as stream temperature (Lapides et al., 2022) within the ELOHA framework, these applications are relatively rare, highlighting an opportunity to better couple hydrology and water quality within ELOHA-based approaches. 

Another commonly used flow management framework is FFs, which use gauge observations to construct natural flow regimes that identify key ecological and geomorphic processes (Yarnell et al., 2020). These regimes may be depicted in the form of a hydrograph that illustrates the seasonal flow components (e.g., wetseason baseflow, fall pulse flow) that support ecosystem functions and inform adaptive management strategies (Yarnell et al., 2015). Studies of FF applications for river management within the US have focused heavily on California. Stein et al. (2021) discussed the development of the California Environmental Flows Framework, demonstrating its ability to recommend minimum and maximum standards for flow regime duration, magnitude, and timing necessary to support and restore aquatic ecological functions. The pressure that climate change places on worsening low-flow extremes (and consequently on aquatic ecosystem health) will necessitate the development of flow management frameworks that recommend both place-specific and basin-wide approaches to managing river water quality. Thus, the combination of existing and new flow management frameworks will play a necessary role in meeting the river water quality challenges the US currently faces, requiring close collaboration between scientists and water managers for optimal framework development. 

# 5 Limitations of the review 

This review’s assessment of changes to river water quality constituents and ecosystem health, the impacts of climate change on low-flows, and the state of instream flow management are based on the current state of the literature and are thus subject to the following limitations. 

- Studies of low-flow impacts to water quality are not uniformly distributed and are lacking especially in Pacific and US-affiliated Islands; therefore Figures 3, 4 are more representative of lowflow impacts for the CONUS, particularly in coastal states. This uneven spatial coverage limits our ability to offer US-wide generalizations about relationships between climate change, low-flows, water quality, and instream flow management. 

However, this paper’s discussed regional drivers and impacts present opportunities for scientists and managers to exchange and transfer river management strategies into less-studied, hydrologically similar contexts. 

- We intentionally included multiple low-flow definitions and indicators to strengthen regional representation and the number of included constituents. This decision also accounted for the interdisciplinary context of this study; whereas hydrological studies may take model-based approaches to represent lowflows statistically (e.g., 7Q10, the annual minimum 7-day mean flow with a 10-year recurrence interval), ecological studies may instead characterize low-flows based on a threshold of impacts to ecosystem services. However, doing so limited our ability to draw conclusions or comparisons about how specific lowflow conditions influence water quality constituents, in other words, this study does not attempt to provide average national quantifications of stream temperature increases in response to specific low-flow thresholds. Mechanisms by which water quality responds to low-flow regimes were thus presented at regional and smaller spatial scales, with identified patterns (e.g., the response of turbidity to urban versus rural land types) being transferable only with context of their local geographies and enlisted low-flow definition(s). 

- We also acknowledge the many other riverine constituents critical to ecological function but not represented in this review. Stream temperature, DO, salinity, turbidity, and nutrients are widely used for monitoring by many regulatory and environmental agencies and data on them are thus prevalent enough to synthesize here (though studies relevant to these five constituents may have been excluded due to our chosen terminology and search approach). 

- It should also be mentioned that the mixed relationship that turbidity and nutrients have with low-flow regimes may in part be a consequence of our purposive, nonsystematic approach to qualitatively analyzing published literature. A deeper quantitative analysis of the drivers that produce these relationships would assist in clarifying these relationships for different geographic regions and scales, though such analysis extends beyond the scope of this study, as would increased data collection focusing on these relationships. As such, conclusions drawn about the relationships that turbidity and nutrients possess with changing low-flow regimes in the US are confined to observed patterns in regional and local mechanisms (e.g., point versus non-point sources, urban versus rural environments, mountainous versus flat terrain), with no overarching trends in the constituents themselves asserted. 

- Finally, place-based examples in this study are not representative of entire states or regions of the US. The regions indicated in Figure 2 follow arbitrary political boundaries that may not best represent the diversity and locations of aquatic ecosystems; hence, regional-scale interpretations of relationships between water quality and low-flows presented here should be interpreted with ecosystem-specific knowledge. We call for an increase in studies of low-flow impacts on river water quality and ecosystem function in understudied regions of the US, particularly in interior states and on islands. # 6 Conclusion 

The impacts of climate change on temperature and rainfall extremes have combined with myriad anthropogenic factors to observably alter low-flow regimes across the US, thus impacting river water quality and associated aquatic ecosystem health. In this paper, we set out to describe the current state of knowledge of these impacts and to show how instream flow management has and can be better used to face these challenges. Our review showed that low-flows are consistently associated with higher stream temperatures nationwide, particularly in urbanized areas and in Western US rivers. Higher water temperatures mean lower oxygen saturation limits; hence declining DO concentrations often accompany lower low-flows, worsened by local contributors like groundwater withdrawal, biological demand, and organicrich riverbeds. Elevated salinity during low-flows creates further difficulties for ecological, municipal, and agricultural water usage. Observed changes in turbidity and nutrient concentration are mixed and are often dependent on point versus diffuse source origins. Turbidity’s response to low-flows is sensitive to topographic relief, river network position, water depth, wind, and organic matter loading. Nutrient concentrations from point sources are typically higher during low-flows, whereas concentrations from non-point sources are generally lower and are sensitive to land-water connectivity, runoff rates, and rural versus urban settings. 

Our review also identified water quality management challenges, strategies, and solutions during low-flows. Declining aquatic ecosystem health is of particular concern. If model-projected declines of low-flows transpire and reductions of low-flow magnitude continue, then thermal stratification, hypoxic stagnant water, impeded light penetration, invasion of salt-tolerant species, and many other ecological impacts could increase in US rivers. These ecological risks are sometimes river and region-specific, with localized case studies able to showcase successful river management practices. Such case studies may facilitate collaboration to address similar low-flow management challenges within and across US regions. Sharing of management practices is aided by flow management frameworks that guide the sustainable use and development of river ecosystems. However, the flow management frameworks presented in this review show that the spatial scales of their application and their explicit incorporation of water quality are both highly variable. We therefore suggest that scientists seeking to understand river management priorities collaborate with managers to develop and use frameworks that enlist water quality measures, thus better informing the long-term resilience of river ecosystems and socio-ecological services. 

Through its novel interdisciplinary approach to better understand connections between low-flows, water quality, and management issues across the US using place-based examples, this review also revealed a paucity of available data about water quality constituent response to low-flows, especially for Hawai’i, 

US-affiliated Islands, and inland regions of the CONUS. These spatial gaps in data coverage limit our ability to make robust conclusions, such as for turbidity and nutrient response to low-flow regimes, but they also represent the potential for the transferability of river management challenges and solutions to other, hydrologically similar regions. For instance, the river management strategies employed in heavily-studied regions, such as Texas’s coastal estuaries (see Figure 2), may be applicable to less-studied estuaries along the Gulf Coast, creating a potential for transferal of knowledge and expertise between scientists and managers. This work captures the current state of this potential and advocates for collaborative, creative approaches to the management of changing low-flow regimes. 

More studies of the interlinkages between climate change, lowflows, water quality, and river management in under-studied regions are nevertheless required. We therefore propose nationwide and location-based gap analyses to identify needed future studies. Our work has shown that necessary future work includes (a) modelbased climate projections of low-flow extremes and their water quality impacts, (b) spatial analyses of low-flow and water quality problem hotspots that inform river management, (c) increased monitoring station coverage in data-poor regions, (d) attribution of the relative contribution of different natural versus anthropogenic (e.g., reservoir operations, de-icer application, effluent release) processes to changes in low-flow regimes, and (e) incorporation of water quality into flow management frameworks. Balancing flow regimes to support national water security, productive agriculture, and ecosystem health depends on more robust representation and management of water quality constituent response to low-flows, particularly as climate change exacerbates current challenges for managers, ecosystem health, and society more broadly.
