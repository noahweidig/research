# Sugarcane burning: a systematic review of emissions, monitoring approaches, and environmental and health impacts

## Abstract

The sugarcane industry is a major economic driver in South Africa supporting regional employment. However, traditional pre-harvest sugarcane burning releases hazardous air pollutants including fine particulate matter (PM 2.5 ), black carbon carbon monoxide (CO), and nitrogen dioxide (NO 2 ), as well as greenhouse gases carbon dioxide (CO 2 ), methane (CH 4 ), and nitrous oxide (N 2 O). This systematic review evaluates global research on sugarcane burning emissions, Earth observation tools, atmospheric transport models, and associated human health risks. Following PRISMA 2020 guidelines, a multi-database search across Scopus and Web of Science yielded 177 primary empirical studies. The synthesis reveals that research capacity is heavily concentrated in the United States (n = 48) and Brazil (n = 29), whereas South Africa (n = 5) remains understudied. Methodological analysis shows that unadjusted global fire inventories underestimate sugarcane emissions due to coarse spatial resolution and uncalibrated fuel loads. Furthermore, clinical and occupational studies document significant harvester health risks, including acute kidney injury (AKI) driven by heat strain and toxic silica ash exposure. This paper highlights the critical need to integrate high-resolution multi-sensor satellite observations, localised crop-specific emission factors, and rural health surveillance to address air quality monitoring gaps in data-scarce regions.

## 1 Introduction 

Agriculture plays a pivotal role in food security. Approximately 2.5 billion people globally, 60% of whom live in developing nations, rely heavily on rainfed agriculture for food (Balgah et al., 2023; Ngoma et al., 2021) and income (Arumugam et al., 2021). The global population is projected to grow by 2.3 billion by 2050 (Food and Agriculture Organization, 2009; Ngoma et al., 2021; Tilman et al., 2011), and consequently, the population in southern Africa is expected to grow by 241 million from 224 million (Mutengwa et al., 2023). This implies that agricultural production also needs to be significantly increased to meet rising demand and ensure long-term food security (Paudel et al., 2023), placing the sector at the centre of Sustainable Development Goal 2 (SDG 2) (Asim et al., 2024). Across sub-Saharan Africa, agriculture contributes approximately 15%–17% of regional GDP and employs nearly half of the rural workforce (FAO, 2018; Mo Ibrahim Foundation, 2022). 

Sugarcane ( _Saccharum officinarum_ ) is cultivated in over 100 countries across 22 million hectares, yielding between 1.6 and 1.9 billion tonnes annually. Brazil, India, and China dominate global production, while South Africa ranks among the top 15 global producers, contributing roughly 1% of global sugarcane output (FAO, 2023). In South Africa, sugarcane farming is concentrated in the KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga provinces (Shikwambana et al., 2021). Commercial large-scale growers produce approximately 69% of local sugarcane, smallholder farmers produce 11.2%, and miller-cum-planter estates produce the remaining share (Chipfupa and Tagwi, 2024; SASA, 2020). The sector generates an average annual income of approximately R24 billion (roughly US1.3 billion), supporting over 85,000 direct jobs and 350,000 indirect livelihoods in rural communities (Baiyegunhi and Arnold, 2011; SASA, 2020). 

Despite its economic importance, traditional sugarcane harvesting relies heavily on pre-harvest field burning to clear excess leafy trash, facilitate manual cutting, and deter venomous fauna (Leite et al., 2018; SASA, 2020). Open field combustion releases hazardous air pollutants including fine particulate matter (PM2.5), coarse particles (PM10), black carbon, carbon monoxide (CO), and nitrogen oxides (NOx) along with greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide (N2O) (Leite et al., 2018; Molefe et al., 2026; Nowell et al., 2022; Paudel et al., 2023; Saleem et al., 2025). On a global scale, the broader Agriculture, Forestry, and Other Land Use (AFOLU) sector accounts for 21%–24% of total global greenhouse gas emissions (Nabuurs et al., 2022), contributing through synthetic fertilizer applications (Meemken and Qaim, 2025). 

It is critical to distinguish harvesting practices: Pre-harvest sugarcane burning versus unburnt green cane harvesting. Burnt cane is routinely harvested mechanically in major bioenergy regions such as Brazil, whereas unburnt green cane can be cut manually by hand harvesters (Leite et al., 2018). Furthermore, pre-harvest leaf burning differs significantly from post-harvest trash or residue burning in fuel moisture, combustion completeness, smoke density, and chemical composition (Dattamudi et al., 2020; Santiago-De la Rosa et al., 2017). 

Inhaling sugarcane smoke exposes farm workers and nearby rural communities to severe public health risks (Nowell et al., 2022; Santos et al., 2015; Stem et al., 2024). Clinical and epidemiological research connects sugarcane smoke exposure to elevated pediatric respiratory hospital admissions, reduced birth weight, acute kidney injury (AKI) driven by physical heat strain and toxic silica ash, and chronic kidney disease of non-traditional origin (CKDu) (D’Errico 

et al., 2025; Leite et al., 2018; Nowell et al., 2022; Santos et al., 2015; Stem et al., 2024). 

This systematic literature review evaluates global progress in monitoring, Modelling, and assessing sugarcane burning. The objectives of this review are to (1) map global research trends and geographic gaps in sugarcane burning and air quality literature using bibliometric analysis, (2) synthesize Earth observation tools, chemical transport models, machine learning downscaling, and ground monitoring networks used to track smoke plumes, (3) evaluate public and occupational health risks associated with pre-harvest burning of sugarcane, and (4) identify key operational bottlenecks and research gaps in monitoring sugarcane burning emissions in South Africa. 

## 2 Methodology 

### 2.1 Literature search and article extraction 

The systematic selection (Table 1) of research articles for this review followed a strict PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and MetaAnalyses) 2020 methodology, which is a standardized guideline that helps researchers to extract relevant articles aligned with their areas of interest while ensuring transparent and reliable results. This approach also makes it possible to assess the validity and quality of existing research using a set of criteria, which helps reveal weaknesses, inconsistencies, and contradictions (Paré et al., 2015). The primary search was executed across two major bibliographic databases: Scopus and Web of Science. Structured Boolean query strings anchored the search around sugarcane cultivation, agricultural fire emissions, Earth observation tools, and health risks. Search strings combined population terms (sugarcane, Saccharum, pre-harvest burning, crop residue burning), exposure and methodological terms (remote sensing, satellite, TROPOMI, MODIS, VIIRS, WRF-Chem, CMAQ, emission factor, machine learning), and outcome terms (PM2.5, PM10, black carbon, NO2, respiratory, renal, mortality, exposure). The publication window was set to 2015–2025. 

The multi-database search yielded a total of 3,359 records (1,002 from Scopus and 2357 from Web of Science). An automated and manual deduplication pipeline using DOI matching and title normalization removed 1,094 duplicate records, leaving 2265 articles for initial screening. During the title and abstract screening phase, 1978 articles were excluded for failing to meet the core focus of sugarcane burning, air pollution, or exposure science. Out of 287 full-reports sought for retrieval, 12 could not be obtained, leaving 275 full-articles for formal eligibility assessment.

### 3.3 Spatial distribution of publications 

Global spatial distribution of the top ten research locations (Figure 4) reveals a pronounced geographical imbalance in the application of Earth observation, atmospheric modelling, and exposure assessment to sugarcane pre-harvest burning. Studies were categorised based on primary study area and regional focus to accurately reflect where empirical field monitoring and smoke tracking take place. Publication activity is heavily concentrated within a small number of technologically advanced economies and heavily regulated sugarcane production regions. The United States leads global scientific output with 48 publications, driven by extensive atmospheric observation infrastructure, highdensity ground monitoring networks, and intensive field campaigns (e.g., FIREXAQ) evaluating sugarcane emissions in the Florida Everglades (Dattamudi et al., 2020; Nowell et al., 2022; Pouliot et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2021). 

Brazil represents the second largest research center with 29 publications, reflecting widespread scientific evaluation of São Paulo State’s Protocolo Agroambiental (Green Protocol) and the environmental, agronomic, and health impacts of transitioning from 

manual burned harvesting to mechanical green harvesting (Du et al., 2018; Leite et al., 2018; Mataveli et al., 2017; Saiani and Perosa, 2016; Santos et al., 2015; Valente and Laurini, 2021). China and Thailand follow with 14 publications each, driven by regional policy imperatives to quantify crop residue emissions and evaluate field burning bans using multi-sensor satellite retrievals (e.g., TROPOMI, GEMS, Sentinel-2) and machine learning (Bahşi et al., 2023; Chaya, 2024; Junpen et al., 2020; PNVR and Bandaru, 2023; Poortinga et al., 2021; Saiani and Perosa, 2016; Shen et al., 2021). Additional contributions originate from India (n = 9), Mexico (n = 8), Nicaragua (n = 6), France (n = 5), and global multi-country modelling studies (n = 5). This spatial distribution highlights a misalignment between the global distribution of sugarcane cultivation and local research capacity. 

While high-income nations and major bio-energy producers generate robust, localised exposure datasets, many key sugarcaneproducing regions across the Global South remain significantly under-represented in the atmospheric literature. South Africa, for example, accounts for only 5 documented studies, and only three address sugarcane burning in South Africa, despite ranking among the world’s top 15 sugarcane producers, with annual production exceeding 18–20 million tonnes concentrated across the KwaZuluNatal and Mpumalanga provinces (SASA, 2020). ## 4 Methodological analysis 

### 4.1 Techniques and technologies used in the studies 

Research methods across the synthesised corpus (N = 177) are grouped into operational broad research categories (Figure 6) to show the distribution of the scientific landscape. Supplementary Table S1 shows studies showing location, methods, pollutants and 

key findings. Each category is assigned an explicit operational definition and decision rule to prevent overlap: Quantitative and Statistical Analysis represents primary empirical evaluations of spatial-temporal trends; Computational and Atmospheric Modelling encompasses 3D chemical transport, machine learning, and health exposure models; Mixed-Methods and Multi-Proxy Integration covers studies combining two or more distinct data streams; Laboratory and Field Experimental Trials includes direct physical testing, emission factor measurements, and clinical cohorts; and Qualitative and Policy Reviews covers regulatory, gap, and narrative reviews Figure 7 further illustrates the spatial distribution approaches employed across the studies. Quantitative and statistical analysis accounts for 38% of the synthesized literature (n = 67), serving as the primary analytical framework for evaluating spatiotemporal pollutant trends, crop residue fire extents, and community health burdens driven by seasonal sugarcane pre-harvest burning. For instance, Yin et al. (2019) deployed Theil–Sen median trend analysis to evaluate decadal biomass burning impacts across mainland Southeast Asia, while Mokgoja et al. (2023) used the non-parametric Sequential Mann–Kendall (SQMK) test to evaluate trace gas trends (SO2, NO2, CO) and meteorological drivers in Durban, South Africa. Remote sensing burn assessments within this quantitative category frequently leverage cloud-computing platforms; Singh et al. (2022) and Bahşi et al. (2023) utilized Google Earth Engine (GEE) to compute the dNBR (Differenced Normalized Burn Ratio), mapping out the expansion of forest fires and the resultant residue. Ponomarev et al. (2021) and Wiggins et al. (2021) used FRP (Fire Radiative Power) to estimate annual carbon emissions and evaluate the accuracy of fire emissions inventories. In epidemiological and health research within this quantitative category, Souza et al. (2016) and Ramos et al. (2019) applied Poisson and logistic regression models to establish statistically significant associations between daily PM10 and NO2 spikes from pre-harvest sugarcane burning and pediatric hospitalizations for pneumonia and asthma in São Paulo, Brazil. ### 4.4 _In situ_ data sugarcane and biomass burning 

_In situ_ measurements are widely used to validate remote sensing observations and atmospheric model outputs. Studies included in this review revealed that there are significant discrepancies between satellite and _in situ_ sensors. One such example is the study by Bittner et al. (2022), which found that low-cost ARISense sensors recorded carbon monoxide concentrations two to four times higher than surface estimates derived from MOPITT satellite observations and the MERRA-2 reanalysis dataset in rural Malawi. Real-time air quality monitoring during pre-harvest field fires demonstrates extreme, short-term particulate spikes that can be otherwise missed by global sensors (Le Blond et al., 2017; Stem et al., 2024). Personal breathing-zone sampling on sugarcane harvesters reveals severe occupational hazard levels during manual cutting, with deployed sample pumps recording (1.9 mg/m<sup>3</sup> ), black carbon (13.7 μg/m<sup>3</sup> ), endotoxins, and carcinogenic crystalline silica ash (D’Errico et al., 2025). Ground burning of post-harvest crop residue releases 1.63 times more PM2.5 particles than standing pre-harvest field burns, with the shorter flaming stage emitting the finest particles (Dattamudi et al., 2020).

It is also worth noting that regional transport studies rely heavily on _in-situ_ data, as demonstrated by Wu et al., 2018, highlighting southern transport as the main driver of air quality conditions in Beijing, China. In contrast, when examining vertical profiles using the Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) model, Mathur et al. (2018) found that observations at an altitude of 400 m are up to four times more effective at characterizing surface daily maximum 8-h average ozone (DM8O3) variability than a fourfold increase in traditional ground-level monitoring site density. _In situ_

Lastly, Zhou et al. (2021) demonstrated the effectiveness of integrated sensing by validating high-resolution carbon monoxide plume retrievals through airborne ultra-spectral measurements. 

### 4.5 Reanalysis data for sugarcane and biomass burning 

Research on reanalysis and data integration emphasizes the significance of long-term, spatially continuous datasets for providing decadal-scale insights and evaluating the performance of air quality models. These datasets are critical for understanding the dispersion and accumulation of pollutants originating from sugarcane burning, particularly in regions with limited groundbased monitoring. Similarly, the work of Mokgoja et al. (2023) in Durban serves as a vital regional template for sugarcane research by combining MERRA-2 with TROPOMI and OMI datasets to reveal that meteorological conditions can often have a greater influence on pollutant levels than changes in emission sources. Warm surface temperatures, low relative humidity, and suppressed planetary boundary layer heights restrict vertical smoke dispersion, trapping black carbon and sulphur dioxide near the ground during harvest months (Kganyago and Shikwambana, 2019; Shikwambana et al., 2021). In Pakistan, Shahid et al. (2025) analyzed a 2021 haze event using MERRA-2 and ceilometer LIDAR data, identifying a decline in planetary boundary layer height as a key factor driving the accumulation of surface sulphate and black carbon. This finding is relevant to sugarcane burning cons, where reduced boundary layer heights can limit vertical dispersion and promote the near-surface accumulation of particulate matter from low-intensity fires (Nowell et al., 2022). However, regional validation studies such as those by Becker et al. (2021) in Kathmandu, who conducted a study validating MERRA2 and CAMS reanalysis against _in situ_ observations, revealed that while these products can reproduce surface trends after linear correction, they fail to adequately capture early-morning peaks in the diurnal cycle that frequently coincide with the specific timing of agricultural burns. To overcome these gaps, the two-stage statistical model developed by Ma et al. (2019), which integrates assimilated meteorological data with MODIS observations, offers a robust approach for monitoring the efficacy of multipollutant control measures, demonstrating that the substantial reductions in PM2.5 levels in China were largely driven by the implementation of multipollutant control measures introduced in 2013.

shifts under extreme physical exertion intensities, reaching core temperatures of 38.1<sup>°</sup> C–38.2<sup>°</sup> C (Lucas et al., 2023). Multi-year workplace intervention trials demonstrate that implementing structured Rest-Shade-Hydration (RSH) protocols providing hourly 20-min rest breaks in shaded tents with cold water and electrolytes reduced incident kidney injury among burned cane cutters from 21% down to 1% over four harvest seasons while increasing overall cutting productivity by 9%–19% (Glaser et al., 2020; Hansson et al., 2024; 2025).

## 5 Discussion and conclusion 

The synthesis of the reviewed literature demonstrates that while Earth observation, atmospheric Modelling, and machine learning technologies have advanced rapidly, their application in data-scarce sugarcane regions of the Global South remains fragmented. Most atmospheric studies rely on polar-orbiting satellite remote sensing because it offers cost-effective regional coverage. However, standard operational active fire products (e.g., MODIS at 1-km resolution) systematically under detect small, short-lived sugarcane field burns under 5 ha, missing roughly an order of magnitude of burned area in one Thai case study and about 60% of global cropland burned area. Furthermore, once-a-day polar satellite overpasses fail to capture the diurnal cycle of sugarcane fires, which typically burn for 30–60 min in the early morning or late afternoon. 

Modelling techniques like machine learning and chemical transport models show great promise for predicting the movement of smoke plumes across regions. However, their predictive capabilities are hindered by a lack of accurate input data, such as local emission factors and detailed land use maps. Relying on generic, global biomass emission inventories underestimates agricultural crop residue emissions because global inventories do not account for crop-specific fuel loads, combustion stages, or localized residue moisture. This puts countries such as South Africa at a disadvantage. Developing region-specific sugarcane emission factors incorporating pre-harvest leaf burning, post-harvest trash blanketing, and mill bagasse combustion is therefore essential for reducing model uncertainty and establishing reliable baseline inventories. Addressing these challenges requires a transition toward multi-tier data fusion, coupling highresolution satellite scar mapping (Sentinel-2) and sub-hourly geostationary fire tracking (Meteosat/SEVIRI) with rural lowcost sensor networks, localized sugarcane emission factors, and workplace health surveillance. 

Integrating air-quality modelling with occupational health and clinical epidemiology represents an important research gap. Manual harvesters are exposed to high levels of smoke, dust, and black 

carbon from sugarcane burning, which may contribute to Acute Kidney Injury (AKI), Chronic Kidney Disease of non-traditional origin (CKDnt), and systemic inflammation. The implementation of structured Rest-Shade-Hydration (RSH) interventions, together with a transition toward mechanical green harvesting and the utilization of agricultural residues for bioenergy, could help reduce occupational exposure while maintaining agricultural productivity. This integrated approach could support progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals related to good health, clean energy, and sustainable communities (SDG 3, 7, and 11). 

## 6 Limitations of the review 

Although the literature search was expanded across Scopus and Web of Science using a population-anchored Boolean string, restricting primary database limiters to Open Access and English-language peer-reviewed articles may have excluded relevant non-English literature (particularly Portuguese and Spanish studies from South America) and regional technical reports from South African sugar industry bodies (e.g., SASA, SASRI). Second, record screening and data extraction were conducted primarily by a single lead reviewer, introducing potential selection bias, and no formal clinical risk-of-bias appraisal tool (e.g., ROBINS-I) was applied to the included health studies due to the broad, interdisciplinary scope of the corpus. Finally, because keyword co-occurrence mapping relies on terms present in published titles and abstracts, mandatory search terms naturally function as central structural hubs in bibliometric network diagrams, requiring careful qualitative interpretation of the resulting thematic clusters. 

The novelty of this study lies in its focus on the use of satellite and reanalysis data to address gaps in ground-based air-quality monitoring in South Africa. By quantifying the distribution of methodologies used in the literature, this review identifies research gaps in South Africa in comparison with global publication trends. It further highlights the potential of advanced data-fusion and modelling techniques to support emission monitoring in data-scarce regions. These approaches can complement the existing South African Air Quality Information System (SAAQIS) network by providing information for areas that are not adequately covered by ground-based monitoring stations.
