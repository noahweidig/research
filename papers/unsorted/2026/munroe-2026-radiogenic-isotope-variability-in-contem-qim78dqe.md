# Radiogenic isotope variability in contemporary mineral dust across the intermountain west of the United States

## Abstract

Mineral dust is a highly mobile component of Earth’s Critical Zone that influences ecosystem function, soil development, biogeochemical cycling, and environmental archives. Radiogenic isotopes provide a powerful means of characterizing dust because isotope ratios largely preserve source signatures during atmospheric transport. Here we present a dataset of 100 modern dust samples analyzed for 87 Sr/ 86 Sr and ε Nd from a network of 20 passive dust collectors distributed across the Intermountain West of the United States, including sites in the Uinta Mountains, Wasatch Mountains, Great Basin ranges of Nevada, southern Utah, southern Idaho, and urban environments. Pb isotope compositions were determined for samples from 8 of these collectors. Consideration of collector position, grain-size distribution, and local geology identifies a limited number of samples influenced by locally derived material, but most measurements provide a robust characterization of the isotope fingerprint of contemporary dust deposition. Distinct geographic patterns are evident, with dust from Nevada exhibiting lower 87 Sr/ 86 Sr ratios and higher ε Nd values than dust collected in Utah mountain environments, whereas samples from southern Utah, Idaho, and urban locations occupy intermediate but distinguishable isotopic fields. These isotope fingerprints can be provisionally linked with potential source areas upwind. In concert with Sr and Nd, Pb isotope ratios provide additional discriminatory power. Repeated sampling demonstrates that individual sites exhibit modest year-to-year variability, yet temporal variation is generally smaller than spatial differences among regions. The resulting geographic clustering indicates that dust deposited across the Intermountain West is not isotopically homogeneous, but instead exhibits persistent regional differences reflecting varying source landscapes and transport pathways. These results highlight the value of distributed monitoring networks for characterizing dust variability across broad regions and provide a foundation for future investigations of dust transport, landscape connectivity, and Critical Zone processes in western North America.

## **Introduction** 

Mineral dust is among the most mobile and influential materials cycling through Earth’s Critical Zone. Lofted into the atmosphere from arid and semi-arid regions, dust can travel thousands of kilometers before deposition, linking distant landscapes through exchanges of mineral matter and nutrients<sup>1</sup> . Its effects are diverse, ranging from the fertilization of marine ecosystems<sup>2</sup> to modification of soil properties<sup>3</sup> , and alteration of radiative forcing in the atmosphere<sup>4</sup> . At the same time, dust contributes to archives that record past environmental change, including ice cores<sup>5</sup> , marine sediments<sup>6</sup> , and, by definition, loess deposits<sup>7</sup> . As a result, the entrainment, transport, and deposition of dust link the global Critical Zone across a range of spatial and temporal scales<sup>8</sup> . 

The mineralogical and geochemical characteristics of dust particles influence their resulting effects on the atmosphere and surface environments<sup>9,10</sup> . These characteristics are, in turn, directly controlled by the geology of the dust source regions<sup>11</sup> . Linking dust to its sources, therefore, provides insight into the feedbacks among climate, land surface conditions, and atmospheric circulation<sup>12,13</sup> . 

Radiogenic isotopes are indispensable tools for dust provenance studies because isotope ratios are relatively insensitive to fractionation during transport, preserving the signal of their source area even through long-range travel. Sr and Nd isotopes, in particular, have been analyzed to develop “fingerprints” for dust collected in the Caribbean<sup>14</sup> , the Mediterranean<sup>15</sup> , and across Asia<sup>16</sup> . The addition of Pb isotopes provides further discriminatory power, particularly in regions where Nd and Sr signatures overlap<sup>17</sup> , and Hf isotopes have also shown potential to resolve dust sources<sup>18</sup> . 

Despite these advances, understanding of the range of radiogenic isotope variability in dust remains incomplete. Many studies are geographically restricted, limited to a handful of dust transport events, or focus on material archived in sedimentary deposits that integrate over broad timescales. Furthermore, seasonal shifts in atmospheric circulation, and changes in land cover or aridity in source regions, can alter the relative importance of different dust sources on timescales of months to years<sup>19,20</sup> . Without datasets that capture this variability, efforts to interpret dust fluxes in terms of environmental change remain incomplete. 

Previous work in the intermountain west of the United States, the broad region extending from the Sierra Nevada eastward to the Rocky Mountains, concluded that much of the mineral dust accumulating at higher elevations is derived from with the intermountain region<sup>21</sup> . This interpretation that much dust travels just ~ 10 s to 100 s of km is based on the observation that properties of modern dust captured by passive samplers vary much more than would be expected if the dust were well-mixed by long-distance transport. Recognition that the extensive dryland landscapes dominating the lower elevations of this region are a major source for dust deposited in the mountains has implications for land management decisions. Yet additional data spanning a wider temporal range are needed to assess the robustness of this conclusion. 

As part of the DUST^2 project, a component of the Critical Zone Collaborative Network, an array of 20 passive dust collectors, constructed following published designs<sup>22</sup> , was established in the southwestern United States (Fig. 1, Table 1). Most collectors were deployed in mountain locations above the local treeline, although a few were in urban settings. In northern Utah, collectors were deployed in the Uinta Mountains (DUST-1 through DUST-8, and DUST-18 through DUST-20), the Wasatch Mountains (DUST-9), and at lower elevations in the cities of Salt Lake (DUST-10) and Provo (DUST-17). In southern Utah, collectors were installed in the Tushar Range (DUST-13) and in the La Sal Mountains (DUST-14). In eastern Nevada, collectors were positioned in the South Snake Range (DUST-12), the Ruby Mountains (DUST-11), and the Independence Range (DUST-15). A final collector (DUST-16) was deployed in the Albion Range of southern Idaho (Fig. 1). 

The initial aim was to empty each collector twice a year; a sample retrieved in early summer represented winter dust, and a second sample retrieved in the fall was composed of dust that accumulated during the summer. In practice, however this was not always possible due to snow conditions, wildfire-related restrictions, adverse weather, and other complications. Furthermore, not all collected samples were analyzed for radiogenic isotopes, given the expense and complexity of the measurements.

In the laboratory, all samples were wet-sieved to 63 µm, and only the material < 63 um was analyzed. This fraction is referred to as the “bulk” dust. Material > 63 µm was considered to be locally derived and was not assessed further. Additionally, the bulk fraction for 7 particularly large samples from a multi-year collection (2017–2019) was further subdivided into a < 30 µm (fine) and a 30–63 µm (coarse) fraction by settling and centrifugation. These two fractions were analyzed separately to test for grain-size related variability, and the results were recombined using their masses, along with the measured abundances of Rb, Sr, Sm, and Nd, to yield calculated values of<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr and εNd for the bulk fraction of these 7 samples. 

Ultimately, 100 samples were analyzed for both<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr and εNd. In addition, Pb isotope ratios (<sup>206</sup> Pb/<sup>204</sup> Pb, 207Pb/204Pb, 208Pb/204Pb, 207Pb/206Pb, 208Pb/206Pb) were determined for 18 samples representing 8 different collectors spanning the urban settings and areas to the west and south. 

Here we present the dataset of radiogenic isotope measurements of modern dust retrieved by this collector network at seasonally resolved time intervals. By analyzing<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr and εNd in a large number of geographically diverse samples, and Pb isotopes in a subset of them, we test the hypotheses that the radiogenic isotope fingerprint of modern dust will vary spatially due to proximity to different dust sources, and that the fingerprint of dust at a given site will be generally stable from year to year, reflecting a consistent blend of contributing source regions. Our dataset highlights the ability of radiogenic isotopes to illuminate connectivity within the global Critical Zone, and to refine our understanding of dust as both an archive and an active agent of environmental change. 

## **Results and discussion** 

### **Scope of dataset** 

The 100 dust samples analyzed represent 20 different collector locations, with as many as 10 different time intervals for a given site (with the maximum number at collector DUST-1). Winter dust collections were analyzed for at least one site in five years, whereas summer dust was analyzed separately for at least one site in two years. Annual collections (fall to fall) occurred in 2022–23, and 2023–24. A single multi-year collection spanned summer 2017 to fall 2019 (Table S1). 

Across the entire dataset, the<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr ratio ranges from 0.70707 (at DUST-15) to 0.73126 (DUST-1), with a mean of 0.71425 ± 0.00420 (Fig. 2. Table S1). Values of εNd range from − 4.94 (DUST-15) to − 14.69 (DUST-19), with a mean of − 10.76 ± 1.78 (Fig. 2, Table S1). The<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr ratio exhibits a positive skew (+ 1.28), whereas values of εNd are more symmetrically distributed (skew of + 0.63). 

The uranogenic Pb isotope ratios<sup>206</sup> Pb/<sup>204</sup> Pb and<sup>207</sup> Pb/<sup>204</sup> Pb record patterns that are similar to one another (Table S2) so only the<sup>206</sup> Pb/<sup>204</sup> Pb ratio is discussed for efficiency. The<sup>206</sup> Pb/<sup>204</sup> Pb ratio of raw dust samples ranges from 18.026 to 19.381 with a mean of 18.783 ± 0.375 (Fig. S1). Residual dust samples (leached to remove anthropogenic Pb) yield a narrower range of<sup>206</sup> Pb/<sup>204</sup> Pb ratios (18.063 to 19.270) with a relatively radiogenic mean of 18.870 ± 0.326 (Fig. S1). Additionally, for all samples collected in 2021, the mean value of the raw dust (18.608 ± 0.290) is less radiogenic than the residual dust (18.768 ± 0.335), though that trend reverses with more radiogenic raw values (19.204 ± 0.131) compared to residual dust (19.117 ± 0.091) in the 2022 samples (Fig. S1). The differences between paired samples in both these years are significant ( _p_ < 0.05). 

Between 2021 and 2022,<sup>206</sup> Pb/<sup>204</sup> Pb ratios of raw dust samples from individual sites shift to more radiogenic values (Fig. S2). Values for residual samples are also more radiogenic in 2022, but the magnitude of the shift is smaller (Fig. S2). 

Spatially, the least radiogenic<sup>206</sup> Pb/<sup>204</sup> Pb values were measured on samples from collector DUST-9 in the Wasatch Mountains, whereas the most radiogenic values were measured on a sample from DUST-11 in the Ruby Mountains (Fig. 3). The samples from DUST-9 also exhibited a relatively minor shift in values with leaching. In contrast, samples from DUST-14, DUST-15, and DUST-17 exhibited large shifts, particularly in 2021 (Figs. ### **Quality control** 

Permitting restrictions imposed by land management agencies required that the dust collectors were deployed on the ground surface in most locations. The exceptions are DUST-9, which was located on an instrument platform ~ 4 m above the ground, and DUST-10 and DUST-17, which were deployed on roofs (Figs. 1 and 4). This situation raises the possibility that the material captured by the collectors may be a mixture of exotic dust and local material derived from the surrounding ground surface. To avoid this as much as possible, collectors were intentionally positioned in settings featuring either (nearly) continuous vegetation, or in blockfields of coarse frost-shattered boulders (Fig. 4). In both situations, the potential for loose fine material to be entrained from the soil surface and saltated into the collector is greatly reduced. Wet-sieving of the samples to 63 µm was also intended to exclude coarser material of potential local provenance. 

Nonetheless, at five sites in the Uinta Mountains (DUST-1, DUST-4, and DUST-18 through DUST-20) where bare soil or outcroppings of friable bedrock are present in the vicinity of the collectors, some analyses (n = 18) of the bulk dust yielded outlying<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr ratios, trailing off to the lower right in Fig. 2. These samples were clearly separated by a two-step cluster (TSC) analysis. The more radiogenic ratios for these samples resemble those reported previously for bedrock collected near these locations<sup>23</sup> , suggesting that locally sourced material mixed with exotic dust in the collector and carried over into the bulk < 63 µm size fraction that was analyzed.

Despite this concern, several observations support the argument that the remaining<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr and εNd measurements faithfully represent the radiogenic isotope fingerprint of modern dust in this region. First, the Uinta Mountain samples with the more radiogenic<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr ratios are easily distinguished in a biplot of<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr and εNd, as reinforced by the cluster analysis (Figs. 2). This pattern suggests that when contamination occurs, it exerts a large and obvious effect. Second, some samples from the Uinta Mountain collectors that yielded the contaminated samples nonetheless exhibit an isotope fingerprint similar to dust from locations where the presence of continuous vegetation or block fields rules out local contamination as a concern (Fig. 2). Therefore, even where the potential for local contamination exists, it is not an issue in every collection, likely due to variability in wind conditions and snow cover during the collection period. Third, the shifts in isotope fingerprint revealed by analysis of the coarse and fine fractions in the 2017–2019 Uinta Mountain samples are small compared to the full range of<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr and εNd in the dataset (Fig. S3). Thus, even if the analyzed bulk samples still contain a minor amount of (< 63 µm) local material, the resulting shift in<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr and εNd is small compared to the variation in Sr and Nd isotope compositions more broadly across the Intermountain West. Fourth, DUST-9, DUST-10, and DUST17 were all deployed in elevated positions (Fig. 4), so samples from these locations should not be vulnerable to contamination by local material. Finally, the radiogenic isotope fingerprints of local bedrock from the vicinity of DUST-11 through DUST-16 have been shown to exhibit no correlation with the dust from these collectors<sup>25</sup> , so local contamination does not seem to be an issue at these locations. Overall, when the outlier samples from a few collectors in the Uinta Mountains are removed the resulting dataset can be considered a valid characterization of the radiogenic isotope fingerprint of the modern dust captured by the passive collector network.

Group 1 (Fig. 5) corresponds with nearly all of the samples (DUST-1 through DUST-8) from the Uinta Mountains, as well as samples from DUST-14 in southeastern Utah (Figs. 1 and 6). Values of<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr and εNd in Group 1 average 0.71446 ± 0.00100 and − 10.6375 ± 0.69219 respectively. 

In contrast, Group 3 (Fig. 5) is composed entirely of samples from DUST-11 and DUST-15 (Fig. 6), both located in northeastern Nevada (Fig. 1).<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr in Group 3 averages 0.70824 ± 0.00065, and values of εNd average − 6.8256 ± 0.9899. 

Group 2 contains all samples from DUST-9 (Wasatch Mountains), and DUST-17 (Provo), half the samples from DUST-10 (Salt Lake City), along with one sample from DUST-3 and three samples from DUST-6 (Fig. 6). Both of these samplers were located at the extreme western end of the Uinta Mountains, close to the Wasatch Mountains (Fig. 1). Thus, much of Group 2 represents locations along or near the Wasatch Front in northern Utah. Additional sites in Group 2 are DUST-12 in east-central Nevada, DUST-13 in southwestern Utah, and DUST-16 in southern Idaho (Fig. 1). All of these are located along the main south-north trending corridor through which dust transport often occurs in response to frontal system passage<sup>26,27</sup> . The average ratio<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr in Group 2 (0.71137 ± 0.00088) is intermediate between Groups 1 and 3, whereas values of εNd are similar to those in Group 1 (10.7311 ± 1.2046). 

Overall, the dissimilarity of the dust isotope fingerprints across our collector network corroborate previous observations from a smaller dataset<sup>21</sup> , and strongly support the hypothesis that modern dust reaching the locations of our collectors is not far-traveled and homogenous, but rather primarily sourced from the surrounding area, carrying with it a signal of diverse source area geology [e.g.<sup>28</sup> ]. 

Furthermore, the tendency for samples from individual collectors in subsequent years to exhibit consistent group membership (Fig. 6) supports the hypothesis that the radiogenic isotope fingerprint of dust from different locations would be consistent from year to year. To be clear, the<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr ratios and εNd values of subsequent samples from any given collector are not identical; as is shown in Fig. 2, there is some variability between subsequent samples at specific collectors, likely related to changes in the mosaic of landscape positions serving as dust sources. However, the variability in the isotope fingerprint exhibited by subsequent samples from each site (the spread of points from a given collector in Fig. 2) is much smaller than the differences observed between sites (the full spread of points in Fig. 2), indicating that the isotopic variability is controlled more by where the dust is coming from, and that the array of sites contributing dust to a given collector is generally consistent over time. This temporal consistency has implications for paleo-dust provenance studies, because it suggests that spatially distinct source regions can maintain recognizable radiogenic isotope fingerprints despite year-to-year variability in dust production and transport. Modern multi-year observations such as these therefore provide a stronger basis for interpreting shifts in the isotope composition of dust preserved in sedimentary archives as changes in source contribution [e.g.<sup>16</sup> ], rather than simply short-term variability within a single source region. 

### **Geographic connections** 

A complete catalog of<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr ratio and εNd values for all possible dust sources areas in the western United States does not exist. However, it is revealing to compare the fingerprints of the dust collected in this study with samples of surficial sediments that have been analyzed and presented in other work. For instance, DUST-11 and DUST15 in northeastern Nevada, which were delineated as Group 3 by the TSC analysis, have relatively low<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr ratios (~ 0.708) and high values of εNd (~ − 7). These match the isotope fingerprints reported for the < 63 µm fraction for samples of alluvium collected in southwestern Nevada<sup>29</sup> , suggesting that fine material winnowed from the land surface in this region is transported to the mountains in the northeastern part of the state (Fig. 7). Farther south, in the vicinity of Las Vegas, Nevada, and across the border into the Mojave Desert region of California,<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr ratios in surficial sediments are ~ 0.711 and values of εNd are less radiogenic (− 10 to − 15), which matches the distribution of samples comprising Group 2 (Fig. 7). This connection is consistent with dust transport from this region to the north-northeast toward areas along the Wasatch Front as observed in remote sensing data<sup>26,27</sup> . ### **Synthesis and implications** 

The results presented here represent a culmination of 15 years of dust monitoring across the Intermountain West. Beginning with four collectors in the Uinta Mountains in 2011, the network expanded to eventually encompass twenty sites distributed across diverse mountain ranges and urban environments. Over this time, the scientific questions evolved in parallel with the collector network itself. Initial efforts focused on documenting modern dust deposition in alpine environments and identifying likely source regions for dust reaching the Uinta Mountains<sup>23,35,36</sup> . Subsequent work demonstrated that regional drought exerts a strong influence on mountain dust deposition<sup>22</sup> , considered how dust fluxes varied over millennial timescales<sup>37</sup> , established that much of the dust reaching the high mountains is derived from surrounding drylands<sup>21</sup> , and revealed how mixing with anthropogenic dust alters the properties of dust from natural sources<sup>38</sup> . The isotope results presented here extend those earlier studies by placing individual sites into a regional framework spanning much of the Intermountain West, demonstrating broader geographic patterns that emerge only when many locations are considered simultaneously. 

Our collection of paired<sup>87</sup> Sr/<sup>86</sup> Sr and εNd measurements, coupled with a subset of Pb isotope characterizations, represents a substantial contribution to the global inventory of radiogenic isotope analyses of modern atmospheric dust. Many previous isotope studies have relied on smaller numbers of samples collected during individual dust events or from geographically restricted regions or time intervals [e.g.<sup>14,17,39,40</sup> ]. By contrast, our dataset spans more than a decade of observations, contrasting seasons, and a diverse range of physiographic settings. This breadth makes it possible to distinguish persistent regional differences from short-term variability and provides an important benchmark against which future changes in dust emissions, atmospheric transport, and land-surface conditions can be evaluated. 

The long duration of this monitoring effort further highlights the value of sustained environmental observing networks. Repeated sampling demonstrates that although isotope fingerprints fluctuate modestly through time (i.e. Fig. S2), the differences among regions remain notably consistent (Figs. 2, 5, 6, and S4). This finding emphasizes that meaningful characterization of dust systems requires observations that extend across multiple years and encompass diverse landscapes. The scientific value from long-term monitoring, therefore, addresses questions concerning persistence, variability, and environmental change that cannot be addressed through shorter sampling campaigns. 

This study also emphasizes that passive collectors deployed at ground level provide a practical means of building geographically extensive monitoring networks, but careful site selection is essential to minimize incorporation of locally derived sediment (Fig. 4). The comparisons presented here demonstrate that vegetation cover, coarse blockfields, and elevated installations substantially reduce this concern, while paired analyses of coarse and fine fractions provide an effective means of identifying potential contamination where it occurs (Fig. S3). These observations provide guidance for future monitoring programs seeking to balance logistical simplicity with analytical rigor. 

Finally, viewed from the perspective of Critical Zone science, this networked sampling effort illustrates how modern dust links landscapes across regional scales<sup>8</sup> . Dust deposited in mountain ecosystems records the integrated influence of source geology, climate, and atmospheric circulation, while simultaneously becoming incorporated into soils, snowpacks, streams, and ecosystems<sup>41</sup> . Continued study of these connections will be increasingly important as expanding dryland dust sources, changing climate, and evolving land use alter the availability of fine sediment in the western United States<sup>42,43</sup> and in other settings<sup>44</sup> . This dataset of radiogenic isotope measurements produced as part of the DUST^2 project, therefore, provides not only a regional baseline

for contemporary dust, but also a framework for interpreting future environmental change through the lens of Critical Zone connectivity. 

## **Methods** 

### **Analytical methods** 

Material was removed from the passive collectors and prepared for analysis through washing and wet-sieving with distilled water, removal of organic matter with 35% H2O2, repeated centrifugation, wet-sieving to 63 µm to isolate a bulk dust sample, and oven-drying as described in prior work<sup>22,38</sup> . 

All Sr, Nd, and Pb isotope ratio data was collected in the ICP-TIMS lab at the University of Wisconsin-Madison. The analytical protocols for Sr and Nd for the early dust samples (collected before 2019) are detailed in previous work<sup>23</sup> , and the same procedures were utilized for the samples presented here. Strontium isotope ratio measurements were performed on a Micromass Sector 54 multi-collector thermal ionization mass spectrometer. Neodymium and Pb isotope ratio measurements were performed on a Nu Plasma II multi-collector inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (MC-ICP-MS). 

Lead isotope ratios were determined on a subset of dust samples using two different preparation techniques. The first approach, results from which are referred to as “raw” samples, used an additional ~ 5–10-mg aliquot of the same dust powder processed for Sr and Nd isotopes. In the second approach, referred to as “residual” samples, an additional aliquot of the raw powder was leached with dilute HBr in an attempt to remove the liable anthropogenic Pb from the sample<sup>45,46</sup> . For the leaching process, approximately 10 mg of dust powder was weighed, placed into a centrifuge tube, and leached in 0.5 ml of 0.6M HBr for 15 min, during which samples were agitated on a vortex mixer every 5 min. At the conclusion of leaching, the samples were centrifuged and supernatant removed via pipette. The residual was then rinsed with 18.2 megaohm water, agitated in a vortex mixer, centrifuged, and the supernatant was removed via pipette. The water rinse step was repeated three times to ensure the residual sample was free of leachate. After leaching, the residual sample was left in a desiccator until completely dry. Residual sample powders were then weighed, dissolved, and processed in the same manner as bulk samples. Both raw and residual samples were dissolved in Savillex vials on a ~ 150 °C hotplate in a mixture of concentrated HF and HNO3. Following dissolution, samples were evaporated and redissolved in 8 M HCl and fluxed on a ~ 150 °C hotplate overnight then evaporated again. Concentrated HBr was then added to all samples and evaporated. Samples were then dissolved in dilute HBr, and Pb was separated from the matrix via anion-exchange chromatography with BioRad AG 1X8 100–200 mesh Cl<sup>−</sup> form resin. 

Lead samples were analyzed in dry plasma mode utilizing an Airdus II desolvating nebulizer and a Savillex self-aspirating nebulizer over the course of two analytical sessions. Purified Pb samples were dissolved in 2% HNO3 and prepared as 40 ppb Pb + 4 ppb Tl solutions which yielded an average<sup>208</sup> Pb<sup>+</sup> signal of ~ 11 × 10<sup>–11</sup> A. Thallium (NIST SRM-997) was added to correct for mass bias following the method of<sup>47</sup> using a value of 205Tl/203Tl = 2.38965 and an exponential normalization. NIST SRM-981 was analyzed every 3 unknowns and a bracketing correction factor was applied based on the published values of the standard<sup>48</sup> . Secondary standard NIST SRM-982 was analyzed throughout the run. Mercury interference was monitored at masses 201 and 202 and a correction was applied to mass 204 though it was insignificant (<sup>204</sup> Pb/<sup>204</sup> Hg > 22,000). 

Procedural blanks were measured during each analytical campaign. Blanks averaged 102 pg Sr (n = 6), 74 pg Nd (n = 6), and 49 pg Pb (n = 2) resulting of the three in a minimum sample/blank ratio of 1200 (for Nd) and no blank correction was applied. For each element, external and in-house standards and reference materials were routinely measured and consistently produced values in agreement with published and long-term values for the lab. Measured values for standards and reference materials are summarized in Table S3.
