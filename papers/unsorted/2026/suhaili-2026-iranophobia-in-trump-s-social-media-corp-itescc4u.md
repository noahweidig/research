# Iranophobia in Trump’s Social Media: Corpus-Assisted Critical Discourse Analysis

## Abstract

Strained diplomatic relations between Iran and the United States have historically been characterised by ongoing strife, involving sensitive issues like nuclear programs, economic penalties, and Middle Eastern power struggles. The tension intensified during Trump’s presidency. This study highlights the most favoured term used by the US President, Donald Trump, on social media to talk about Iran. It employs corpus-assisted critical discourse analysis (CACDA) combined with Fairclough's three-dimensional framework to examine Iranophobia in Trump's social media posts from 2016 to 2020 across X (formerly Twitter ), Facebook, and Instagram. The analysis was conducted at three levels: micro (keyword extraction, collocation, and concordance), meso (social media platform bias), and macro (global impact of Trump's Iran policies). Findings revealed Iranophobia across six categories: (1) war and conflict, (2) terrorism, (3) government and politics, (4) nuclear weapons, (5) economics, and (6) human rights. The meso- level analysis demonstrated that Trump's social media content was biased when depicting violence in the Middle East, which might explain why X blocked his account for inflammatory content with social impact in the US. The macro-level analysis indicated how Trump's harsh economic policies against Iran generated global Iranophobia. The research concludes that social media not only offers alternative discourse but can also recontextualise traditional media discourse in representing Iranophobia, albeit more aggressively and overtly.

## **Introduction** 

Social media platforms have generated more opportunities for individuals to articulate, acquire, convey, and reproduce their perspectives, ideas, and ideologies using textual and multimodal means of communication. Some of these media presentations may aim to elicit specific emotional responses from their audiences (Munandar & Basuki, 2021). Given this potential influence, analysing interactions on social media becomes crucial in understanding political communications’ impacts on public perception. Increased awareness about social media can help people avoid manipulation 

using unreliable sources (Spatan & Rich, 2025). Therefore, this influence must be critically examined (Naerland & Dahl, 2022). According to linguists, analysing social media text is essential to understanding how societal issues are processed (Bahri & Widhyharto, 2021). Media are both a mirror and a moulder of reality, as they validate some worldviews and ideological constructions in film, television, photography and print journalism (Andersson, 2025; Happer & Philo, 2013). In this sense, there is also a certain level of bias in the media against indigenous and other minority groups (Ismail et al., 2020; Mulyanto, 2022).

## **Literature Review** 

### **Iranophobia** 

Iranophobia in conceptual terms is an irrational fear, dislike, or perception of Iran and all that it entails. Javdani Moghaddam (2019) describes this as a political project based on Baudrillard’s hyperreality, where the negative images of Iran are no longer based on empirical reality but have become reality itself by means of the continuous reproduction of simulations by Western media, politics, and culture. This meaning-making is closely linked to Said's (2014) theory of Orientalism, which describes how the West systematically constructs a representation of the East—in this case, Iran—as backward, authoritarian, and dangerous through the construction of stereotypes and prejudices. Eventually, these constructed perceptions are used to legitimise American foreign policy, including military interventions against Iran. 

Mirmohammad Sadeghi and Hajimineh (2019) also argue that Iranophobia is a conscious project of the US and its regional allies, especially Israel and some Arab states, to delegitimise the authorities and capabilities of the Islamic Republic of Iran. Likewise, Basarati and Rezaei (2019) describe Iranophobia as a political tool of terror, as a mechanism to intentionally incite collective fear so that some agendas (political, economic or social) may be justified. Iranophobia, therefore, is not only an individual psychological symptom, but also a structured discourse systematically embedded in global political dynamics. Past studies on Iranophobia have used discursive strategies similar to other xenophobias, such as Islamophobia and Sinophobia, that construct non-Western subjects as existential threats. For example, the British media tend to focus on violence and cultural differences with the Muslim, echoing Trump’s rhetoric framing Iran as a source of terrorism (Baker et al., 2013).

### **Discourse and Ideology** 

In an ideological context, discourse is often shaped by participants' subjective interpretations based on their mental models or direct beliefs (Van Dijk, 2006a). However, ideology is not always transparent. Speakers sometimes deliberately conceal their ideological affiliations (Määttä, 2014) to avoid political repression, as Islamist groups did before the Arab Spring. Therefore, the existence of ideology in a discourse is dependent on the communication situation and the particular context of the issue (Beetz et al., 2021). Ideology within discourse depends heavily on the communication context (Beetz et al., 2021). Because individuals do not always express their beliefs openly, ideological discourse is inherently non-deterministic and varies based on personal considerations and dynamic situational factors (Van Dijk, 2006b). 

### **Corpus-Assisted Critical Discourse Analysis and UCREL Semantic Analysis System (USAS)** 

Critical discourse analysis (CDA) is an overarching paradigm to reveal how language constructs and sustains power relations and ideology in social and political contexts (Van Dijk, 2006a; Wodak & Meyer, 2001). However, the use of small or selectively 

constrained data sets has been criticised as potentially subjective (Stubbs, 2001, 2005). This perceived shortcoming can be complemented by quantitative corpus linguistics in CorpusAssisted Critical Discourse Analysis (CACDA), which provides a systematic and replicable method for analysing large quantities of textual data (Partington, 2008). In this way, CACDA combines statistical (e.g., keyword and collocation analysis) and interpretive approaches to reveal ideological patterns in discourse. It is especially well-suited for the analysis of large social media corpora, such as Trump’s post on Iran. 

The UCREL Semantic Analysis System (USAS) provides an automated system for categorising lexical items into particular semantic categories, thus enhancing ComputerAssisted Qualitative Data Analysis (CAQDA) (Archer et al., 2002; Piao et al., 2005). USAS categorises words predictively, reducing researcher bias and strengthening systematic, empirical qualitative interpretations. For example, Prentice (2010) showed the effectiveness of USAS in analysing the discourse on Scottish independence. The automatic semantic tagging revealed repeated lexical patterns reflecting ideological positions in nationalist text. 

Considering the effectiveness, this study uses USAS in the analysis. The findings identify six major semantic domains in Donald Trump’s social media discourse: war/conflict, terrorism, government/politics, nuclear weapons, economics and human rights. The six categories were selected in line with prior research on the representation of Iran in Western media. The war/terrorism narratives are considered in line with post-9/11 security concerns, while political and human rights themes are critical in the context of the authoritarian regime of Iran (Basarati et al., 2023; Javdani Moghaddam, 2019; Fayyaz & Shirazi, 2013). Likewise, the nuclear issue and economic sanctions are often framed as global

## **Methods** 

### **Corpus-Assisted Critical Discourse Analysis** 

Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) is often used to reveal social inequalities in language practices, for example in research on representation of Muslim minorities (Baker et al., 2013; Fayyaz & Shirazi, 2013), but CDA has been criticised for its selective and subjective choice of texts. This method does not work with a large amount of data. The Corpus-Assisted Critical Discourse Analysis (CACDA) model has been proposed to integrate CDA with corpus linguistics (Baker et al., 2013; Fayyaz & Shirazi, 2013). It employs quantitative tools for 

statistical analysis, extending the CDA model, which remains theoretical and qualitative (Stubbs, 2001). 

Methodologically, this research is positioned with critical thinking, which presupposes that language and discourse are closely connected in the construction of particular viewpoints in society (Partington et al., 2013). CACDA is effective in deconstructing political discourse on social media and provides a replicable framework for investigating other types of national or ethnic stereotypes in digital communication. Such an integration ultimately provides a strong quantitative validation for qualitative interpretive claims (Wodak & Meyer, 2001). 

### **Data Collection and Sampling Criteria** 

Trump’s verified social media accounts (X, Facebook and Instagram) between 20162020 (during his presidential term) were systematically gathered. The inclusion criteria for posts were: 1) explicit mention of “Iran”, “Iranian” or a related term; 2) directly addressed Iran-related policies or events; and 3) contained implicit references to Iranian affairs based on contextual analysis. The exclusion criteria were: (1) retweets without additional comment; (2) technical errors of incomplete text in the posts; and (3) duplicate content across platforms. This systematic selection process yielded 358 posts, consisting of 10,342 tokens, which is approximately 12% of Trump’s entire social media output on Iran in this time frame. 

The CACDA approach integrates quantitative corpus analysis and qualitative discourse interpretation in a coherent threestage process. It is based on statistical _keyness_ analysis with the help of the _Sketch Engine_ software, which compares the target corpus with the English Web 20 (reference corpus of more than 43 billion tokens) and detects significant keywords with a threshold of p<0.001. Next, the collocational patterns are analysed with the LogDice formula in a

### **Data Analysis** 

The data analysis in this research employs a linguistic corpus approach combining quantitative and qualitative methods. Quantitatively, the study is conducted by determining keywords ( _keyness_ ) using Sketch Engine software, which compares the target corpus (Trump's social media texts with 10,342 tokens)  against  the  reference  corpus (English  Web  20  with 43,125,207,462 tokens). The _keyness_ score is calculated using simple mathematical parameters. The _keyness_ score of a word is calculated using the formula: **Keyness = (f_focus - f_ref) / (f_focus + f_ref + smooth)** , where f_focus is the normalised frequency (per million) in the target corpus, f_ref is the 

normalised frequency in the reference corpus, and smooth is a mathematical parameter with a standard value of 1. The top keywords reflect the domain of the focus corpus very well and can be used to explore differences between corpora Kilgarriff (2012), followed by collocation analysis using the _logDice_ formula to identify relationships between words. After obtaining a list of significant collocations, the analysis continues qualitatively through concordance analysis by categorising topics based on USAS (UCREL Semantic Analysis Systems). The data is then analysed using Fairclough’s critical discourse analysis approach, which encompasses three dimensions: text analysis (lexical and coherence), discourse practice (text production), and sociocultural practice (America-Iran political situation context). This analysis aims to reveal Iran’s representation and the ideology embedded in language use.

### **Macro Level** 

The macro analysis of Iranophobia in Trump’s social media reveals complex institutional, situational and societal factors that influenced anti-Iran rhetoric during his presidency. This analysis, based on Fairclough’s framework, demonstrates how systematic sociocultural factors influenced the construction and dissemination of Iranophobic stories (Khan et al., 2021). 

US foreign policy toward the Middle East has been largely driven by economic interests, namely, the pursuit of oil and the protection of strategic allies (Prifti, 2017). This priority has been institutionalised since the time the Carter Doctrine tied access to energy in the Persian Gulf to vital national security interests. This was then operationalised through the establishment of special military commands like the US Central Command (USCENTCOM) to secure critical maritime routes from potential supply shocks. The petrodollar system is not only a short-term political choice, but an institutional framework through strategic alliances with oil-producing countries and long-term security guarantees for regional allies to prevent the domination of competing powers and to ensure the functioning of local regimes, which accommodate Western economic interests (Gouasmia & Elaggoune, 2025). Beneath the normative discourse of humanitarian missions, the US bureaucratic structures and detention facilities in the region operate as pragmatic instruments for the reproduction of the global economic hegemony (Linsenmaier et al., 2021).

Past research has shown that US foreign policy is based on four pillars: Power, Prosperity, Peace, and Principle. The Middle East is a major focus of US foreign policy because it has 61.5% of the world’s oil reserves (Ramadhan, 2019). Such economic dependence generates a structural need for regional hegemony, which Iran views as a threat to US energy security. Institutionally, Israel is an ally, so it has received more than $115 billion in US military and economic aid since 1949 (Al Sarhan, 2017). That has created tensions with Iran, which refuses to recognise Israel’s legitimacy. The US policy discourse and the Iranophobic narrative are based on the triangular relationship of the US, Israel and Iran. The turning point was the 1979 Islamic Revolution, turning Iran from a close ally of the US under the Shah into an ideological enemy. A change that could not be separated from the larger dynamics of the Cold War. The US support for the Shah’s regime during the Cold War was not just about bilateral interests but a containment doctrine; Iran was a strategic buffer against Soviet expansionism into the Persian Gulf, and the CIA-sponsored coup in 1953 that restored the Shah was a case in point of Washington’s willingness to subordinate democratic principles to Cold War imperatives (Kinzer, 2003). The Islamic Revolution’s anti-colonial and anti- Western ideology thus threatened not only American regional hegemony but also the logic of Cold War clientstate architecture. The end of the Cold War and the collapse of the Soviet Union brought about a strategic reorientation. Without a Soviet threat, the United States recalibrated its Middle East policy around new threat hierarchies: terrorism, weapons proliferation, and rogue states, into which Iran was increasingly slotted. The US administration, from Carter to Trump, has followed a series of sanctions and containment measures that built tensions, culminating in the assassination of General Qassem Soleimani and withdrawal from the JCPOA nuclear deal during Trump’s presidency. 

The Iranian revolution was anti-colonial and anti-Western by nature, as well as a direct challenge to the US hegemony in the region. Escalating through successive US administrations from Carter to Trump, sanction-driven tensions culminated in the US withdrawal from the JCPOA nuclear deal and the assassination of General Qasem Soleimani. Throughout this period, the nuclear issue remained a primary flashpoint for Iranophobic rhetoric, even while Iran complied with international inspections under the Obama administration (KhosraviNik, 2022). 

Trump’s ideological background as a conservative Republican, heavily shaped by evangelical Christianity, magnified the Iranophobic themes. This was further intensified by the domestic political and economic motives influenced by pro-Israel lobbying networks (Jewish/Israeli lobbying) such as the AIPAC (American Israel Public Affairs Committee). Through an institutional approach, these lobbying groups channelled enormous electoral campaign funds to guarantee the compliance of the Republican political elite and actively funded neoconservative think tanks to portray Iran as a total existential threat to the economicsecurity interests of the US and its key Gulf allies. The alliance between Evangelical voters, driven by an eschatological, theopolitical narrative, and pro-Israel lobby groups exerted pressure on the Trump administration, driving aggressive unilateral actions to secure geopolitical dominance and serve key donor interests (Mandacı, 2024; Wagner, 2004). 

Trump’s “America First” and its connection with Christian Zionist theology offer an alternative to the philosophy of Iran’s Islamic revolution. According to Mearsheimer and Walt (2007), the pro-Israel lobby heavily influences US foreign policies, threatening regional stability with Iran as a primary target. These policies include recognising Jerusalem as the capital of Israel and the “Muslim ban,” which are indicative of deep racial and

This demonstrates that Iranophobia operates as a total hegemonic discourse, influencing social interactions and travel restrictions beyond the scope of any single presidential administration (Wullweber, 2019).

### **Representation of Iranophobia in Trump’s Language** 

Trump’s lexical choice discursively builds an Iranophobic frame. ## **Conclusion** 

Using the CACDA method and the USAS framework, this paper maps how Trump’s social media rhetoric from 2016 to 2020 fueled Iranophobia. The analysis identifies six key themes, including war, terrorism, politics, nuclear weapons, economics and human rights, which portray Iran as a threat to global security and US interests. The X, Facebook and Instagram posts show polarising narratives, which circumvent traditional media filters and back foreign policy strategies such as the “maximum pressure” campaign. However, this study has limitations in that it only considers text and ignores multimodal aspects (images, memes, videos). It also emphasises discourse production and not audience reception and is limited to Trump’s administration. Future research is recommended to employ multimodal critical discourse analysis, include the analysis of public comments, and conduct longitudinal studies across presidential administrations. These findings underline the need for media literacy education, so that the public can identify and resist ideological manipulation and construction of national stereotypes by political leaders in the digital space.
