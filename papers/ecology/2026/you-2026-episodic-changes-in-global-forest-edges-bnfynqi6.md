# Episodic changes in global forest edges in the 21st century

## Abstract

Seventy percent of global forests lie within 1 km of an edge, yet their temporal dynamics remain poorly characterized. By developing the high spatial-resolution (0.00025°) global forest edge maps for 2000-2020, we find a 24 million km (7%) decline in edge length. During 2015-2020, edge length falls while forest area slightly grows, driven by patch merging in temperate/boreal regions and tropical fragmentation. In contrast, countries such as Laos, Liberia, India, and China gain edges from new forest patches. These episodic changes closely track policy-driven land management, including logging bans, agricultural expansion, and reforestation. Given forest edges’ critical roles in biodiversity and microclimate regulation, our results highlight contrasting biome patterns and the need for adaptive policies balancing conservation, carbon storage, and land use.

Nearly 70% of the world’s forests lie within 1 km of an edge<sup>1</sup> , underscoring the global prevalence of transitional zones where forests meet other ecosystems. These edges are fundamental morphological characteristics shaped by processes such as expansion, contraction, fragmentation, and merging<sup>2</sup> . Forest edges play a crucial role in maintaining biodiversity and facilitating ecological processes<sup>3</sup> because their structure, composition, and function differ substantially from interior forests<sup>4,5</sup> . They often have more open canopies, support earlysuccessional and disturbance-adapted species, and exhibit faster nutrient cycling. These differences strongly influence microclimates, such as light availability, wind exposure, temperature variability, and moisture levels, which in turn shape tree growth patterns and the abundance and diversity of associated wildlife communities<sup>6,7</sup> . Accordingly, forest edge length has been considered as a key state variable, comparable to forest area and fragmentation but far less studied<sup>8</sup> , representing the measurable extent of forest-nonforest interface where energy, matter, and organisms cross boundaries. 

Forest landscape structure can be described using multiple complementary dimensions, including forest area and edge length, which quantify total extent and boundary exposure, while fragmentation represents a broader condition characterized by patterns of patch subdivision and connectivity. These dimensions can evolve 

independently because different ecological and anthropogenic processes reshape forests in distinct ways. For example, fragmentation often increases edge length while reducing area by subdividing continuous forests<sup>5,9,10</sup> , yet edge length may decline when small patches disappear through attrition or when boundaries are simplified. Connectivity indices alone can therefore misrepresent morphology. Relationships between edge and area are also process dependent: deforestation generally increases edge while reducing area<sup>10</sup> ; patch merging often decreases edge but increases area<sup>11</sup> ; Expansion through afforestation or regeneration can increase both forest edge and area<sup>10,12,13</sup> ; and conversion to agriculture or urban land typically reduces both of them<sup>14,15</sup> . These contrasting dynamics illustrate the complexity of forest dynamics and the possible diverse ecological consequences of edge effects<sup>16</sup> . They also highlight the need for global mapping of edge length, which remains scarce despite extensive work on fragmentation<sup>1,10,17–19</sup> . Quantifying global forest edge dynamics is essential to complement area- and fragmentation-based targets with edge-sensitive objectives to preserve core habitat, biodiversity, and ecosystem function.

## Results and discussion 

### Forest edge dynamics across the globe 

Global forest edge length declined from 323 million km in 2000 to 299 million km in 2020 (−7%; Fig. 1b). This net reduction reflects the disappearance of 90 million km of edges, partly offset by the formation of 66 million km of new edges (Supplementary Fig. 1). Among the newly formed edges, those resulting from forest loss (13% of the 2020 edge total) were more dominant than those emerging from forest expansion (9%) (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). Conversely, more edge was removed by forest expansion (15% of the 2000 total) than by forest loss (13%) (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). Area and edge co-declined during 2000–2015, then diverged in 2015–2020 as edge continued to fall while area rose slightly, with the strongest drop in 2000–2005, smaller changes in 2005–2015, and a sharper, decoupled changes in 2015–2020 (Fig. 1d). This net decline in global forest edge length is consistent with recent assessments of increasing fragmentation in the 21st century<sup>19</sup> , whereas our time-resolved analysis further suggests that the pace of edge reorganization has moderated in recent years, revealing temporal variability not captured by cumulative metrics alone. 

### Forest edge dynamics across climatic zones 

We found substantial differences in forest edge length and its temporal dynamics across climate zones (see Methods). In the year 2000, Tropics and Subtropics together accounted for over 45% of global forest edges, while the Temperate and Boreal/Polar zones contributed 32% and 22%, respectively. In terms of temporal dynamics, the Polar zone remained highly stable, with over 2 million km of forest edge in 2000 and only a marginal 1% loss by 2020. In contrast, the Boreal zone, which contained over 68 million km of forest edges in 2000, experienced an 11% decline. Moderate decreases were observed in the Warm and Cool Temperate zones, encompassing around 47 and 55 million km of forest edges in 2000, with respective declines of 3% and 5%. The Tropical and Subtropical zones, starting with 76 million km and 68 million km of forest edges in 2000, saw a reduction of 8% and 9% by 2020 (Fig. 1). 

Patterns of edge dynamics also varied by climatic zones. The Temperate zone contributed the largest share of new edges due to forest loss over 2000–2020 (40% of the global total for this dynamics class; see Methods), whereas the Subtropical and Tropical zones dominated new edges from forest loss (51%). By 2020, Temperate and Boreal regions accounted for the largest shares of edges lost due to forest gains (38% and 30%, respectively), while the Subtropical and Tropical zones contained the majority of edges lost due to forest loss (62%). Yet trajectories were not smooth: they changed in episodes and in several cases decoupled from area. In the temperate belts, area dropped sharply in 2000–2005, was broadly flat during 2005–2015 and rose markedly in 2015–2020; against this backdrop, edge kept contracting in Cool Temperate despite the late area rebound (decoupling), whereas Warm Temperate showed a joint rebound of area and edge after 2015 (re-coupling) (Supplementary Fig. 2a). ### Forest edge dynamics across spatial scales 

Forest edge patterns and dynamics also vary markedly across spatial scales from continental to subnational level. At the continent scale in 2000, more than 70% of the world’s forest edge length was concentrated in Asia (28.8%), North America (21.7%), and Africa (21.6%), closely reflecting their shares of global total forest area (Asia 33.0%, North America 16.7%, and Africa 17.2%). Prominent concentrations occurred along the western coast of North America, the Amazon Basin, and across northwestern, central (Congo Basin), and eastern Africa (Fig. 1b). From 2000 to 2020, Africa and South America showed the largest net edge declines—8.7 million km (−12.5%) and 3.1 million km (−7.3%), respectively—largely associated with deforestation. Asia declined by 5.6 million km (−6.0%), North America by 5.0 million km (−7.1%), and Europe by 1.6 million km (−4.7%), whereas Australia registered a slight net edge gain of 0.06 million km (+0.5%) (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Beyond these totals, forest edge trajectories were episodic rather than gradual, with the magnitude and, in some regions, the direction of change shifting markedly among successive five-year intervals. In several continents, edge length also decoupled from forest area. Across most continents, the largest step occurred at the start of the record (2000–2005), followed by relatively mild changes during 2005–2015; after 2015, trajectories diverged (Supplementary Fig. 2b). In 2015–2020, North America and Europe decoupled, with forest area rebounding while edge length continued to contract. Asia, by contrast, showed a coupled rebound, with area and edge increasing together. Oceania decoupled in the opposite sense, with a pronounced increase in edge despite continued area loss. South America and Africa are largely co-declining across all time periods, with their steepest steps early in the record (Supplementary Fig. 2b). 

Forest edge dynamics due to area change attribution further provide more insights. Asia and North America experienced substantial new edge formation from both forest gain and loss, with Asia leading in edge creation from forest expansion (11%). North America showed more mild edge changes (Supplementary Fig. 1). Conversely, Africa had the highest proportion of edge loss due to deforestation (14%), while Europe and Australia maintained high edge stability (72% and 90% of 2000 forest edges remained unchanged, respectively), reflecting effective forest conservation efforts<sup>20</sup> .

### Implications for assessing global forest landscape pattern 

To fully understand the impacts of global deforestation, reforestation, afforestation, and the change in forest landscape patterns, it is essential to go beyond tracking forest area alone and incorporate forest edge dynamics<sup>32</sup> . Because forest edge length scales with area following a power-law relationship<sup>33,34</sup> , we employed a log transformation to both forest edge length and forest area and used linear regression to analyze their relationship across different countries, thereby avoiding the limitations of a simple edge-to-area ratio, which can misrepresent scale-dependent patterns and bias comparisons across regions of varying sizes (see “Methods”). As expected, we found a strong linear correlation between log-transformed forest edge length and forest area at the country level (Supplementary Fig. 5). Using the year 2000 as a baseline, we established a global average relationship to estimate expected forest edge lengths for each country in both 2000 and 2020. We then compared actual values to these expectations and quantified the residuals. Our analysis revealed substantial disparities in forest landscape patterns among countries (Fig. 4a). 

Notably, tropical countries across Africa, Asia, and South America exhibited shorter-than-expected forest edges relative to the global edge–area relationship (see “Methods”), primarily due to the dominance of large, contiguous primary forests with less fragmented perimeters compared to secondary or managed forests. Interestingly, Japan and South Korea in temperate Asia also displayed shorter-thanexpected forest edges, likely due to successful reforestation efforts and effective urbanization policies that support forest preservation<sup>35</sup> . ## Methods 

### Defining the forest edge 

### Governance may override ecology in driving episodic forest edge dynamics 

Our results suggest that global forest edge changes in the 21st century did not follow a continuous or ecologically gradual path; rather, they often exhibited episodic changes closely tied to land-use decisions and governance interventions (SupplementaryFigs. 3 and 4). The strongest signals of forest area decline and edge expansion were concentrated in the early 2000s, whereas changes became progressively weaker after 2015, with a modest rebound in forest area and a slowing of edge reorganization. This pattern suggests a possible attenuation of fragmentation pressures in the most recent period, consistent with growing influence of forest governance, restoration initiatives, and natural recovery following earlier disturbances. These observed patterns suggest that policy, more than ecological processes alone, may play a dominant role in shaping forest landscape structure over decadal timescales. 

For instance, major decreases in forest edge length due to forest loss in Africa were not evident until after 2005 but have since continued steadily. Meanwhile, natural processes such as fires have continued to influence edge dynamics, contributing to persistent edge expansion in West Africa and Siberia. In Europe and North America (especially the eastern U.S.), the direction and magnitude of forest edge dynamics shifted between 2000–2005 and 2015–2020, likely in response to evolving forest management practices and policy priorities<sup>36,37</sup> . In several regions, the late-period stabilization or partial recovery of forest area coincides with expanded conservation programs and reforestation efforts, suggesting that policy actions may help moderate earlier trajectories of forest fragmentation. A particularly striking case is China, where large-scale afforestation initiatives launched after 2015 led to rapid gains and localized edge expansion, even though overall national forest edge length decreased<sup>27</sup> . These patterns illustrate how policy changes, such as new conservation incentives, logging bans, or large-scale reforestation efforts, may be associated with pronounced changes in forest edge structure over relatively short periods. 

These episodic transitions highlight the asymmetric pace at which human and ecological processes operate. Governance can rapidly alter disturbance regimes through policy, markets, and land-use decisions, whereas ecological responses such as forest regrowth and structural recovery typically unfold more gradually. As a result, forest landscapes may exhibit periods of rapid reorganization followed by phases of relative stabilization. Importantly, our analysis shows that forest edge length can decline even when changes in forest area are modest, underscoring that structural reconfiguration, including patch merging or infilling, can reduce edge exposure without large area gains. This decoupling emphasizes the value of tracking forest morphology alongside traditional area-based indicators. 

Rather than implying direct causality, our findings point to a strong correspondence between changes in forest edge dynamics and broader socio-ecological changes, highlighting the role of governance as a key contextual factor shaping landscape trajectories. Effective conservation therefore requires moving beyond forest area targets to incorporate edge-sensitive perspectives that consider habitat configuration, connectivity, and exposure. The observed weakening of edge expansion and modest area recovery after 2015 provides cautious evidence that coordinated management and natural regeneration can slow or partially reverse fragmentation changes, although substantial regional variation remains. The forest edge is defined as the boundary between the forest and neighboring non-forest land covers, such as grasslands, agricultural lands, or urban areas<sup>2</sup> . Forest definitions vary depending on research purposes. In this study, we used the University of Maryland’s definition<sup>21</sup> , which identifies any Landsat pixel representing tree heights of 5 m or above as forest, which is concordant with the FAO guidelines<sup>20</sup> . Accordingly, we defined a forest edge pixel by the fourneighbor rule as any forest pixel that shares a boundary with a nonforest pixel in any of the four cardinal directions (up, down, left, or right). 

### Forest extent data 

Resolution is important in forest edge mapping. Although various products are available for generating global land cover maps, creating a reliable global forest edge map is hampered by the coarse spatial resolutions (250–1000 m) of these satellite products: These resolutions miss the crucial edge effects, which vary with distance from the forest edge, being most prominent within the first 100 m<sup>38,39</sup> . Conversely, high-resolution satellite data (<10-m resolution) may also be unsuitable for mapping forest edges due to the infinite coastline paradox, whereby increased resolutions lead to ostensibly limitless perimeter measurements such as mature tree canopies often span several meters across. Therefore, moderate-resolution sensors, such as Sentinel-2 and Landsat, with 10 to 30-m resolution, respectively, are more appropriate for forest edge mapping. To date, a few products have been developed with moderate-resolution data with time series, such as Hansen’s forest cover map and Sentinel-2 10 m Land Use/Land Cover data<sup>22,40</sup> . However, these products either primarily focus on tracking canopy cover loss and gain or provide data for only single years or relatively short periods compared to the lifespan of tree growth, thus impeding the ability to capture dynamic changes. 

We used the medium-resolution forest extent data from the University of Maryland’s Global Land Cover and Land Use Monitoring in Support of Environmental Sustainability project to map the forest edge<sup>21</sup> . This binary data provides forest extent for 2000 to 2020 with 5-year gaps, with an overall accuracy above 97% and a producer accuracy above 94%. It is structured on the WGS 1984 coordinate system, featuring a spatial resolution of 0.00025° (approximately 30 m near the equator and 10 m in the Arctic). All forest edge mapping and statistical analyses were conducted at this original resolution. The global forest extent data is stored as 261 10° × 10° chunks. 

### Limitations and potential uncertainty brought by land cover data 

Although the high accuracy of the dataset minimizes potential misclassification, errors of omission (undetected forest pixels) and commission (misclassified non-forest pixels) may introduce minor biases in estimating forest edge dynamics. Omission errors could lead to a slight underestimation of forest edge length, whereas commission errors could marginally overestimate edge length by creating artificial boundaries where none exist. However, because our study tracks edge dynamics over multiple five-year intervals (2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020), rather than relying on a single-time-step comparison, the influence of individual misclassifications is minimized. The multitemporal approach ensures that our results reflect actual forest-edge transitions rather than artifacts from classification inconsistencies. ### Mapping the global forest edge and area 

We used a raster-based method for mapping forest edges globally. First, we identified forest edge pixels based on the previously defined criteria of forest edge. Once forest edges were identified, we converted the spatial resolution from degrees to meters to obtain meaningful edge length values. Since each pixel in the original dataset measures 0.00025° × 0.00025°, its exact size in meters varies with latitude. To account for this, we used the geodesic distance function from the “geopy” package (https://geopy. readthedocs.io/en/stable/#module-geopy.distance) in Python to calculate pixel dimensions at different latitudes. Each pixel at the spatial resolution of 0.00025° was then assigned a value equal to the total length of its forest edges, and all non-edge pixels were set to zero. To ensure spatial continuity in edge mapping across the dataset’s 10° × 10° chunks, we applied the edge detection method consistently across both interior and exterior pixels. To prevent edge discontinuities at chunk boundaries, overlapping exterior pixels were spliced together, preserving a seamless global forest edge dataset. Independent validation using very high–resolution imagery confirms that the mapped forest edges and their temporal dynamics are consistent with observed landscape transitions (see “Manual validation of forest edge dynamics”). For visualization purposes, we aggregated the results to a coarser resolution of 0.01° (~1 km²) using a sum aggregation method. This step was performed exclusively to enhance readability in figure visualization (Figs. 1, 3 and Supplementary Figs. 1, 3, 4) without altering the underlying high-resolution dataset used for analysis. Additionally, we calculated the area of each forest pixel at its original resolution (0.00025°). Since the forest extent data is stored in degrees, we used the geodesic distance function from the “geopy” package in Python to convert pixel dimensions into meters. This ensured that area calculations accounted for variations in pixel size due to latitude. The final forest area maps, like the edge length maps, maintain the original resolution but are presented in square meters for interpretability. All of the computations were done in the Center for High Throughput Computing at the University of Wisconsin-Madison<sup>42</sup> . 

While our approach accurately captures forest edge dynamics at a high resolution, one limitation is that edge length calculations do not incorporate local terrain slope variations. The geodesic distance function we applied assumes a flat Earth surface, meaning that in mountainous regions, where forests are often restricted, actual edge lengths along the land surface may be underestimated. This effect is particularly relevant for forest edges created by natural disturbances such as landslides or avalanche chutes. Future refinements could 

integrate high-resolution digital elevation models to improve slopeadjusted edge length calculations in topographically complex areas. 

### Forest edge dynamic mapping and statistics 

Forest edge dynamics were quantified by jointly evaluating changes in forest edge length and forest area between two reference years, allowing edge changes arising from forest expansion, forest loss, and structural reconfiguration to be distinguished in a deterministic and spatially explicit manner. Based on the direction of change in forest edge length and forest area, we identified five types of forest edge dynamics: stable edges, new edges due to forest gains (increaseincrease edges), new edges due to forest loss (increase-decrease edges), edges lost due to forest gains area (decrease-increase edges), and edges lost due to forest loss (decrease-decrease edges). Forest edge dynamics were computed for successive 5-year intervals (2000 to 2005, 2005 to 2010, 2010 to 2015, and 2015 to 2020), as well as for the full 2000 to 2020 period, with the earlier year in each comparison used as the baseline for quantifying changes in forest edge length and forest area. 

Taking the 2000 vs. 2020 comparison as an example, we defined raster layers Area2000 and Area2020 to represent forest area, and Edge2000 and Edge2020 to represent pixel-level forest edge length for 2000 and 2020, respectively. Forest area and forest edge length were computed independently for each year using the same forest definition and spatial resolution. Edge dynamics therefore reflect differences between independently derived layers rather than iterative or cumulative calculations. ### Manual validation of forest edge dynamics 

To evaluate the reliability of the mapped forest edge dataset and its temporal dynamics, we conducted an independent validation using very high–resolution (VHR) imagery available in Google Earth. 100 validation sites were selected globally using a stratified random sampling approach from the 0.01° (~1 km at the equator) analysis grid, with each site representing a local landscape window centered on a sampled pixel, including dense forests, sparse woodlands, and regions experiencing edge expansion, contraction, or stability (Supplementary Fig. 7). 

Visual interpretation focused on evaluating whether mapped forest–nonforest boundaries and their changes through time corresponded to observable landscape patterns. Because forest delineation is inherently definition-dependent, particularly in sparsely vegetated or transitional ecosystems, validation emphasized structural agreement rather than strict pixel-level correspondence. heterogeneous landscapes, it can be difficult to determine visually whether vegetation meets the forest definition (tree height larger than 5 m), and such ambiguity reflects the gradual nature of forest boundaries rather than mapping errors. These uncertainties are expected to be greatest in sparse forests and ecotonal regions and were considered when interpreting agreement. 

Temporal availability of VHR imagery varies geographically and historically. For each reference year, we used the most recent available imagery within approximately two years of that year to represent ground conditions, prioritizing scenes acquired during the growing season and excluding images with substantial cloud cover. When multiple images were available, the most recent cloud-free growingseason image was selected. Sites lacking sufficient imagery coverage were excluded from dynamic validation to avoid introducing interpretation uncertainty. In addition, because the forest edge maps are produced at five-year intervals and represent conditions aggregated over a period rather than a single acquisition date, some rapidly changing landscapes may exhibit partial temporal mismatches between observed imagery and mapped conditions. 

To evaluate edge dynamics, we compared the temporal evolution of forest patterns in the VHR imagery with the corresponding periods represented by the forest edge dataset. Specifically, we aligned the analysis window with the time span over which high-resolution imagery was available and assessed whether observed changes indicated expansion, contraction, fragmentation, merging, or stability. Only sites with interpretable temporal sequences were included in dynamic agreement assessments. 

Interpretation was conducted independently by two reviewers to reduce subjective bias. Inter-reviewer agreement was evaluated, and discrepancies were resolved using a conservative decision rule, whereby the lowest-quality assessment was retained whenever disagreement occurred. 

Static edge presence showed high consistency with visual interpretation, with an overall agreement of 96.5% across all comparisons (Supplementary Data 2 and Supplementary Fig. 8). Year-specific match rates ranged from 91.3% in 2000 to 98.9% in 2020, reflecting both improvements in imagery availability and clearer delineation of persistent forest boundaries. For sites with interpretable change signals, the direction of edge dynamics showed full agreement between mapped products and visual interpretation. 

We note that discrepancies may arise in areas undergoing rapid or ongoing land-cover change. Small discrepancies are expected in regions undergoing rapid or continuous change because very high–resolution imagery captures conditions at specific acquisition dates, whereas the forest edge dataset represents annual conditions within five-year intervals. Such temporal differences likely make agreement estimates conservative. It is also important to recognize that the validation relies on visual comparison between high-resolution (~1 m) imagery and Landsatderived (~30 m) forest edge maps, which operate at fundamentally different spatial scales. For example, narrow linear features such as small roads and trails are clearly visible in sub-meter resolution imagery but may not be resolved at Landsat resolution, particularly during the growing season when tree canopies overhang and obscure these features (Supplementary Fig. 9). Consequently, some apparent inconsistencies between predicted edges and highresolution imagery reflect inherent scale differences rather than mapping errors. This scale mismatch further supports the conservative nature of our accuracy estimates. 

Because the edge detection algorithm is a deterministic transformation of a validated forest extent product, this validation primarily evaluates whether derived edge patterns and their temporal evolution correspond to observable landscape structure rather than model parameter uncertainty. Overall, the results indicate that the forest edge dataset robustly captures both spatial configuration and dynamic 

changes of forest boundaries at the global scale while acknowledging expected uncertainty in transitional environments. These considerations reflect inherent challenges in global forest monitoring and do not affect the interpretation of large-scale patterns in forest edge dynamics.
