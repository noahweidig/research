# Fire risk to U.S. structures nearly triples by mid-century

## Abstract

Fire is a nationwide crisis, driven by climate change, land use, and continued development into fire-prone landscapes. As these processes blur the boundary between wildland fuels and the built environment, living with fire is becoming a reality for a growing number of communities. We reconstruct fire risk to structures from 1945-2023 and project its evolution through 2060 across the U.S. using high-resolution historical data on area burned, settlement patterns, and fire impacts. Under business-as-usual conditions with no adaptation, structural losses are projected to nearly triple nationwide, with 133,200–449,100 buildings expected to be damaged or destroyed by mid-century. The West remains the epicenter of losses. There, 30% of census tracts projected to face the highest fire risk intersect with socioeconomic hardship, potentially placing the greatest losses in some of the most vulnerable communities. Coupled with an eastward expansion of risk already underway, these findings signal a narrowing window for targeted intervention.

The escalating fire crisis across the contiguous United States (CONUS) is testing the limits of emergency response and community resilience. Despite annual firefighting expenditures exceeding US$3 billion, recent years have seen the most destructive and lethal fires on record, with economic losses surpassing suppression budgets by more than a factor of 130<sup>1</sup> . While increases in fire size, frequency, and growth rate<sup>2,3</sup> pose growing threats to homes, livelihoods, and public safety, changes in fire alone do not fully account for the sharp rise in structural losses observed in recent years<sup>4</sup> . Instead, this trend stems from a confluence of factors: sprawling development into flammable terrain, heightened structural and social vulnerability, and widespread landscape modification, all compounded by human- and climate-driven lengthening of the fire season<sup>5–7</sup> . 

Since the mid-1970s, housing expansion into fire-prone regions has accelerated substantially, resulting in a tenfold increase in the extent of build-up land in fire hotspots<sup>8</sup> . Much of this growth has occurred as low- and medium-density developments that abut or intermix with vegetated areas<sup>9,10</sup> , often driven by affordability and enabled by lenient land-use regulation<sup>11,12</sup> . These settlement patterns 

render the term “wildfire” increasingly untethered from wildlands and more reflective of a broader fire hazardscape, one in which communities find themselves on the frontline of fire–human interactions. Development and risky land use thus continue along entrenched paths shaped by market forces and supported by public infrastructure investments, even in the face of repeated losses<sup>13,14</sup> . 

This persistent pattern calls for forward-looking assessments that anticipate the consequences of evolving fire dynamics and continued development under current trajectories. We reconstruct historical fire risk to structures from 1945 to 2023, and project its evolution across the CONUS through 2060. We adopt the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change definition of fire risk as the expected annual structural loss, arising from the interaction of fire occurrence (hazard), the presence of structures in harm’s way (exposure), and the underlying social and structural vulnerability<sup>15</sup> . These components are not static; they are shaped by ecological and historical legacies and continually reconfigured by land-use decisions. As a result, fire risk is spatially heterogeneous, temporally dynamic, and crucially, amenable to intervention.

Drawing on decades of high-resolution data on fire, development, and structural loss, linked to climate projections, our analysis addresses two common shortcomings in most existing assessments. First, exposure is often treated as static, with hazard evaluated against a fixed snapshot of the built environment even as development continues to expand into fire-prone landscapes<sup>16</sup> . Second, settlement patterns are typically represented only coarsely<sup>17</sup> , limiting the ability to capture how expansion and densification interact with changing fire dynamics. By jointly projecting fire (hazard) and development (exposure), this high-resolution, multi-source, data-driven assessment quantifies how changing fire dynamics and continued development reshape structural fire risk across the United States. Informed by past losses and current socioeconomic vulnerabilities, our findings reveal a shifting geography of risk that challenges long-held assumptions, demonstrating that without strategic action fire’s toll is poised to grow not only in well-known hotspots but also in places long assumed to be relatively safe (Fig. 1). 

## Results 

Guided by the hazard-exposure-vulnerability approach, we evaluated how changes in these components shape spatial and temporal variations in fire risk to the built environment. Hazard is represented by observed and projected burned area, exposure by the distribution and number of structures, and vulnerability by the Exposure-Adjusted Structural Loss Index (EASLI), a metric derived from historical observations that captures how settlement configuration and fire characteristics influence structural losses. We first examine each component individually and then integrate them to estimate fire risk to structures, which we subsequently interpret in the context of current socioeconomic vulnerability. 

### Burned area to double by 2060, with regional disparities 

To quantify the evolving fire hazard, we relied on the spatiotemporal wildfire model developed by Joseph et al., which integrates a 30-year wildfire record with meteorological, climatic, and housing-density data in a Bayesian hierarchical framework<sup>18,19</sup> . The model jointly predicts fire occurrence and burned area using spatially varying nonlinear effects, representing fire counts with a zero-inflated negative binomial distribution and fire sizes with a lognormal distribution to estimate the probability of wildfire events. In our implementation, the model was driven by an ensemble of weather and climate variables from three General Circulation Models (Centre National de Recherches Météorologiques Model (CNRM), Hadley Centre Global Environmental Model 

- Earth System version (HadGEM-ES), and Meteorological Research Institute Model (MRI)) under the RCP 4.5 emissions scenario, a commonly used stabilization pathway representing moderate mitigation outcomes<sup>20</sup> . Fire projections carry inherent uncertainties that extend beyond the selection of climate models or emissions pathways, arising from interactions with vegetation dynamics, evolving suppression technologies, fuel management strategies, and shifts in land-use policies. Given these uncertainties, our results should be interpreted as plausible scenarios rather than precise forecasts. 

Observational data suggest that cumulative burned area surged in recent decades; a trend set to intensify (Fig. 2). Between 1985 and 2023, nearly 589 million hectares burned across the country, with 85% concentrated in the West (Fig. 2 A-D). Yet the eastern CONUS also faces a worsening fire hazard. Consistent with prior estimates<sup>21</sup> , our projections forecast a doubling of annual area burned nationwide by mid-century, relative to 2011–2020 (mean growth factor for the CONUS: 2.10; Fig. 2 E-G). The western U.S. is poised for the most pronounced rise, with annual area burned tripling (growth factor: 3.6), while the Central U.S. will also see substantial increases (growth factor: 1.7). In contrast, some regions in the southern and eastern U.S. will experience declining or stable fire activity, in accordance with spatial variations anticipated by Barbero et al.<sup>22</sup> , who attributed these trends to climate and land-use changes. 

Though the West will retain the highest absolute burned area, increased fire activity in the grasslands and prairies of the Central U.S. is especially notable, reflecting shifts in fuel and drying trends<sup>23</sup> . These patterns align with forecasts of heightened fire severity in historically fire-prone areas and emergent fire hotspots in regions previously considered low-fire potential<sup>24,25</sup> . As a result, cumulative burned area between 2023-2060 will exceed 857-1,059 million hectares in the West, 66-111 million in the Central region, and 17-35 million in the East (Fig. 2 H-J). The emerging picture is thus one of escalating CONUS-wide fire hazard marked by regional disparities, where sharp increases in burned area in the West and Central U.S. contrast with pockets of relative stability or even reduced fire activity in parts of the South and East (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. 1).

The models performed robustly across regions, with fewer than 2% of the ecoregions exhibiting deviance explained below 25% in both training and validation data sets (Supplementary Fig. 2). Further, 73.6% of those in the Central region, 77.2% in the East, and 46.7% in the West exceeded 75% deviance explained in both data sets, highlighting the models’ ability to generalize to unseen data and supporting their use for longterm exposure assessments. 

Historical housing data indicate that nearly 32 million residential structures existed across CONUS in 1945, 73% of which were concentrated in the East. By 2005, that number had more than quadrupled, followed by sustained development, albeit at a slower pace (Fig. 3A–B, Supplementary Figs. 3-4). Despite generalized growth, regional differences were pronounced (Fig. 3B). From 1945 to 2005, structure density in the West increased by a factor of 6.15, compared to fourfold increases in both the Central and East regions. These findings echo prior studies documenting rapid urban and rural expansion in the West and South<sup>28,29</sup> . The early 2000s ushered in further shifts, including deindustrialization and economic stagnation in urban cores, job growth in suburban business districts, and exurban sprawl<sup>30</sup> . Our reconstructions mirror these trends, showing increased structural density in historically lower/moderate-density regions, particularly in the West, and suggesting a reorganization of growth rather than a uniform increase in density. 

Although development has slowed since 2005, growth is projected to continue through 2060 (Fig. 3C-E, Supplementary Figs. 3-4). The slowdown in construction after the mid-2000s coincides with the peak and subsequent collapse of the U.S. housing market and the broader financial crisis, which sharply reduced housing starts, tightened mortgage credit, and depressed residential investment for much of the following decade<sup>31</sup> . In parallel, a growing body of research suggests that increasingly restrictive local land-use regulation, including environmental review processes and other procedural barriers, has limited housing supply in many metropolitan areas even as population and housing demand have continued to grow<sup>32</sup> . Many jurisdictions have adopted “smart growth” approaches emphasizing mixed-use zoning, transit-oriented development, and green infrastructure to reduce automobile dependence, and preserve open space<sup>28,33,34</sup> . These policies often encourage infill redevelopment and higher-density construction within existing urbanized areas rather than expansion into undeveloped landscapes. Consistent with these trends, our projections suggest continued but spatially reorganized growth, with the East remaining the densest region overall, but future development proceeding most rapidly in the West. This emerging pattern likely reflects the combined effects of post-recession housing market dynamics and evolving land-use policies aimed at balancing population growth, housing demand, and environmental sustainability. 

Past and future occupancy trends lend further confidence to our projections. High cross-correlation functions (CCF) between modeled structure counts and various Socioeconomic Shared Pathways-based population projections<sup>35</sup> reveal a tight coupling between housing growth and demographic trends, particularly at lag zero (Supplementary Fig. 5). Such coupling is consistent with findings by Wan et al.<sup>36</sup> , who demonstrate that high-resolution Built-Up Property Records (BUPR) from HISDAC-US closely track the spatial distribution of human settlement and can reproduce fine-scale population patterns across the United States. 

High CCFs at lag zero further suggest that housing development has historically followed, rather than anticipated, population increases. Under moderate socioeconomic and emissions scenarios, average household size is expected to fluctuate between 2.5 and 2.7 individuals 

per household<sup>37</sup> , values consistent with our projected occupancy rates of 2-3 individuals per structure under the SSP2 scenario. SSP2 is a “middle-of-the-road” population scenario driven by moderate population growth, uneven economic development, and moderate progress toward sustainability<sup>38</sup> . This pattern persists across all regions, though localized deviations may arise in areas where population decline outpaces housing growth (Supplementary Fig. 6), particularly in parts of the Midwest and Rust Belt<sup>32</sup> . 

### Fires are similarly destructive per area burned nationwide 

Fire risk to structures has traditionally been quantified as the product of the probability of fire occurrence in a given area and the number of structures located within it<sup>15</sup> . While spatially explicit and clear, this framework rests on the simplifying assumptions that (i) fire characteristics are homogeneous, and (ii) structures are equally vulnerable and evenly distributed across the landscape. These assumptions obscure the well-established role of fire behavior in driving structural loss<sup>3</sup> , as well as the key influence of the wildland–urban interface (WUI) in shaping both fire behavior and property exposure<sup>39,40</sup> . 

To account for the strong influence of landscape configuration on losses, we estimated risk within small, relatively homogeneous units (EPA Level IV ecoregions). Rather than constraining the analysis to a predefined WUI boundary, we integrated more than two decades of structural damage records<sup>41</sup> , settlement data<sup>26,42,43</sup> , and fire perimeters<sup>44</sup> to derive the Exposure-Adjusted Structure Loss Index (EASLI). EASLI is defined as the annual proportion of structures damaged or destroyed during 2000-2023, scaled by the annual burned-area proportion over the same period, thereby expressing structural loss relative to both exposure and fire extent. 

This empirical metric captures historical patterns of structure loss and reflects multiple dimensions of structural vulnerability, including the spatial arrangement of buildings, settlement patterns, and the built environment’s role as combustible fuel, and can be interpreted as the proportion of the built environment likely to intersect with wildfire activity. In this sense, EASLI generalizes the WUI concept by capturing gradients of fire–development interaction across landscapes rather than relying on a binary classification, allowing WUI-like conditions to emerge directly from observed relationships between wildfire activity and the built environment without imposing the fixed vegetation thresholds or housing-density cutoffs inherent to specific WUI products. Because fire hazard varies strongly across space, areas where intense development occurs in low-hazard environments (e.g., urban cores) contribute little to regional structural loss, while landscapes where development intersects flammable vegetation dominate the signal captured by the index. Aggregating EASLI at the Level IV ecoregion scale therefore reflects the dominant context of each region, collapsing multiple dimensions of settlement configuration into a single metric that does not resolve within-region heterogeneity but enables cross-region comparison. 

We held EASLI constant through 2060. This stationarity assumption aligns with our goal of producing ‘business-as-usual’ projections, in which longstanding regulatory and planning practices persist unchanged. In practice, this assumption is unrealistic, as building codes, land-use policies, and mitigation programs are already evolving in many states. For example, new wildfire-resistant building standards have recently been proposed or adopted in places such as Colorado and California. Nevertheless, holding EASLI constant provides a nonaction baseline that isolates the influence of climate-driven hazard changes and development trajectories, establishing a benchmark against which the impacts of future mitigation strategies can be evaluated and priorities for mitigation identified.

Uncertainty in EASLI estimates varies across regions (Supplementary Table 1). Multiplicative dispersion, quantified as the geometric standard deviation (GSD) of bootstrap realizations within each ecoregion, is highest in the West (mean GSD = 2.66), intermediate in the East (mean GSD = 2.06), and lowest in the Central U.S. (mean GSD = 1.61). Bootstrap-based rank probabilities further clarify the 

robustness of these patterns (Fig. 4D; Supplementary Fig. 7). Across 2000 resampled realizations, several western ecoregions consistently fall within the highest EASLI quantiles (top 20%), whereas the lowest ranks (bottom 20%) occur predominantly in the Central and East regions (Supplementary Fig. 8). Most ecoregions, by definition, occupy intermediate EASLI ranks (middle 60%), but rank assignment is generally more stable in the West. In contrast, a few Central and East ecoregions shift between intermediate and high EASLI classes across bootstrap realizations, indicating that although fires in these regions can generate high exposure-adjusted losses, such outcomes are less consistently expressed in the historical record. This uncertainty propagates into our risk projections and should be considered when interpreting regional contrasts. 

### Fire risk to structures nearly triples nationally by 2060 

Based on projected hazard, structure counts, and the ExposureAdjusted Structure Loss Index (EASLI), we estimated fire risk to structures (i.e., the expected number of structures annually damaged or destroyed) across the CONUS from 2023 to 2060. Although the magnitude of change varies across models, all show a clear trend: fire risk increases steadily, with the most pronounced increases in the West and in the northern portions of the Central and Eastern regions (Figs. 1, 5 A-C, and Supplementary Fig. 9). By 2051–2060, risk is projected to be 3.2 times greater than in 2011–2020 under HadGEM2-ES, 2.27 times greater under MRI, and 1.85 times greater under CNRM, signaling a near-tripling of risk nationwide by mid-century (Fig. 5D-G). Cumulatively, these increases translate to an estimated 133,200 to 449,100 structures damaged or destroyed between 2024 and 2060, with annual losses reaching 700–2350 structures per million (Fig. 5H-J).

Beyond these national estimates, regional patterns reveal substantial heterogeneity (Fig. 1). The West, already the nation’s highestrisk region, is projected to experience a 2.18- to 3.72-fold increase relative to 2011–2020 (3.72-fold under HadGEM2-ES, 2.90-fold under MRI, and 2.18-fold under CNRM), with cumulative losses of 261,000 to 319,000 structures. However, the Central and Eastern United States, traditionally considered lower-risk areas, show striking relative increases. Risk in the Central region, although lower in absolute terms (20,700 to 29,200 cumulative structural losses between 2024 and 2060), is projected to rise by as much as 5.54-fold under HadGEM2-ES relative to 2011–2020. The Eastern United States, with cumulative losses between 53,400 and 136,300 structures over the same period, also shows a marked uptick, with growth rates exceeding 5.91-fold in certain scenarios. These projections foreshadow a future in which fire losses are no longer confined to the West and suggest a growing misalignment between historical perceptions and the evolving geography of risk (Fig. 5E–J). 

### Risk varies spatially with exposure, temporally with hazard 

Decomposition of fire risk into its three components, i.e., proportion of area burned (hazard), number of structures (exposure), and the Exposure-Adjusted Structure Loss Index (EASLI; vulnerability), reveals a clear separation between the factors governing its spatial structure and those driving its temporal variability. Spatial attribution indicates that all three components contribute meaningfully to the geographic distribution of fire risk, but their relative importance varies regionally. Nationwide, spatial variation in risk is primarily shaped by the number of structures (mean exposure share = 58 + /− 0.04% in the West; 56 + /− 0.07% in the Central; and 55 + /− 0.05% in the East region). In the 

Central and East regions, however, where overall fire activity is lower, area burned plays a more prominent role in differentiating high- and low-risk ecoregions (Fig. 6A–F). With few exceptions, the relative dominance of these drivers is projected to remain stable through midcentury, although their magnitude is expected to grow (Fig. 6G-I; Supplementary Fig. 10). 

In 2011–2020, the contribution of EASLI to spatial variability in risk was significantly lower than that of hazard and exposure (mean EASLI share = 16 + /- 0.03% in the West; 14 + /- 0.08% in the Central; and 12 + /- 0.08% in the East region) but reached values of up to ~30% in certain ecoregions. This indicates that, although fire activity and the built environment establish the baseline level of risk, EASLI captures important spatial variation in structural vulnerability that is not explained by hazard or exposure alone. EASLI exhibits relatively high uncertainty in parts of the Great Plains (Central region), as well as a few ecoregions in the northwestern and southeastern US (Fig. 4). This uncertainty is amplified in areas where EASLI contributes moderately to risk and should be considered when interpreting our results. 

In contrast, decomposition of temporal changes in risk reveals that variability is dominated by changes in fire activity across all regions (90% in the West, 91% in the Central, and 97% in the East), a pattern projected to persist through mid-century. By construction, EASLI remains temporally constant in our framework and therefore does not contribute to temporal variability in risk. This contrast reveals a fundamental distinction in fire risk dynamics: spatial patterns are shaped by exposure and influenced by hazard and structural vulnerability, whereas interannual variability is almost entirely driven by burned area.

## Discussion 

Nationwide, fire risk to the built environment is projected to nearly triple by mid-century, with multiple states facing even steeper four- to six-fold increases (Figs. 1, 5). Because our framework assumes moderate structural exposure, conservative climate projections, and a static Exposure-Adjusted Structural Loss Index (EASLI), these projections represent a business-as-usual trajectory that reliably captures the central tendency of an evolving risk landscape. However, ongoing development trends marked by outward expansion<sup>8</sup> , increasingly fast-moving fires<sup>3</sup> , and evolving interactions between fire and the built environment may elevate the probability of large suburban and urban conflagrations. If such tail events become more frequent, structural losses could exceed the business-as-usual projections presented here. 

Historical records help place such extreme events in context. ICS209 incident reports indicate that 205,096 structures were damaged or destroyed nationwide between 2000 and 2023. Although these losses are substantial, most wildfires do not affect the built environment. Only ~0.17% of recorded fires account for all structural losses, and 32% of total losses are concentrated among the five most destructive fires. This heavy-tailed distribution indicates that regional and national totals are dominated by a small number of catastrophic events rather than by the typical fire season. Our results support this interpretation: temporal variability in area burned explains more than 90% of the interannual variability in structure losses prior to 2024 (90% 

in the West, 91% in the Central, and 97% in the East), a pattern expected to persist through mid-century (Fig. 6). 

The January 2025 Los Angeles fires exemplify this dynamic. Together, the Eaton and Palisades fires destroyed roughly 16,000 structures and caused tens of billions of dollars in insured losses, making them among the most destructive wildfire disasters in modern U.S. history. Relative to the historical record, this single disaster represents about four times the median annual structural losses observed nationwide between 2001 and 2023. Looking forward, damages in this event alone correspond to 7–23% of the cumulative national losses projected for the entire 2024–2060 period (133,200–449,100 structures). Consistent with historical patterns, future fire losses will likely materialize through a series of very severe events rather than through steady year-to-year increases. 

The episodic nature of structural losses makes the emerging geography of fire risk especially consequential. The West, and California in particular, will remain the epicenter of fire risk, but emerging hotspots across the Central and Eastern regions raise the prospect of fires progressively threatening less prepared communities. The Chimney Tops 2 Fire, which spread from Great Smoky Mountains National Park into Gatlinburg/Pigeon Forge, Tennessee, burning over 2000 structures, demonstrates that the East is not immune to wildland fires becoming urban conflagrations<sup>48,49</sup> . In regions with historically infrequent fire, these events tend to have outsized impacts, often amplified by limited community preparedness. Indeed, we find that when fires occur in the East they can be as destructive as in the West (Fig. 4), highlighting the need for mitigation approaches that address both chronic exposure and episodic, high-consequence events. 

The intensifying and expanding fire risk arises from the interplay between rising fire hazard and the exposure of homes. Despite regional differences in moisture availability, vegetation, and land use, the annual number of fires nearly doubled in the West and East and

Human activity has also played a direct role in reshaping recent fire patterns. Over the past two decades, human ignitions started 84% of the fires that threatened communities, tripling fire season length and expanding the area susceptible to fires, especially in the central and eastern U.S.<sup>50</sup> . These factors, exacerbated by a long-standing history of fire suppression, have contributed a 6.2-fold increase in median area burned in the Central U.S. and a quadrupling in the West since the late 20th century<sup>2</sup> . This trend is projected to continue in the coming decades, nearly doubling the annual area burned by 2060 relative to present (Fig. 2). 

Although year-to-year dynamics in losses are overwhelmingly governed by fluctuations in fire, spatially, this pattern collapses. Instead, the geography of fire risk is shaped by the combined influence of exposure, hazard, and vulnerability, with exposure dominating nationwide (mean share = 56%). In regions with lower baseline fire activity, particularly the Central and Eastern U.S., variation in area burned plays a comparatively larger role in differentiating high- and low-risk ecoregions, suggesting that patterns of human development have thus profoundly shaped where and how fire risk manifests. 

These spatial patterns naturally raise the question of how the built environment has evolved across the landscape. Our reconstructed development trajectory (Fig. 3) aligns with well-documented historical events, particularly post-World War II suburbanization driven by population growth, economic expansion, and federal housing and transportation policies, including mortgage guarantees from the Federal Housing Administration and Veterans Administration and the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956, which collectively facilitated commuting and reinforced low-density metropolitan expansion. 

The profound transformation of the built environment across the CONUS since 1945 thus reflects the convergence of postwar economic policies, demographic shifts, technological change, and regional landuse decisions. In responding to these social and economic forces, development has also inadvertently increased the exposure of people and homes to fire<sup>8</sup> . Addressing fire risk, then, demands more than a focus on hazard alone; it requires reckoning with historical legacies and confronting the choices we make about how, where, and under what conditions we build. 

Development not only increases the number of assets exposed but also contributes to the conditions that elevate the probability of fire occurrence and spread. As more buildings and infrastructure are added to flammable landscapes, combustible materials are introduced, and the likelihood of ignition rises via electrical faults, equipment failures, and human carelessness<sup>51,52</sup> . The 2018 Camp Fire in Paradise, California, sparked by a faulty powerline, exemplifies this dual role. Its catastrophic toll was driven by a convergence of natural and human elements, limited evacuation routes, and deep-seated social vulnerabilities<sup>53–55</sup> . Similar dynamics played out in the 2021 Marshall Fire in Colorado and the devastating fires in Hawai’i shortly thereafter. These events show that risk, both present and future, is deeply embedded in the compounding legacies of social and structural vulnerability. In effect, past settlement patterns have locked in a baseline of structural exposure and vulnerability across much of the country (Fig. 4), setting risk trajectories that are unlikely to shift without deliberate, sustained intervention. 

A key manifestation of this legacy is the expanding overlap between people and flammable landscapes. Where the built environment meets or intermingles with undeveloped vegetation, frequently referred to as the wildland–urban interface (WUI), communities increasingly find themselves on the frontline of fire-human interactions. Globally, WUI areas are expanding rapidly, intensifying human and built environment exposure to fire hazard<sup>9,10</sup> . The CONUS is no 

exception, with multiple studies documenting increasing exposure of people, buildings, and infrastructure to fires, primarily due to development encroaching into fire-prone landscapes<sup>56–58</sup> . 

Our projections incorporate these interactions through the Exposure-Adjusted Structure Loss Index (EASLI; Fig. 4), a spatially explicit measure of structural vulnerability that captures how development and fire activity co-occur across landscapes. Because this structural context is held constant through 2060, our findings trace a status-quo trajectory in which the spatial patterns of development and vulnerability observed today persist into the future. They therefore highlight the scale of the challenge should current fire and development trajectories and structural vulnerabilities persist and constitute a call to move beyond reactive fire suppression toward scienceinformed, proactive strategies aligned with the scale of change already underway<sup>59</sup> . Each home built today represents a commitment to future exposure, and every policy, enacted or deferred, helps shape where and how that risk unfolds in the years ahead. 

Adapting to this new fire reality, nonetheless, requires contending not only with future growth but also with the legacy of past development. Neighborhoods already built into fire-prone landscapes cannot simply be relocated, yet the risks they face are increasing. Retrofitting homes, expanding defensible space, and upgrading aging infrastructure will be critical steps toward adaptation. But resilience does not rest on engineering alone. The feasibility of these interventions is shaped and constrained by inequality, and so is the burden of fire risk. We find that while the expansion of fire risk is occurring broadly, 30% of census tracts projected to face the highest levels of fire risk by midcentury in the West intersect with existing patterns of socioeconomic hardship (Fig. 7), with even greater shares in California, Colorado, Florida, and Washington (Supplementary Table 2). This reveals a growing disconnect between the historical perception of fire as a western hazard primarily affecting affluent communities and its evolving geography, in which impacts are spreading nationwide and cutting across the socioeconomic spectrum. 

Yet a deeper challenge remains. Decades of land-use policy, housing inequality, limited access to resilience mechanisms like disaster aid and insurance, and infrastructure neglect have entrenched both exposure and social vulnerability. These pressures converge to produce systemic risk, wherein overlapping burdens in housing, income, and language not only condition the pace and effectiveness of recovery but also limit the capacity to mitigate and prepare for fire. Because adaptation unfolds slowly, present-day inequalities and housing conditions create path dependence that will shape fire outcomes for decades. Reducing future losses therefore entails proactive risk communication, targeted mitigation, and sustained investment in local adaptive capacity before these vulnerabilities further magnify losses and deepen inequality<sup>60</sup> . 

Financial systems are central to this dynamic. Insurance, in particular, plays a decisive role in shaping who can withstand and recover from disaster, and remains a cornerstone of that safety net<sup>61</sup> . Yet in high-risk areas of California and Colorado, coverage has already been withdrawn or restricted following risk reassessment, leaving residents more vulnerable just as their need for assistance grows. Sustaining fireadapted communities in the face of rising risk will depend on policy innovations that keep insurance markets both solvent and inclusive. 

Living with fire demands multi-level strategies that reflect the structural intricacy of the risk. Local efforts, such as defensible space, home hardening, and Community Wildfire Protection Plans, are essential but insufficient on their own<sup>62</sup> . They must be integrated with broader interventions, including land-use reform, climate-informed codes, fuel management, and investments in early warning systems and critical infrastructure<sup>63</sup> . Equally important are risk monitoring, public engagement, and planning for insurability<sup>64</sup> . Given these complexities, strategies must also guard against scenarios where fragmented or short-sighted actions weaken collective resilience. With the

## Methods 

In this study, we estimate spatial and temporal trends in fire risk to structures across the contiguous United States (CONUS). Fire risk is defined following the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) framework as the expected loss arising from the interaction between fire hazard, exposure, and vulnerability<sup>65</sup> . To operationalize this definition, we first estimated spatiotemporal trends in fire hazard by analyzing historical and projected burned area across ecoregions. Second, we quantified changes in development patterns to characterize exposure, measured as the number of structures present across landscapes through time. Third, we refined the conventional fire-risk formulation by incorporating an empirical measure of structural vulnerability that accounts for heterogeneity in fire characteristics and settlement patterns. Finally, we examined projected fire risk under the lens of social vulnerability to assess how biophysical hazard and structural exposure and vulnerability intersect with socioeconomic conditions across communities. All analyses and figures were produced with R 4.3.1<sup>66</sup> , using the packages biscale 1.0.0<sup>67</sup> , classInt 0.4-9<sup>68</sup> , clipr 0.8.0<sup>69</sup> , cowplot 1.1.1<sup>70</sup> , doParallel 1.0.16<sup>71</sup> , exactextractr 0.9.1<sup>72</sup> , fasterize 1.0.4<sup>73</sup> , foreach 1.5.1<sup>74</sup> , ggpattern 1.1.4<sup>75</sup> , ggplot2 3.5.1<sup>76</sup> , ggpubr 0.6.0<sup>77</sup> , ggtext 0.1.2<sup>78</sup> , ggthemes 4.2.4<sup>79</sup> , Hmisc 5.1-1<sup>80</sup> , mgcv 1.9-1<sup>81</sup> , modEvA 3.17<sup>82</sup> , pacman 0.5.1<sup>83</sup> , png 0.1-8<sup>84</sup> , pryr 0.1.6<sup>85</sup> , purrr 1.0.2<sup>86</sup> , ragg 1.3.2<sup>87</sup> , raster 3.6-23<sup>88</sup> , scales 1.3.0<sup>89</sup> , scico 1.5.0<sup>90</sup> , sf 1.0-14, stringr 1.5.0<sup>91</sup> , terra 1.7-39<sup>92</sup> , tidyverse 2.0.0<sup>93</sup> , viridis 0.6.4<sup>94</sup> , and their corresponding dependencies. 

### Spatiotemporal trends in fire hazard 

We employed data on area burned from the Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity<sup>44</sup> and Stephen et al.’s model<sup>19</sup> to document (1985-2023) and project (2023-2060) temporal trends in area burned at the EPA Level IV ecoregion scale. The modeling framework, initially developed by Joseph et al.<sup>18</sup> , integrates a 30-year fire record with meteorological/ climatological and population data to construct spatiotemporal models incorporating spatially varying nonlinear effects using Bayesian finite maxima. Model training utilized data from 1984 to 2009, withholding data from 2010 to 2016 for validation. A Hamiltonian Monte Carlo method, implemented in the probabilistic programming language Stan, was used for parameter estimation. Four chains of 1000 iterations each were executed, with the first 500 iterations discarded as a warm-up phase. For this study, we derived weather/climate ensemble results from three General Circulation Models (GCMs) under the RCP 4.5 emissions scenario, namely, Centre Régional de Recherches Météorologiques Model (CNRM), Hadley Centre Global Environmental Model—Earth System version (HadGEM-ES), and Meteorological Research Institute Model (MRI). 

### Characterization of the built environment 

Changes in settlement patterns are typically mapped using satellite imagery or other spatial data offering insights into land use and the built environment<sup>95,96</sup> . However, these data sources present certain spatial and temporal characteristics that constrain their effectiveness in risk assessments. Extensive, high-resolution remotely sensed data, for example, have only been available since the advent of satellite technology in the 1970s, precluding long-term historical analyses. Additionally, these data sets often employ coarse classifications of development intensity and offer less reliability in rural regions<sup>42</sup> . The limitations of remotely-sensed products in settlement analysis include resolution constraints, spectral and temporal limitations, data integration challenges, misclassification issues, and reliance on ancillary data for validation<sup>97</sup> . 

To overcome these limitations, we relied on property-record data representing the historical built environment. Specifically, we employed the Historical Settlement Data Compilation for the US (HISDAC-US<sup>26,42,43</sup> ). HISDAC-US provides a gridded time series of settlement layers derived from the Zillow Transaction and Assessment Data set (ZTRAX), which includes over 370 million records on housing and land-parcel attributes. 

From this dataset, we extracted Built-Up Property Records (BUPR) gridded surfaces, which contain residential, commercial, industrial, non-profit, and agricultural buildings or building units within 250 × 250 m grid cells across the conterminous United States from 1810 to 2020. BUPR constitutes an empirical record of historical settlement patterns derived from property assessment data. To refine our dataset, we integrated information from the U.S. Geological Survey’s Protected Areas Database (PAD-US 4.0) and excluded structures located on federal, tribal, state, or local public lands. The resulting dataset represents privately owned structures located on private land, including residential properties, as well as commercial and industrial buildings. We focused on privately owned structures for two primary reasons. First, the HISDAC-US database is derived from property assessment records, which are most complete and consistently reported for privately owned parcels, whereas structures on public lands (e.g., administrative facilities, recreation infrastructure, utility buildings) are often inconsistently represented or absent from parcel-based tax assessment systems. Second, the majority of wildfire-related structural losses recorded in national incident reporting datasets (e.g., ICS-209-Plus) occur within privately owned residential and mixed-use developments, which constitute the dominant component of the built environment at risk from wildland–urban interface fires. This approach may omit a small number of publicly owned structures affected by wildfire (e.g., National and State Park facilities), but these structures represent a minor fraction of the national structure inventory and do not substantially influence large-scale estimates of wildfire risk to the built environment.

### Model validation 

To ensure model robustness, we validated the GAMs by comparing the predicted number of structures per Level IV ecoregion to an independent 30% holdout set excluded from training. Model performance was evaluated based on the percentage of deviance explained between observed and predicted values. This approach enabled us to test the models’ ability to capture temporal trends in settlement patterns across diverse ecological regions.

### Sensitivity analysis of the exposure-adjusted structure loss index and risk components 

To evaluate the robustness of the Exposure-Adjusted Structure Loss Index (EASLI) and to quantify the relative contributions of hazard, exposure, and structural vulnerability to fire risk, we implemented a multi-step sensitivity and decomposition framework combining bootstrap uncertainty analysis with variance and temporal decomposition methods. 

### Bootstrap-based uncertainty in EASLI 

The sensitivity of in EASLI to sampling variability in the historical record was quantified using nonparametric bootstrap resampling<sup>103</sup> . Annual observations of burned-area proportion, structure counts, and observed structural losses were resampled with replacement (B = 2000) within each EPA Level III ecoregion. For each bootstrap replicate, EASLI was recomputed, preserving the joint dependence structure among hazard, exposure, and loss. For each ecoregion, the bootstrap distribution of EASLI pd was summarized using the mean, median, standard deviation, and percentile-based 95% confidence intervals. Multiplicative uncertainty was characterized using the geometric standard deviation in log space, reflective of the strictly positive, right-skewed ratios commonly encountered in the analysis. 

Bootstrap realizations were additionally used to evaluate the robustness of regional differences in EASLI and the stability of inferred risk patterns. For each replicate, ecoregions were ranked according to estimated risk while holding hazard and exposure constant, such that variation in rank reflects uncertainty in EASLI alone. The resulting rank distributions were used to estimate the probability that a given ecoregion consistently falls within the highest 20%, middle 60%, or lowest 20% of risk values. These probabilities quantify the confidence with which ecoregions can be assigned to relative vulnerability tiers, distinguishing regions with persistently high or low exposure-adjusted losses from those whose rankings are sensitive to sampling variability. 

Observed structural losses from the ICS-209-Plus dataset were used as an independent benchmark to assess whether ecoregions identified as high-vulnerability under the model correspond to historically high-damage regions. This comparison informs the empirical validity of the EASLI metric as a proxy for realized structural loss patterns.

### Socioeconomic vulnerability and fire risk 

We used version 2 of the Climate and Economic Justice Screening Tool<sup>105</sup> to obtain socioeconomic data, focusing on the percentage of households below 200% of the federal poverty line and housing cost burden across all census tracts in the CONUS. For each tract, we estimated both current and future fire risk by calculating the area-weighted mean fire risk, using the overlap between EPA Level IV ecoregions and census tracts as weights.
