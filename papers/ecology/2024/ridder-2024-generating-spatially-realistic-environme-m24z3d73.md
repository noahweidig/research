# Generating spatially realistic environmental null models with the shift-&-rotate approach helps evaluate false positives in species distribution modelling

## Abstract

To circumvent reporting spurious correlations, species distribution models often explicitly account for spatial autocorrelation, for example by including spatially structured random effects. The validity of statistical inference derived from such models has been tested by simulations using null environmental predictors that do not have any causal dependency with the response. Such null environmental predictors can be obtained by permutations of the original predictors or by simulating spatial structures resembling the original predictors. In such approaches, it is important that the permuted or simulated predictors reflect the nature of spatial variation present in the original predictors. Here we present a novel approach for generating realistic null predictors by a shift-&-rotate (S&R) approach: we extract environmental variables after randomly translating and rotating the sampling area within a window of defined environmental layers. In this way, the null environmental variables have fully realistic spatial variation and covariation, but no relationship to the response variable. We implement the S&R approach to three main R-functions and demonstrate with a simulation study how they can be used to untangle causal versus non-causal relationships within species distribution modelling. These methods allow us to quantify the predictive power attributed within the models due to non-causal correlations generated by the realistic structure of the environmental covariates. In our case study, we identify when a model incorrectly estimates parameter values, yet still has high predictive power due to the structured nature of the predictor variables. The use of null models is imperative in ecological modelling for testing the accuracy of statistical inference in complex ecological systems and the choice of these null models is far from trivial. Here we provide R functions for generating spatially realistic null models to use in species distribution modelling as well as other spatially explicit fields such as landscape genetics.

#### **1** | **INTRODUCTION** 

Unravelling spatially structured ecological patterns is a task that has engaged geographical ecologists and macroecologists for decades. Yet, despite impressive advancements in statistics and modelling, scientists still debate over how to detect and account for biases that arise due to the effect of space (Beale et al., 2007; Diniz- Filho et al., 2003; Hodges & Reich, 2010; Lennon, 2000). The distribution of species in space is driven by biological and environmental processes that generate spatial structures (spatial dependence). Yet, when we attempt to measure these structures across scales using species distribution models (SDM), we often find spurious associations when response and explanatory variables show spatial autocorrelation (Diniz- Filho et al., 2003; Kéry & Royle, 2016). 

Spatial autocorrelation (SAC) is a phenomenon in which the values of samples closer in space tend to be more similar than those farther away in space (Tobler, 1970). This biases model results because individual data points cannot be considered independent (Legendre & Legendre, 1998). Therefore, an apparent association between a response and an explanatory variable that is supported by a given number of data points may, in fact, be supported by a much smaller effective number of data points (Dale & Fortin, 2002; Legendre & Legendre, 1998). Statistical tests failing to account for such pseudoreplication in the dataset will tend to produce an excess of false positive associations (Dale & Fortin, 2002; Guélat & Kéry, 2018; Kéry & Royle, 2016; Mielke et al., 2020). This can be particularly prominent in environmental variables with broad scale spatial structures such as temperature, which often show inflated importance in literature unless properly accounted for (see the red shift problem; Lennon, 2000). Both the severity of such issues and the methods to control for them is still a topic of debate (Beale et al., 2007; DinizFilho et al., 2003; Hodges & Reich, 2010; Lennon, 2000; Roberts et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2023). 

There are several widely used strategies for accounting for SAC in species distribution modelling. Depending on the model type one can include autocovariates (e.g. autologistic models) or spatially correlated random effects (Dormann et al., 2007; Hughes & Haran, 2013; Ver Hoef et al., 2018). Both assume that autocorrelation among the residuals decreases with distance and attempt to correct for this within the modelling algorithm (Dormann et al., 2007; Hughes & Haran, 2013; Ver Hoef et al., 2018). Another popular method is filtering or thinning species occurrence points which is often not ideal and has been shown to not improve model performance (Aiello- Lammens et al., 2015; Ten Caten & Dallas, 2023). Unfortunately, many of the methods attempting to account for SAC can introduce additional biases in estimating covariate effects (Paciorek, 2010; Reich et al., 2006). Moreover, it can be unclear if the 

strategies efficiently remove bias due to SAC and succeed in avoiding false positive associations (Hodges & Reich, 2010). 

Null models play a crucial role in species niche modelling by providing a baseline to untangle the effects of SAC and to assess the significance of observed patterns. Two main types of null models exist for both species- habitat association models and species distribution models. The first randomizes the response variables (i.e. the occurrence or abundance data) and the second randomizes the predictor variables (i.e. habitat or environmental variables). 

In the first case, null models have been generated using point pattern processes (Gamma test; Plotkin et al., 2000), pattern reconstruction (Tscheschel & Stoyan, 2006), unconstrained random pixel samples (Bohl et al., 2019; Raes & ter Steege, 2007), as well as constrained random pixel samples (Bohl et al., 2019; Kass et al., 2021; Soley- Guardia et al., 2024). The second class of null models randomizes the predictor variables. Several methods for generating these exist, such as conditional autoregressive simulations (Cressie, 1991), restricted randomization tests (Fortin & Jacquez, 2000), Torustranslation (Upton & Fingleton, 1985) and Moran spectral randomization (MSR; Wagner & Dray, 2015). We find the latter to be the most useful of the mentioned approaches but find that it still requires the user to specify the level of similar spatial structure, or independence with the original variables. The Torus- translation method is also unsuitable in many cases because it requires the study site to be a rectangular grid (Upton & Fingleton, 1985) and can generate unrealistic spatial structures (see Section 4). 

Here we propose a new solution for generating null distributions of environmental predictors that have spatially realistic patterns without any a priori assumptions about spatial structure or independence. It uses real environmental variables sampled from new locations that respect the relative positioning of the original samples. We achieve this by randomly translating and rotating the map of sampling points, extracting values of the environmental layers at those points, and using them in place of traditional null covariates. Thus, the resulting null models contain the realistic spatial structure of the specific environmental variable and yet have no causal relationship to the response variable. We call this the shift- &- rotate approach (abbreviated S&R) for generating spatially realistic null models to help detect biases related to SAC in species distribution modelling and other fields of spatial analysis. It can be applied if predictor variables are defined over an area larger than the actual sampling area. It fulfils the same objective as the torus- translation test (Harms et al., 2001; Upton & Fingleton, 1985) but does not require sampling points to be regularly placed on a rectangular grid. Additionally, it can be applied to any kind of predictor variables (e.g. categorical or continuous) and does not require making any assumptions of their distributional nature, giving a benefit over simulationbased approaches.

We demonstrate how the S&R approach can be used to unravel causal versus non- causal relationships in species distribution modelling. First, we simulate community data by using different combinations of real environmental covariates as predictors. We then fit regression models to these datasets using the framework of Hierarchical Modelling of Species Communities (HMSC; Ovaskainen & Abrego, 2020) with three types of environmental predictors: true environmental predictors, permuted environmental predictors (traditional null models) and environmental predictors permuted via the S&R approach (Figure 1). This enables us to compare models where the relationship between the predictor and the response variables is broken and spatial dependency in predictor values is either also broken or still intact. Our results show that even models that include spatial random effects may overestimate the importance of spatially structured predictors, and that the rate of such false positives can be controlled by comparison to spatially realistic environmental null models generated by the S&R approach. 

#### 2.1.1 | The 100 study sites 

Motivated by the potential of the RAINBIO database on vascular plants (Dauby et al., 2016) to help understand species- environment relationships in African tropical forests, we based our simulation study on an intensively sampled grid from western Central Africa. Cells of 0.5 × 0.5 degrees were selected as grid cells from 8° E to 15° E longitude and 6° S to 6° N latitude. The resulting grid consisted of 336 cells which was further reduced to exclude the ocean, leaving 270 grid cells for which environmental predictors will be assigned. 

To generate replicate landscapes that operate as spatially realistic null datasets, we generated 99 additional study sites by shifting and rotating the original study area on the globe via our S&R functions (see Data Availability Statement). Thus, for each of these 99 additional study sites, we selected a random location from the globe and placed the rotated sampling grid at this location, with a rotation angle following a uniform distribution from 0 to 360 degrees. Only locations and rotations where the entire sampling grid with 270 grid cells was located in land were accepted.

#### 2.1.4 | The 400 original datasets and the true values that generated them 

The above procedure generates 400 datasets (communities of 100 species generated for each of the 100 landscapes and for each of the four scenarios), which we code as DX.E1, DX.E6, DX.E1.S and DX.E6.S. Here _X_ = 1, …, 100 indicates the study site, E1 and E6 denote whether the occurrence data were generated with one or six environmental drivers, and S indicates that the occurrence data was generated with a spatial random effect included. Each dataset contains the species occurrence data (matrix of 270 × 100 presence absences), the environmental data (matrix of 270 × 6 environmental predictors, some of which may be irrelevant for a specific dataset), spatial coordinates (270 _x_ - and _y_ - coordinates), which would be known to the data analyst. Additionally, each dataset contains the true values of the beta parameter (matrix of 100 × 7 parameters, some of which may be zeros) that were used to generate the data, and that we will use to evaluate the correctness of the inferred results. We note that the beta matrix has one more parameter than is the number of environmental predictors because it also contains the intercept, however we do not compare the estimates to true values for the intercept because it is typically not of ecological interest. 

#### **2.2** | **Generating null datasets that break the relationship between predictors and response** 

We created two kinds of null datasets that are otherwise similar to the original datasets, but where the relationship between the environmental predictors and the species data has been broken. One was based on permutation of the original environmental variables, the other based on extracted external environmental variables using the S&R approach. 

#### 2.2.1 | Permuted environmental variables 

For each of the 100 landscapes, we randomized the environmental covariates by randomly permuting the 270 grid cells and applying this permutation jointly for all six environmental covariates but not for spatial coordinates or for the community data. We applied the same set of permutations to all four scenarios of each study site _X_ , and denote the 400 permuted datasets (one per study site) by DX.E1.P, DX.E6.P, DX.E1.S.P and DX.E6.S.P, with _X_ = 1, …, 100, and P denoting permutation within study site. This permutation approach breaks not only the relationship between the predictors and the response, but also the spatial dependency in predictor values among the grid cells. Thus, we do not recommend the use of this approach,

#### **2.3** | **Statistical analyses of the data** 

The above- described procedure generates in total 1200 datasets, of which 400 are original, for 400 the environmental predictors are permuted within study site, and for 400 the environmental predictors are generated using the S&R approach. The 400 original datasets are made of 100 replicates (=study sites) combined with four scenarios to generate data (assuming one or six environmental drivers, and including or not a spatial random effect). We analysed each of these datasets with four model variants, thus resulting in a total of 4800 models fitted to the data. 

#### 2.3.1 | Model definition and fitting 

As analysis tool, we used the joint species distribution framework of Hierarchical Modelling of Species Communities (HMSC; Ovaskainen & Abrego, 2020). The four variants of the statistical models that we fitted correspond to the four data generating scenarios. We denote these models by M.E6, M.E6.S, M.E1 and M.E1.S, showing whether we included one or six covariates (E1 vs. E6) and whether a spatial random effect was included (indicated by S). Model fitting was based on Bayesian inference, where we applied the default prior distributions (Ovaskainen & Abrego, 2020) and utilized the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling scheme of Tikhonov et al. (2020). We fitted each model with four MCMC chains that were 375 iterations long, out of which we excluded the first 125 iterations as transient, thus resulting in 1000 posterior samples over the four chains. We evaluated MCMC convergence by the distribution of potential scale reduction factors over the estimated _훽_ parameters ( _̂훽kj_ ) and determined that convergence was met. We note that the number of MCMC iterations is smaller than typical applications of HMSC, but that it was necessary in the current study due to high computational load generated by the high number of models that we fitted (4800 models fitted in four different ways, four crossvalidation procedures see below). 

#### 2.3.2 | Evaluating model performance 

We evaluated the performance of the models in terms of prediction and inference. In the S&R null models, the AUC of non- spatial models (M.E1 and M.E6) performed much better than by random in explanatory power and random cross- validation (Figure 5c,f). #### **4** | **DISCUSSION** 

The S&R approach allows us to unlink the relationship between the predictor and the response variables while maintaining realistic spatial dependency in predictor values. This approach allowed us to illustrate how statistical models that do not account for spatial autocorrelation can lead to false conclusions due to an excess of false positives, even if their predictive performance is tested with appropriate cross- validation procedures. In other words, high species- environment associations can be obtained by chance when the environmental predictors are structured in space. Integrating a spatial random effect in models improves the situation (Figure 6c) but the false positive rates remain still higher than nominally expected. We want to stress that including a spatial random effect to account for SAC may not be enough to prevent high rates of false positives. By using spatially realistic null models we are able to pinpoint the effects that the spatial structure in the environmental 

variables plays within the models' predictive abilities, separate this effect from actual causal relationships and assess the models with measures of significance. 

#### **4.1** | **Statistical significance testing with S&R null models** 

The S&R null model provides distributions of _̂훽_ values (for single species) or AUC values (for all species) resulting from non- causal but realistic (spatially structured) environmental predictors. Therefore, the statistical significance of observed estimates can be assess using one- tailed (for AUC, Figure 4b) or two- tailed (for _̂훽_ , Figure 3) nonparametric tests based on S&R null models. 

For example, for AUC, our null hypothesis (HO) is that the observed AUC value for a given dataset is not significantly greater than the distribution of AUC values generated by the S&R null model applied to this dataset (AUCS&R). The alternate hypothesis is that the observed AUC > AUCS&R, meaning that the predictor variable(s) better explain the occurrence data than expected by chance for realistic environmental covariates. The proportion of AUCS&R values ≥ observed AUC provides an estimate of the _p_ - value associated to the HO. The upper 0.95 quantile of AUCS&R provides a threshold to define when the AUC is considered significant for a nominal type- I error rate (i.e. false positives) _α_ = 0.05 (Figure 4b). For _̂훽_ , the 0.025 and 0.975 quantiles under the S&R null model provide equivalent thresholds (Figure 3). Note that for _̂훽_ , it is important to standardize the covariates (i.e. mean = 0, variance = 1) prior to data analysis, otherwise _̂훽_ would depend on the variances of covariates and not only on their spatial patterns. We note that in addition to the approaches outlined in Figures 3 and 4, there are many other alternatives on how exactly the null model can be used as a baseline of comparison. For example, one could compare the posterior probability by which a given _훽_ parameter is estimated to be positive (or negative) to the null distribution of such posterior probabilities. 

The S&R null model can be applied to many statistical models using covariates defined continuously in space, such as the WorldClim database, and providing scale- independent summary statistics describing model fit with respect to single covariates (e.g. _훽_ ) or to all of them (e.g. AUC, but also _R_<sup>2</sup> , …). The aim is to compare the value of these summary statistics with their distribution obtained under the S&R null models to assess if the explanatory power of the covariates could be obtained by chance. 

To help users apply the S&R approach, we have developed several R functions that differ by the nature of the spatial coordinates used: degrees latitude and longitude on the globe or _X_ - _Y_ Cartesian coordinates on a plane (see Supporting Information). Providing a set of spatial coordinates, for example species occurrences points and pseudo- absences ones, these S&R functions will generate a set of new coordinates preserving the spatial distances between points, after randomly shifting, rotating and optionally reversing the set of points. Given this new set of coordinates, the user can extract the environmental covariates and then apply the same statistical

#### **4.2** | **Comparison with alternative methods** 

As mentioned in the introduction, several methods have been developed for generating spatial null models. It is possible to generate null models by permuting samples of the original predictor variables as we illustrated (Mrkvička et al., 2021), or using various types of spatially constrained randomization procedures (Bauman et al., 2019; Beale et al., 2007; Hanks et al., 2015; Kim, 2021; Mäkinen et al., 2022). For example, the commonly used torus translation works by shifting the environmental layers in all cardinal directions within a 2- dimensional torus, which means that when the layers shift beyond the edge of the plot, they reappear on the opposite edge. This method can only be used in rectangular study sites and only when there are no strong environmental gradients (Harms et al., 2001; Upton & Fingleton, 1985). Due to these reasons, it is not suitable for many studies (including our case study which falls partially in the ocean). Furthermore, this test creates very distinct “edges” in the environmental layers which we believe do not accurately reflect true spatial structure. 

Moran Spectral Randomizations (MSR) can also simulate spatially structured environmental data that is to some degree “independent” from the species abundance data (Wagner & Dray, 2015). This method re- creates artificial variables using information on the spatial patterns of the predictor variables and uses these in a conditional randomization procedure that preserves the spatial structure (Wagner & Dray, 2015). This method requires less parameterization than other methods and can be applied to irregularly shaped study sites. However, it still requires the users to deal with the trade- off between similar spatial structure (‘singleton’ procedure), or independence with the original variables (‘pair’ procedure), and an adequate choice of spatial weighting matrices, which can affect the trade- off between power and pattern detection accuracy (Bauman et al., 2018). Simulations show that the power of MSR depends on the environmental context (coarse vs. fine grained spatial patterns of predictors), the sampling effort and the spatial distribution of samples (Bauman et al., 2019). While this method is promising, there is still uncertainty regarding how well it accurately reflects the true spatial structure of the environmental variables. 

A comparison of the performances of alternative methods on simulated data is beyond the scope of the present article and merits further investigation. However, we believe that using S&R null models has an advantage over all previous approaches because it requires no assumptions by the user about the degree of spatial structure or independence. Nevertheless, a possible limitation is when the structure of environmental data in the study area is very distinctive so 

that the S&R null models do not provide realistic structures with respect to the study area. To control this risk, our S&R functions can impose constrains on the ranges of latitude- longitude or _X_ - _Y_ coordinates within which the set of points must remain. This option can be useful to avoid areas where covariates are not defined (e.g. oceans) or to ensure that covariates are extracted from landscapes similar to the original data points. We recommend, however, that the size of the area delimited remains at least two or three times larger than the maximal distance between data points to ensure enough degrees of freedom for shifting and rotating the set of points. The S&R function for _X_ - _Y_ coordinates on a rectangular area has an option to treat space as a torus by considering opposing edges as adjacent. Although we do not recommend applying this option in general, it can be useful if environmental covariates are defined continuously on a rectangular area not much larger than the set of points. 

#### **5** | **CONCLUSIONS** 

SDMs are often created to optimize their predictive power and they work very well for this, but issues can arise when researchers attempt to identify the relevant predictors for their model without proper methods. For this reason, we believe it is necessary to assess SDMs with appropriate and realistic null models (Bohl et al., 2019; Kass et al., 2021; Soley- Guardia et al., 2024). We can predict a species occurrence across a spatial domain, but if we wish to have accurate predictive models across time or beyond a study area, we need to understand the predictors in the models that we make. We believe that S&R is a simple and valuable tool to aid us in this endeavour.
