# Microclimate and forest density drive plant population dynamics under climate change

## Abstract

Macroclimatic changes are impacting ecosystems worldwide. However, a large portion of terrestrial species live under conditions where impacts of macroclimate change are buffered, such as in the shade of trees, and how this buffering impacts future below-canopy biodiversity redistributions at the continental scale is unknown. Here we show that shady forest floors due to dense tree canopies mitigate severe warming impacts on forest biodiversity, while canopy opening amplifies macroclimate change impacts. A cross-continental transplant experiment in five contrasting biogeographical areas combined with experimental heating and irradiation treatments was used to parametize 25-m resolution mechanistic demographic distribution models and project the current and future distributions of 12 common understorey plant species, considering the effects of forest microclimate and forest cover density. These results highlight microclimates and forest density as powerful tools for forest managers and policymakers to shelter forest biodiversity from climate change.

- 172 <mark>Integrating the effects of the local and regional environmental drivers on all vital rates that</mark> 

- 173 <mark>contribute to the generative population growth allowed us to project the population growth rate</mark>

- 175 <mark>mechanistically-informed distribution maps at 25 m resolution for the extent of temperate</mark> 

- 176 <mark>Europe. As expected from the vital rate models (Fig. 2), the DDMs showed substantial variation</mark> 

- 177 <mark>in λ, both at the continental-level (area of 28 million km²) and at the landscape-level extent (three</mark> 

- 178 <mark>areas of 4 km²), indicating the importance of fine-grained microclimatic conditions (i.e. summer</mark>

- 179 <mark>and winter temperature offsets), and forest cover density, next to the coarse-grained</mark> 

- 180 <mark>macroclimatic conditions (i.e. growing-season temperature and precipitation) in driving forest</mark> 181 <mark>plant population dynamics. While evidently the variation in  λ at the landscape-level represents</mark> 

- 182 <mark>only a subtle part of the variation at the continental extent, these results highlight the</mark>

- 186 <mark>Considerable changes were predicted in the spatial distribution for all understorey species due to</mark> 187 <mark>macroclimate change (Fig. 4; Extended Data Figs.10,11). - 193 _<mark>Anemone nemorosa</mark>_ <mark>, a 50% reduction of the forest cover density would likely amplify the</mark> 194 <mark>negative impact of macroclimate change by not less than 195%. In contrast, in the generalists</mark> 

195 _<mark>Alliaria petiolata</mark>_ <mark>and</mark> _<mark>Urtica dioica</mark>_ <mark>, beneficial effects brought about by canopy opening could</mark>

- 198 <mark>negatively impacted by canopy opening due to higher light levels that hamper survival, likely as</mark>

- 223 **<mark>Challenges in demographic distribution modelling</mark>** 

10 

- 224 <mark>While the mechanistic nature of DDMs brought advantages over more correlative approaches</mark> 

- 225 <mark>such as species distribution modelling, it also has methodological limitations that should be</mark> 

- 226 <mark>considered when interpreting the results. First, DDMs require us to make assumptions on the</mark> 

- 227 <mark>structure of the vital rate models. While more complex vital models are likely to better describe</mark> 

- 228 <mark>the natural complexity of the system (e.g. by including higher order polynomials and interactions</mark> 

- 229 <mark>terms), the robust parameterization of these models typically trades off with the number of</mark> 

- 230 <mark>observations in the data set. For this reason, we here only considered monotonous relationships</mark> 

- 231 <mark>of the vital rates to the environment, while a qualitative sensitivity analyses against this approach</mark> 

- 232 <mark>suggested that some vital rate models are likely to follow a more complex response curve</mark> 233 <mark>(Extended Data Fig. 7). For instance, the vital rates survival and flowering probability in</mark> _<mark>Allium</mark>_ 

- 234 _<mark>ursinum</mark>_ <mark>more likely followed a unimodal response to the winter temperature offset gradient</mark> 

- 235 <mark>which is not captured by the assumed monotonous response curve in our models. Second, DDMs</mark> 

- 236 <mark>are data hungry models. Ideally, demographic data should be collected in the critical macro- and</mark> 

- 237 <mark>microenvironmental conditions within the study area. This is, however, often impossible due to a</mark> 

- 238 <mark>limitation of time and resources. Given that our work aimed to assess fine-grained variation in λ,</mark> 

- 239 <mark>we put maximal effort the collect demographic data in the critical microclimatic conditions,</mark> 

- 240 <mark>while the range of sampled macroclimatic conditions within the study area was still not fully</mark> 

- 241 <mark>covered (Extended Data Fig. 14). Model extrapolation is inherent to predictive ecology, and the</mark> 

- 242 <mark>accuracy of the presented DDM predictions (far) beyond the macroclimatic range that was</mark> 

- 243 <mark>sampled (in our case, e.g. at higher elevations in the Alps) is evidently lower. A well-designed</mark> 

- 244 <mark>plot network which</mark> _<mark>a priori</mark>_ <mark>considers the goal of the study, is thus key in the efficient and</mark> 

- 245 <mark>successful application of DDMs.</mark>

### 247 **<mark>Conclusions</mark>** 

- 248 <mark>In sum, we here built demographic distribution models based on mechanistic plant responses to</mark> 249 <mark>both microclimate and macroclimate. We found that fine-grained microclimatic conditions and</mark> 

- 250 <mark>forest density, in addition to the macroclimatic conditions, play a fundamental role in</mark> 

- 251 <mark>understorey species’ distributions. Our results suggest that changes in forest-floor conditions,</mark> 

- 252 <mark>regardless of whether the drivers of canopy opening are natural or anthropogenic, can amplify</mark> 

- 253 <mark>macroclimate change impacts on forest biodiversity. We propose that sustainable forest</mark> 

- 254 <mark>management should conserve conditions that favour the survival of forest specialist species</mark> 

- 255 <mark>through maintaining a high canopy cover to limit both abrupt light stress and temperature</mark> 

- 256 <mark>increase. The DDMs suggest that macroclimate change impacts on forest specialist species (e.g.</mark> 

- 257 <mark>emblematic species such as</mark> _<mark>Anemone nemorosa</mark>_ <mark>and</mark> _<mark>Allium ursinum</mark>_ <mark>) could be mitigated by</mark> 

- 258 <mark>increased forest density. Forest generalists whose ranges often promptly respond to changes in</mark> 

- 259 <mark>the macroclimate system, are likely to keep on taking advantage from the natural variation in</mark> 

- 260 <mark>microclimate and light availability after disturbance that is inherent to European broadleaved</mark> 261 <mark>forest dynamics.</mark> 

- 262 <mark>Our integrative methodological framework (R-code for the analyses of the DDMs available as</mark> 

- 263 <mark>Supplementary Material) is adaptable to many other species and ecosystems to examine the</mark> 

- 264 <mark>importance of fine-grained environmental conditions in a warming world, and can undoubtedly</mark> 

- 265 <mark>improve our understanding of species range dynamics under 21</mark><sup>st</sup> <mark>century climate change.</mark>

- 558 6.1. <mark>Vital rate models</mark> 

- 559 <mark>Individual-level measurements of six vital rates describing key transitions in the plant’s</mark> 

- 560 <mark>generative life cycle were regressed against experimental site-level environmental data (5.1) and</mark> 

- 561 <mark>plant size (for implementation in the IPMs) (Fig. S2, S4). - 567 <mark>The vital rates were analysed with mixed-effect models in the R package</mark> _<mark>lme4</mark>_<sup>64</sup> <mark>for each species</mark> 568 <mark>separately. Prior to modelling, the state variable ‘plant size’ was checked for normality based on</mark> 

- 569 <mark>visual inspection and transformed (</mark> _<mark>ln</mark>_ <mark>or</mark> _<mark>sqrt</mark>_ <mark>) when appropriate (Supplementary Table 1,</mark> 570 <mark>Extended Data Fig. 5). To account for the nested experimental design, ‘mesocosm ID’ (9 levels)</mark> 571 <mark>was included as random intercept in all models of vital rates that were measured at the level of</mark> 572 <mark>the individual. As a sensitivity analysis against this approach, we also tested an alternative</mark> 

- 573 <mark>random effect term accounting for the fully nested study design: ‘mesocosm ID’ nested within</mark> 574 <mark>‘experimental site’ nested within ‘forest type’ nested within ‘region’. This, however, did not</mark> 

575 <mark>changed the general trends in parameter estimates of the fixed effects (Extended Data Fig. 6).</mark> 

- 576 <mark>We therefore opted to keep the simpler random structure in all models. To account for</mark> 

577 <mark>environmental stochasticity across the study period, ‘sampling year’ was included as an</mark> 

- 578 <mark>additional random intercept in all vital rates models that were measured for more than one annual</mark> 

- 579 <mark>transition. Growth rate and the seedling size were regressed with linear mixed models (LMMs).</mark> 

27 

- 580 <mark>Survival and flowering probability (binary distributed) were regressed with generalized linear</mark> 

581 <mark>mixed-effect models (GLMMs) and a binomial error distribution. The number of reproductive</mark> 582 <mark>structures and the number of seeds were regressed with GLMMs and a Poisson error distribution.</mark> 

583 <mark>The vital rate models included plant size (when relevant) and all environmental variables as fixed</mark> 

584 <mark>effect terms. Pairwise spearman correlations (r) among the environmental variables were</mark> 585 <mark>acceptable (median |r| = 0.17; maximum |r| = 0.62)</mark><sup>65</sup> <mark>. The seedling size models of</mark> _<mark>Alliaria</mark>_ 586 _<mark>petiolata</mark>_ <mark>and</mark> _<mark>Allium ursinum</mark>_ <mark>and the seed number models did not include the macroclimate</mark> 587 <mark>predictors to avoid over-extrapolation because these vital rates were only measured in the sites of</mark> 588 <mark>Belgium and Sweden</mark><sup>66</sup> <mark>. The optimal level of model complexity was evaluated based on the</mark> 589 <mark>lowest Akaike information criterion with small-sample correction (AICc) by comparing all</mark> 590 <mark>possible nested models with reduced complexity using the R package</mark> _<mark>MuMln</mark>_<sup>67</sup> <mark>. Model fit of the selected models was assessed</mark> 594 <mark>as the percentage of variance explained by the fixed effects (marginal R</mark><sup>2</sup> <mark>; R</mark><sup>2</sup> m <mark>), and the</mark> 595 <mark>percentage of variance explained by both fixed and random effects (conditional R</mark><sup>2</sup> <mark>; R</mark><sup>2</sup> c <mark>)</mark> 596 <mark>following Nakagawa & Schielzeth (2013)</mark><sup>68</sup> <mark>. We only tested linear responses of the vital rates to</mark> 597 <mark>the environmental gradients and plant size. ### 6.2. <mark>Integral Projection Models (IPMs)</mark> 

610 <mark>The effects of the environment and experimental treatments on population growth rates (λ) were</mark> 

611 <mark>investigated by combining the vital rate models (as built in section 6.1) into Integral Projection</mark> 612 <mark>Models (IPMs</mark><sup>42</sup> <mark>). IPMs are analogous to matrix population models that allow the integration of</mark> 

613 <mark>multiple vital rates regressed against a continuous state variable, and are well-suited to gain</mark> 614 <mark>mechanistic understanding on population dynamics to changes in the environment by including</mark> 615 <mark>environmental covariates into each vital rate model of the IPM</mark><sup>43</sup> <mark>. We built IPMs based on ‘plant</mark> 616 <mark>height’ (sqrt- or ln-transformed; see 6.1) as the continuous state variable. For biennial species</mark> 

- 617 <mark>(</mark> _<mark>Geranium robertianum</mark>_ <mark>and</mark> _<mark>Alliaria petiolata</mark>_ <mark>), IPMs included ‘age’ as an additional state</mark> 618 <mark>variable to explicitly account for their biennial life cycle</mark><sup>70,71</sup> <mark>. We did not construct IPMs (and</mark> 619 <mark>DDMs) for</mark> _<mark>Vinca minor</mark>_ <mark>,</mark> _<mark>Geranium sylvaticum</mark>_ <mark>and</mark> _<mark>Oxalis acetosella</mark>_ <mark>due to the absence of</mark> 

- 620 <mark>fecundity data in these species. For these species, responses to the environment were only</mark> 621 <mark>inferred based on the vital rates survival probability and growth rate.</mark> 

- 622 

- 623 <mark>Because we could not infer vital rates related to vegetative reproduction (clonal growth) due to</mark> 

- 624 <mark>the lack of empirical data (this would require destructive sampling), the approach presented here</mark> 

29 

- 625 <mark>estimates population growth rates that apply on generative population growth in the phase of</mark> 

- 626 <mark>population establishment. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that for some forest plants, vegetative</mark> 

- 627 <mark>reproduction is an important aspect of the life cycle. In addition, the estimated λ implicitly</mark> 

- 628 <mark>accounts for the effects of neighbouring competitors as all individuals were transplanted into</mark> 

- 629 <mark>community mesocosms. Details on the IPM structure are described in Supplementary Methods 3.</mark>

### 6.3.1. <mark>Demography-based distribution maps (i.e. maps of λ)</mark> 

633 <mark>Demography-based distribution maps (i.e. maps of λ) for each species were produced by</mark> 634 <mark>projecting the IPMs across the study extent</mark><sup>36,37</sup> <mark>(Extended Data Fig 2). Both continental- and</mark> 635 <mark>landscape-scale maps were produced to assess the model’s behaviour along macro- and</mark> 

- 636 <mark>microclimate gradients. Continental-extent maps were computed by iterating the IPM for each</mark> 

- 637 <mark>species in 1,000,000 random forested locations sampled within the study area. IPMs in these</mark> 

- 638 <mark>locations were supplied with environmental covariate values extracted from the 25-m resolution</mark> 

- 639 <mark>environmental layers. Landscape-extent maps were computed by iterating the IPM in all forested</mark> 640 <mark>25 × 25 m cells in a squared area of 2 × 2 km (400 ha) around the centroid of the experimental</mark> 

- 641 <mark>plots in Italy, Belgium and South Sweden. Hence, both continental-extent and landscape-extent</mark> 

- 642 <mark>maps relied on 25 meter resolution environmental data. Projected maps of λ were computed for</mark> 

- 643 <mark>the current environmental conditions and all combinations of future macroclimate change and a</mark> 644 <mark>50% increase or decrease in forest cover density.</mark> 

- 645 <mark>In all model future scenarios, we assumed temperature offsets to stay constant over time</mark> 

- 646 <mark>although forest disturbance reduces offset values</mark><sup>19,21</sup> <mark>and warming enhances offset values under</mark> 

- 647 <mark>maintained forest cover</mark><sup>22</sup> <mark>. The rationale behind this decision was two-fold: (1) projected future</mark> 

30 

- 648 <mark>high-resolution below-canopy temperature offset data at the European scale are simply not yet</mark> 

- 649 <mark>available to date; (2) using constant temperature offset values under different forest management</mark> 

- 650 <mark>and climate-change scenarios allowed us to isolate the effect of changes in species’ demography</mark> 

- 651 <mark>within the community driven by the independent effect of light</mark><sup>30</sup> <mark>.</mark> 

- 652 <mark>The effects of the experimental heating and irradiation treatments on the intercepts of the vital</mark> 

- 653 <mark>rates were not included in the future DDM predictions. Hence, we applied a</mark> _<mark>space-for-time</mark>_ 

- 654 <mark>substitution to make future predictions, i.e. we only used the spatial gradient from the transplant</mark> 

- 655 <mark>experiment to infer changes over time, not including the temporal change brought on by the</mark> 

- 656 <mark>experimental treatments. All DDM predictions implicitly included forest edge effects by the vital</mark> 

- 657 <mark>rate responses to less buffered microclimates and more light availability that are typical for forest</mark> 

- 658 <mark>edges</mark><sup>21,72</sup> <mark>.</mark> 

- 659 <mark>Geographic uncertainty of population growth rates (λ) was quantified by bootstrapping</mark> 

- 660 <mark>(Supplementary Methods 4).</mark> 

- 661 <mark>Parallel computation was implemented using the R packages</mark> _<mark>foreach</mark>_<sup>73</sup> <mark>and</mark> _<mark>doParallel</mark>_<sup>74</sup> <mark>. Maps</mark> 662 <mark>were produced using the R package</mark> _<mark>tmap</mark>_<sup>75</sup> <mark>, and are in an equal area projection [EPSG:3035]. In</mark> 

- 663 <mark>all maps, λ values higher than the 97.5% quantile were projected to the 97.5% quantile to avoid</mark> 

- 664 <mark>extremely high λ values in response to marginal environments that obscure the main gradient of</mark> 

- 665 <mark>the maps.</mark>

### 6.3.3 <mark>Validation of the distribution maps</mark> 

676 <mark>A general and convenient method to validate DDM predictions is currently not existing. We here</mark> 

677 <mark>developed a method to test whether the DDMs perform better than random based on a</mark> 678 <mark>quantitative comparison of the average population growth rates in occurrence locations (i.e.</mark> 679 <mark>locations were the species was observed)</mark> _<mark>versus</mark>_ <mark>random background locations. The average</mark> 

680 <mark>population growth rates in occurrence locations are expected to be higher compared to the</mark> 681 <mark>background locations if a model is better than random. Significant differences between the two</mark> 

682 <mark>groups were tested with one-sample t-tests. Occurrence locations for each species were extracted</mark> 

- 683 <mark>from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF, http://www.gbif.org). To improve the</mark> 

684 <mark>data quality, all occurrence locations were subject to a systematic cleaning protocol</mark> 685 <mark>(Supplementary Methods 5). Unfortunately, we could not collect enough ‘real’ absence data</mark> 

686 <mark>points for all species, and therefore had to work with background locations as an alternative. At</mark> 

- 687 <mark>these background locations, the target species could be either absent or unobserved.</mark> 

- 688 <mark>For each species, population growth rates were calculated for 1,000 forested occurrence locations</mark> 

- 689 <mark>and 1,000 forested background locations. Because the median coordinate uncertainty of the</mark> 

- 690 <mark>cleaned occurrence locations after cleaning was still relatively low (333 meter), we calculated the</mark> 

- 691 <mark>population growth rate as the average population growth rate of all raster cell within an area of</mark> 

- 692 <mark>~1 km² (i.e. circular area with a radius of 564 meter) around these locations. Additional details</mark> 693 <mark>are provided in Supplementary Methods 6.</mark>
